Sampling from a Distribution

▶ Watch on YouTube

Taking a sample from a distribution means asking a computer for a random number that follows that distribution's shape.

In one breath

A histogram of heights shows where measurements are likely and where they are rare. A smooth curve can stand in for that histogram. When we sample, the computer picks from the tall parts most of the time, and from the short parts once in a while. We do this to study statistics: we generate many samples, run tests on them, and see what happens.

The main idea

Josh starts with a histogram of height measurements. Each dot is one person that we measured. The tall part marks where measurements are most likely. Most people here were between 5 ft 7 in and 6 ft tall.

The low parts show where measurements are unlikely. Very few people were shorter than 4½ ft or taller than 6½ ft. We can lay a smooth curve over the bars and use that instead. Tall means likely, short means unlikely.

Sampling adds one step. We ask a computer for a random number, and it respects those probabilities. So one sample will probably land near the middle. Now and then it comes from an edge, because edges are rare but possible.

Step by step

  1. Start with a histogram of real measurements. Its height at each place shows how likely that measurement is.
  2. Approximate the histogram with a smooth curve. The curve says the same thing.
  3. Ask the computer for a random number that follows those probabilities. That number is one draw.
  4. Group the draws into samples of a chosen size, for example \(n = 3\) per sample.
  5. Feed the samples into a test, such as a t-test. Repeat many times and count how often the test gives the expected answer.

Formulas

Josh shows no formula in this video. The two rules below say in symbols what the pictures say in words.

Height means probability
$$ P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx, \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = 1 $$

Symbols: \(X\) is the value the computer picks. \(f(x)\) is the height of the curve at \(x\). \(a\) and \(b\) are the ends of a range. What it tells you: the chance of landing in a range is the area under the curve there, and all the area is 1.

How the computer draws a value
$$ U \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1), \qquad X = F^{-1}(U) $$

Symbols: \(U\) is a plain random number between 0 and 1. \(F\) is the running total of area up to a value. \(F^{-1}\) undoes \(F\). What it tells you: one simple random number is enough, because \(F^{-1}\) bends it into your distribution's shape.

Worked example from the video

Example

Josh takes two samples of size \(n = 3\) from a single distribution. Here \(n\) is the number of measurements inside each sample. He then runs a t-test comparing sample A with sample B.

Both samples came from the same distribution, so the t-test should report a large p-value. Josh repeats the draw-and-test cycle many times. Counting the large p-values shows how often the test behaves as it should.

He then repeats this with two separate distributions. Now the t-test should report a small p-value. If small p-values appear only rarely, increase the sample size.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out A sample from the edge is not an error. Rare values are part of the distribution, so the computer must return them sometimes. Judge the sampler by many draws, never by one.
Watch out Keep two meanings of "sample" apart. One draw is a single number, while a sample of \(n = 3\) is a small group of draws that you test together. Josh uses \(n\) for the size of that group.
Watch out The point of this exercise is that you know the truth in advance. If you sample from one distribution, any small p-value is a false alarm.

If they ask you in an interview

What does it mean to sample from a distribution?
It means asking a computer for a random value that follows the probabilities the distribution describes. Values where the curve is tall come up often, and values where it is low come up rarely.
Why would you simulate data when you already have real data?
Because with simulated data you know the true answer before you start. You can send many simulated samples through a test and count how often the test agrees with the truth. That tells you what the test can do, without any new experiments.
How does this help you choose a sample size?
You sample from two distributions that really do differ, then run the t-test many times at a chosen size. If the test finds the difference only rarely, the sample size is too small. You raise the size and repeat.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • A histogram of heights shows where measurements are common and where they are rare.
  • A smooth curve approximates that histogram and carries the same information.
  • Sampling means the computer picks a random value that respects those probabilities.
  • Most samples come from the tall middle, and a few come from the short edges.
  • Two samples of size three from one distribution should give a large p-value.
  • Two samples from different distributions should give a small p-value.
  • Simulation lets you measure what a test can do without doing real work.
