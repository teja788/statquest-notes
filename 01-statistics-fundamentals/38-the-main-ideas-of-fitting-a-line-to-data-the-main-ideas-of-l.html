The Main Ideas of Fitting a Line to Data (The Main Ideas of Least Squares and Linear Regression.)

▶ Watch on YouTube Also in: Machine Learning (video 9)

You have data on an XY graph and you want a line through it. This video explains how we score each candidate line, and how we pick the best one.

In one breath

Josh starts with a horizontal line drawn through the average y value of the data. He measures the gap between that line and each data point, then squares every gap so that points above the line do not cancel points below it. Adding those squares gives one number, the sum of squared residuals, which scores how well the line fits. Rotating the line changes that score, and there is a sweet spot where it is smallest. Finding that sweet spot by taking a derivative and setting it to zero is what "least squares" means.

The main idea

You did the experiment and you have data on an XY graph. You usually add a line to show the trend. But many lines are possible. Is one line better than another, and how would you prove it?

Josh begins with the worst fit of all: a flat line through the average y value. In this data that average is 3.5, and he calls the height of the line \(b\). A line fits well when it sits close to the points, so we measure the vertical gap from the line to each point. The gap for the first point is \(b - y_1\).

Here is the problem. The fourth point sits above the line, so \(y_4\) is bigger than \(b\) and the gap is negative. The fifth point is higher still, so its gap is very negative. If we just add the gaps, those negatives make a poor fit look good.

The people who worked this out first tried absolute values. That made the maths tricky, so they squared each gap instead. Squaring makes every term positive and keeps the later maths easy.

Step by step

  1. Draw a horizontal line through the average y value, 3.5. Call the height of the line \(b\).
  2. For every point, measure the vertical gap \(b - y_i\) between the line and the point.
  3. Square each gap, then add all the squares. That number is the sum of squared residuals. A residual is the difference between a real data point and the line.
  4. Rotate the line a little and score it again. The score drops, so the new line fits better.
  5. Keep rotating. The score falls for a while, then climbs again. The best line sits in between.
  6. Write the line in general form, \(y = ax + b\). Here \(a\) is the slope and \(b\) is the y-intercept, where the line crosses the y axis at \(x = 0\). Least squares means picking the \(a\) and \(b\) with the smallest score.
  7. Plot the score against each rotation. Take the derivative, which gives the slope of that curve, and find where the slope is zero. That is the bottom of the dip.
  8. Slope and intercept both vary, so the real picture is a 3D surface. Take the derivative for each one and set both to zero. A computer does this, never a person.

Formulas

Sum of squared residuals for the flat line
$$ \mathrm{SSR} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (b - y_i)^2 $$

Symbols: \(b\) is the height of the flat line, here 3.5. \(y_i\) is the observed y value of point \(i\). \(n\) is the number of points, and \(\sum\) means add over all of them. What it tells you: one number for how far the line sits from the data. Smaller is better, and this flat line scores 24.62.

The generic line
$$ y = ax + b $$

Symbols: \(a\) is the slope, how much \(y\) changes when \(x\) goes up by one. \(b\) is the y-intercept, the value of \(y\) at \(x = 0\). What it tells you: two numbers describe every straight line, so fitting a line means choosing \(a\) and \(b\).

Sum of squared residuals for any line
$$ \mathrm{SSR}(a,b) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \big( (a x_i + b) - y_i \big)^2 $$

Symbols: \(a x_i + b\) is the height of the line at \(x_i\). \(y_i\) is the observed height there. Their difference is the residual for point \(i\). What it tells you: it looks heavy, but it is the same idea as before.

Where the best fit sits
$$ \frac{\partial\,\mathrm{SSR}}{\partial a} = 0 \qquad \text{and} \qquad \frac{\partial\,\mathrm{SSR}}{\partial b} = 0 $$

Symbols: each term is the derivative of the score, that is, how steeply the score changes as you nudge the slope or the intercept. What it tells you: the score is smallest where both derivatives are zero. Solving these two equations gives the best slope and intercept, and a computer does the solving.

Worked example from the video

Example

Josh uses one small data set all the way through. The average y value is 3.5, so the flat line has \(b = 3.5\). The first three gaps are positive and the last two are negative. He squares all five and adds them, giving 24.62.

He then rotates the line and scores it again. The score falls to 18.72, then to 14.05 with a little more rotation. But rotating a long way pushes it up to 31.71, worse than the flat line.

That pattern is the point. The score falls, flattens, then climbs, so the best line lies in between. Solving where both derivatives are zero gives the answer:

$$ y = 0.77x + 0.66 $$

So the fitted line rises by 0.77 for every one unit of \(x\), and it crosses the y axis at 0.66.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out Never add the raw gaps. Points above the line give negative gaps and points below give positive ones, so they cancel. A terrible line can then look perfect. Squaring stops this.
Watch out More rotation is not always better. Rotated too far, the line scored 31.71, worse than the flat line at 24.62. Check the score instead of trusting the direction of travel.
Watch out The single dipping curve is a simplification. Slope and intercept both change, so the real object is a surface over two variables. Both derivatives must be zero at the best fit.
Watch out Nobody solves these derivatives by hand; the computer does it. What matters is the concept. Minimise squared vertical distances, and find the minimum where the derivative is zero.

If they ask you in an interview

What does "least squares" actually mean?
It means we choose the line that makes the sum of squared residuals as small as possible. A residual is the vertical distance between an observed data point and the line. We square each residual and add them up to get one score for the line. Least squares is simply picking the slope and intercept that give the lowest score.
Why square the residuals instead of just adding them?
Because raw residuals carry a sign. Points above the line give negative values and points below give positive values, so a bad line can score near zero. Squaring makes every term positive. People tried absolute values first, but squares proved much easier to work with.
How is the best line actually found?
We write the sum of squared residuals as a function of the slope and the intercept. That function falls as you rotate the line towards the data, then rises once you go too far. We take the derivative for the slope and for the intercept, and find where both are zero. A computer solves it for us.
Why start with a horizontal line at the average y value?
It is a deliberately poor fit, so it makes a clean baseline. It ignores x and predicts the average every time. Scoring it gives us a number to beat, and it shows why the signs of the residuals matter.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • We want a line through our data, and we need a fair way to compare candidate lines.
  • Start with a horizontal line at the average y value, called \(b\), which is 3.5 in this data.
  • Measure the vertical gap from the line to each point, \(b - y_i\), and notice that points above the line give negative gaps.
  • Square every gap and add them up. That total is the sum of squared residuals, and here it is 24.62.
  • Rotating the line drops the score to 18.72 and then 14.05, but rotating far too much pushes it up to 31.71.
  • Written generally, the line is \(y = ax + b\), and the best line is where both derivatives of the score are zero.
  • For this data the least squares line is \(y = 0.77x + 0.66\), and a computer, not a person, does the solving.
