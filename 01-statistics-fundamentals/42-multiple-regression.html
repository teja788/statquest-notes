Multiple Regression

▶ Watch on YouTube Also in: Machine Learning (video 11)

Multiple regression fits a plane instead of a line. Josh shows that the maths from simple regression carries over almost unchanged.

In one breath

People make a big deal of the difference between simple and multiple regression. There is not much difference. Simple regression fits a line from one predictor. Multiple regression fits a plane, because you added more data. R² and the p-value work the same way, with one change to the parameter count. The new part is that you can compare the two fits against each other.

The main idea

In simple regression you predict one thing from one other thing. Josh predicts mouse body length from mouse weight. He fits a line and asks two questions. How much variation does the line explain? That is R². Could a fit this good happen by chance? That is the p-value.

Multiple regression adds more data to the same equation. Josh predicts body length from weight and tail length. With two predictors the fit is a flat plane, not a line. Add food eaten or wheel time, and the fit becomes a higher-dimensional object. That phrase only means more columns of data. The thing you predict never changes, so every sum of squares is still measured around body length.

Step by step

  1. Fit the simple regression. Least squares finds the line from weight, estimating two parameters.
  2. Fit the multiple regression. Least squares finds the plane from weight and tail length, estimating three parameters.
  3. Calculate R² for each fit. The formula is identical for both.
  4. Calculate F and the p-value for each fit against the mean. Only the parameter count changes.
  5. Compare the two fits to each other. Replace the mean terms in the F formula with the simple regression terms. This says whether tail length earns its place.

Formulas

The multiple regression equation
$$ \text{body length} = b_0 + b_1 \cdot \text{weight} + b_2 \cdot \text{tail length} $$

Symbols: \(b_0\) is the intercept, the predicted body length when both predictors are zero. \(b_1\) is the slope for weight and \(b_2\) is the slope for tail length. What it tells you: each new piece of data is one more term with one more slope to estimate.

R² — identical for both
$$ R^2 = \frac{SS(\text{mean}) - SS(\text{fit})}{SS(\text{mean})} $$

Symbols: \(SS(\text{mean})\) is the sum of squared distances to the average body length. \(SS(\text{fit})\) is the same sum around the line or plane. What it tells you: the share of the variation in body length that the fit explains.

F, to get a p-value for R²
$$ F = \frac{\big(SS(\text{mean}) - SS(\text{fit})\big) \big/ (p_{\text{fit}} - p_{\text{mean}})}{SS(\text{fit}) \big/ (n - p_{\text{fit}})} $$

Symbols: \(p_{\text{fit}}\) is the number of parameters least squares estimates. \(p_{\text{mean}}\) is 1. \(n\) is the number of mice. What it tells you: whether the drop in the sum of squares is large for the parameters it cost.

F, comparing the two fits
$$ F = \frac{\big(SS(\text{simple}) - SS(\text{multiple})\big) \big/ (p_{\text{multiple}} - p_{\text{simple}})}{SS(\text{multiple}) \big/ (n - p_{\text{multiple}})} $$

Symbols: the mean terms are swapped for the simple regression terms. \(SS(\text{simple})\) is the sum of squares around the line, with \(p_{\text{simple}} = 2\). \(SS(\text{multiple})\) is the sum around the plane, with \(p_{\text{multiple}} = 3\). What it tells you: whether tail length is worth collecting.

Worked example from the video

Example

Josh models mouse body length. The simple regression uses weight alone, so \(p_{\text{fit}} = 2\). The multiple regression adds tail length, so \(p_{\text{fit}} = 3\). In both cases \(p_{\text{mean}} = 1\), because you only estimate the average body length.

To compare the fits directly, plug in \(p_{\text{simple}} = 2\) and \(p_{\text{multiple}} = 3\). The numerator is then divided by \(3 - 2 = 1\). If the R² values differ a lot and the p-value is small, tail length is worth measuring. Add time on the running wheel and \(p_{\text{fit}}\) becomes 4.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out Plain R² never goes down when you add data. That is why multiple regression uses an adjusted R², which pays a penalty for every extra parameter.
Watch out The parameter count is not fixed at 2 or 3. Every predictor you add changes \(p_{\text{fit}}\), and the F statistic is wrong if you forget to update it.
Watch out A bigger R² alone is not proof. You need the gain in R² to be large and the p-value to be small before you keep the extra variable.

If they ask you in an interview

What is the real difference between simple and multiple regression?
Very little. Simple regression fits a line using one predictor, and multiple regression fits a plane or a higher-dimensional object using several. R² and the F test are calculated the same way, and only the parameter count changes.
Why do you adjust R² in multiple regression?
Because plain R² can only go up when you add a predictor, even a useless one. The adjusted version pays a penalty for the extra parameters, so it can go down when a variable adds nothing.
How do you decide if a new variable is worth collecting?
Fit the model without it and the model with it, then run an F test between the two. The mean terms in the formula are replaced by the smaller model's sum of squares and parameter count. A big R² gain with a small p-value says it earns its place.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • Multiple regression is simple regression with more data added to the equation.
  • One predictor gives a line, two give a plane, and more give a higher-dimensional object.
  • R² is calculated exactly the same way in both cases, but you adjust it for the extra parameters.
  • The F test also stays the same, except that p-fit counts the parameters in your equation.
  • P-mean is always 1, and the best part is comparing the small model with the big one directly.
  • Keep the extra variable when the R² gain is large and the p-value is small.
