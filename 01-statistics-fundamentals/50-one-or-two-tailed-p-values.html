One or Two Tailed P-Values

▶ Watch on YouTube

Josh settles a question people ask him all the time. When a test lets you pick one tail or two, which p-value should you report?

In one breath

A one-tailed p-value only asks if your result is better. A two-tailed p-value asks if it is better or worse. The one-tailed value is smaller, so it is tempting. But you almost always want both sides of the story. Switching to one tail after seeing the data raises false positives from 5% to about 8%.

The main idea

Imagine you have a new cancer treatment. You run a small trial on six patients. Red dots show the new treatment, black dots the standard one. The new treatment looks better, but the result is a bit ambiguous. So you run the stats.

A one-tailed test gives a p-value of 0.03. That beats the usual 0.05 cutoff. A two-tailed test gives 0.06. That misses the cutoff. The two answers disagree, so the choice matters.

The two tests ask different questions. The one-tailed p-value tests only whether your treatment is better. The two-tailed p-value tests whether it is better, worse, or not clearly different. One tail is smaller because it lumps "worse" together with "no clear difference". With a cancer treatment you want to know about harm.

Step by step

  1. Before you collect data, ask what you want to learn. For a cancer treatment you must learn if it helps or harms.
  2. Choose the test and the p-value type now. Deciding later, after you see the data, is p hacking.
  3. Run the experiment and compute the p-value you chose. Compare it to your cutoff, usually 0.05.
  4. Report that p-value, even when the other one looks better. In the trial that means 0.06, not 0.03.
  5. If your test offers no choice of tails, do not worry about it.

Josh then shows why the timing of the choice matters. He starts with a standard normal distribution. Most draws land in the middle, but now and then one is very small or very large.

He draws two samples of three numbers from that same distribution. Both samples come from one distribution, so any significant result is a false positive. He ran 10,000 two-tailed t-tests and got close to 500 of them, the 5% we expect. Then he mimicked switching tails after looking: when sample one sat clearly below sample two, he used a one-tailed test. False positives climbed to about 800.

Formulas

The video shows no algebra. Two simple relations explain its numbers.

One tail from two
$$ p_{\text{one}} = \frac{p_{\text{two}}}{2} $$

Symbols: \(p_{\text{two}}\) is the two-tailed p-value; \(p_{\text{one}}\) is the one-tailed p-value, on the side the data went. What it tells you: for a symmetric test the one-tailed value is half the two-tailed value, so 0.06 becomes 0.03.

False positive rate
$$ \text{expected false positives} = \alpha \times N $$

Symbols: \(\alpha\) is the significance cutoff, here 0.05; \(N\) is the number of tests run, here 10,000. What it tells you: An honest two-tailed test expects \(0.05 \times 10000 = 500\) false positives. Tail switching gave about 800, so the real \(\alpha\) was near 0.08.

Worked example from the video

Example

Six patients: red dots on the new treatment, black dots on the standard one. The new-treatment values sit toward the better end, but the groups are not cleanly separated.

The two-tailed test gives 0.06. The one-tailed test gives \(0.06 / 2 = 0.03\). Against a 0.05 cutoff, one tail says significant and two tails say not significant.

You must report 0.06. The one-tailed number stays silent about the risk that the treatment is worse. For a cancer treatment that is unacceptable.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch outDo not argue that the data already leans your way, so testing the other side is pointless. That uses the data twice. It is p hacking, and it pushed false positives from 500 to 800.
Watch outA one-tailed test cannot separate "worse" from "not clearly different". If harm matters to you, it cannot answer your question.
Watch outThe smaller number is not better evidence. A one-tailed p-value is smaller by construction.

If they ask you in an interview

When would you use a one-tailed test?
In practice, almost never. A one-tailed test only asks whether the effect goes in one direction, and it cannot separate the opposite direction from no effect. We normally want both sides of the story, so I default to two-tailed.
Your one-tailed p-value is 0.03 and your two-tailed is 0.06. Which do you report?
The 0.06, unless I pre-registered a one-tailed test. Choosing the smaller number after seeing the data is p hacking. I would report the two-tailed result as not significant.
How much does switching tails after seeing the data cost you?
More than people expect. In the simulation, 10,000 honest two-tailed tests on null data gave about 500 false positives, the 5% we signed up for. Switching to one tail whenever the data looked promising pushed that to about 800. The real error rate was near 8%.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • A one-tailed p-value asks only whether your result is better in one direction.
  • A two-tailed p-value asks whether it is better, worse, or not clearly different.
  • The smaller number is not stronger evidence; it just ignores one side.
  • Pick the test and the p-value before you collect data, because picking afterwards is p hacking.
  • In the simulation that raised false positives from 5% to about 8%.
  • When the test gives you a choice, always choose the two-tailed p-value.
