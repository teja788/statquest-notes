Covariance

▶ Watch on YouTube

Covariance looks at two measurements taken from the same subjects and asks whether they move together. This video shows how the number is built, what its sign means, and why the size of the number is almost useless on its own.

In one breath

We measure two genes, X and Y, in the same five cells, so every cell gives a pair of numbers. For each pair we take how far X is from its mean, take how far Y is from its mean, and multiply the two differences. We add those products and divide by \(n-1\). A positive answer means a positive slope, a negative answer means a negative slope, and zero means no trend. The size of the answer changes with the scale of the data, so covariance is a stepping stone to correlation.

The main idea

Josh starts with something familiar. We counted mRNA transcripts for gene X in five different cells, then estimated the mean and the variance. If genes are not your thing, imagine counting green apples in five grocery stores. Now we count gene Y in those same five cells, and estimate its mean and variance too.

The important part is that the measurements come in pairs. Each cell gives one value for X and one value for Y. So we can draw each cell as a single dot, using its X value for the horizontal position and its Y value for the vertical position. The question covariance tries to answer is simple: do the pairs tell us something that the two variables on their own do not?

In Josh's first data set, cells with low values for gene X also have low values for gene Y, and cells with high X have high Y. That relationship can be summarised with a line that has a positive slope. If low X had gone with high Y instead, the line would have a negative slope. And if every cell had the same Y value, knowing X would tell us nothing about Y, so there would be no trend at all.

That gives the first main idea. Covariance classifies three kinds of relationship: a positive trend, a negative trend, and no trend. The second main idea is more sobering. You never calculate a covariance and stop there. Covariance is a computational stepping stone towards something more useful, such as correlation.

The machinery behind the sign is easy to see on the graph. Draw a vertical line at the mean of X and a horizontal line at the mean of Y. Those two lines cut the plot into four quadrants, and each quadrant decides the sign of the product that its dots contribute.

Step by step

  1. Measure both variables on the same subjects. In the video that is gene X and gene Y counted in the same five cells, so the data arrive as five pairs.
  2. Estimate the mean of X and the mean of Y separately. These two means are the reference points for everything that follows.
  3. Take one pair at a time. Work out how far its X value sits from the mean of X, and how far its Y value sits from the mean of Y. Both differences can be negative or positive.
  4. Multiply the two differences together. Two negatives give a positive product, two positives give a positive product, and one of each gives a negative product.
  5. Add the five products together. This is what the sigma in the formula asks for.
  6. Divide the total by \(n-1\), which is 4 for five cells. That final number is the estimated covariance.
  7. Read only the sign. Positive means the trend has a positive slope, negative means a negative slope, and zero means there is no trend.

The video works through all three cases with the same five cells and different gene Y values. The positive data set gives a covariance of 116, the negative data set gives −105.15, and the flat data set gives exactly 0.

Formulas

Estimated covariance
$$ \mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(x_i-\bar{x}\right)\left(y_i-\bar{y}\right)}{n-1} $$

Symbols: \(x_i\) is the X measurement from the \(i\)-th subject, and \(y_i\) is the Y measurement from that same subject. \(\bar{x}\) is the mean of all the X values and \(\bar{y}\) is the mean of all the Y values. \(\sum\) says add up one term for every subject. \(n\) is the number of subjects, five in this video, and we divide by \(n-1\) because we are estimating from a sample rather than describing a whole population. What it tells you: the sign of the answer classifies the trend as positive, negative, or absent.

Covariance of a variable with itself
$$ \mathrm{Cov}(X,X)=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(x_i-\bar{x}\right)^2}{n-1}=s_x^2 $$

Symbols: the same symbols as above, with \(y_i\) replaced by \(x_i\) and \(\bar{y}\) replaced by \(\bar{x}\), because the same data now sit on both axes. \(s_x^2\) is the estimated variance of X. What it tells you: variance is just a special case of covariance, so the two ideas are the same machinery applied to one variable or to two.

What happens when you rescale
$$ \mathrm{Cov}(aX,aY)=a^2\,\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y) $$

Symbols: \(a\) is a constant multiplier applied to every measurement, for example 2 when you double the data. What it tells you: multiplying the data by 2 multiplies the covariance by 4, even though the picture of the relationship has not changed at all. This is the reason the size of a covariance cannot be interpreted on its own.

Worked example from the video

To show why the size of a covariance is hard to read, Josh calculates the covariance of gene X with itself. The five counts are 3, 13, 19, 24 and 29, and their mean is 17.6.

Example

Each difference from the mean is multiplied by itself, because X sits on both axes.

$$ (-14.6)^2+(-4.6)^2+(1.4)^2+(6.4)^2+(11.4)^2=407.2 $$
$$ \mathrm{Cov}(X,X)=\frac{407.2}{5-1}=101.8\approx 102 $$

The value is positive, which is correct: a variable always has a positive trend with itself, and every dot falls exactly on a straight line.

Now double every measurement, so the counts become 6, 26, 38, 48 and 58. The dots keep the same shape and still sit on the same straight line. Only the scale of the axes has changed. But each difference from the mean is now twice as large, so each product is four times as large.

$$ \mathrm{Cov}(2X,2X)=\frac{4\times 407.2}{5-1}=407.2 $$

That is four times 101.8, and Josh reports it as 408. The relationship did not change, yet the covariance grew four times. He makes the same point another way. Data sitting perfectly on the line gives 102, while looser data gives 381. Rescaling the looser data flips which number is bigger.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out

Do not read the size of a covariance as a strength. A large covariance can come from data measured in large units, not from a strong relationship. Only the sign is safe to interpret.

Watch out

Covariance does not tell you how steep the line is. A steep relationship and a gentle one can both give the same covariance, and covariance also says nothing about how close the dots sit to the line.

Watch out

A covariance of zero means the positive and negative products cancelled out. In the video's last case the dots go up and then down, so each positive term is matched by a negative one. That is a missing trend, not proof that the two variables have nothing to do with each other.

If they ask you in an interview

What does covariance actually tell you?
It tells you the direction of the trend between two variables measured on the same subjects. A positive covariance means they tend to rise together, a negative one means one rises as the other falls, and zero means there is no trend. What it does not tell you is how strong or how steep that relationship is, because the number depends on the units of the data.
Why do we bother calculating it if the number is hard to interpret?
Because it is a computational stepping stone. Correlation is built directly from covariance, and it removes the scale problem, so it is the thing you usually report. Covariance also feeds into methods such as principal component analysis, where the covariance matrix is the starting point. You rarely calculate a covariance and stop there.
What is the covariance of a variable with itself?
It is the variance. If you put the same data on both axes then \(y_i\) becomes \(x_i\) and \(\bar{y}\) becomes \(\bar{x}\), so the product of the two differences turns into a squared difference. That is exactly the variance formula. It is a nice way to remember that variance is just a special case of covariance.
What happens to the covariance if I change the units of my data?
It changes, even though the relationship does not. If you multiply every value by two, the covariance is multiplied by four, because both differences from the mean double before they are multiplied together. That is why comparing covariances across different data sets is meaningless, and why correlation exists.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • Covariance works on pairs of measurements taken from the same subjects, such as two genes counted in the same five cells.
  • For each pair you multiply the distance of X from its mean by the distance of Y from its mean.
  • You add all those products and divide by \(n-1\) to get the estimated covariance.
  • A positive covariance means a positive trend, a negative one means a negative trend, and zero means no trend.
  • The size of the covariance is not interpretable, because doubling the data multiplies it by four.
  • The covariance of a variable with itself is simply its variance.
  • Treat covariance as a stepping stone to correlation, which is the next video, and to methods such as PCA.
