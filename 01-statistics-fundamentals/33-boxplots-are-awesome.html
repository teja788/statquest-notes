Boxplots are Awesome

▶ Watch on YouTube

A boxplot shows the shape of a whole batch of numbers in one small picture. Josh names each part and shows why the plot beats a plain bar chart.

In one breath

A boxplot is also called a box and whisker plot. The line inside the box is the median, so half the data sits above it and half below. The box holds the middle 50% of the data, split into two quarters by the median. The whiskers reach the rest, and points past them are drawn as dots for outliers. Draw the original points on top and you also see how many measurements each group had.

The main idea

Josh starts with the two parts of the name. The rectangle is the box. The lines sticking out from it are the whiskers. Every piece stands for a share of the data.

The line across the middle of the box is the median, the middle value. 50% of the data is above it and 50% below. A median is not pushed around by extreme values as much as the mean, the ordinary average, is.

Inside the box there are two quarters. 25% of the data sits between the median and the top of the box. Another 25% sits below the median. So the box covers the middle 50%. The whiskers cover the rest, and any point drawn beyond a whisker is an outlier.

Step by step

  1. Find the median of the data and draw it as a line. Half the values are above this line and half are below it.
  2. Draw the box around the median. Its top edge cuts off the 25% above, and its bottom edge the 25% below.
  3. Draw the whiskers out from the box. They stretch towards the smallest and largest ordinary values.
  4. Plot anything beyond the whiskers as a dot. Those dots are the outliers.
  5. Overlay the original data points. Now you also see how many measurements each group has.

Josh calls that last step common practice. Two groups can have similar boxes but very different numbers of measurements. You trust statistics from the crowded group more.

Formulas

This video has no formulas. It is a picture lesson. The one thing worth memorising is the share of data each part holds.

Rule of thumb
$$ \underbrace{25\% + 25\%}_{\text{the box}} \;+\; \underbrace{50\%}_{\text{whiskers and outlier dots}} \;=\; 100\% $$

Symbols: the two middle quarters are the box, split by the median line. The remaining half sits along the whiskers, plus any dots beyond them. What it tells you: the box covers the middle half of the data, and everything else lies outside it.

Worked example from the video

Example

Josh shows several boxplots side by side with the raw measurements drawn on top. One group has only a handful of dots. Another has a thick cloud. Trust the statistics from the crowded group more, because they rest on more measurements.

To make the shares concrete, take 8 sorted values: 2, 4, 5, 7, 9, 10, 14, 30. The median falls between 7 and 9, so it is 8. The box runs from about 5 to 10 and holds the middle four values, which is 50% of the data. The value 30 is drawn as a dot beyond the whisker. If 30 became 300, the median would stay at 8, but the mean would jump from 10.1 to 43.9.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out The line in the box is the median, not the mean. People read it as the average and then wonder why it does not match the number in their table.
Watch out A boxplot never tells you the sample size on its own. Two groups with identical boxes can rest on 5 points or 500. Overlay the data points when you can.
Watch out A dot past a whisker means the value is unusual, not that it is wrong. What to do with it is a separate question.
Watch out Bar plots of the same data look tidy but hide almost everything. They show one number per group, so spread and outliers vanish.

If they ask you in an interview

What does each part of a boxplot represent?
The line in the middle of the box is the median, so half the data is above it and half below. The box covers the middle 50% of the data, split into two quarters by that median line. The whiskers reach out to the rest of the values, and anything past them is drawn as a dot for an outlier.
Why show the median instead of the mean?
The median is the middle value, so extreme values barely move it. The mean gets pulled towards any very large or very small number. When the data has outliers, the median describes the typical value more honestly.
Why prefer a boxplot over a bar chart for the same data?
A bar gives one number per group and nothing else. A boxplot shows the centre, the spread, and the outliers in the same space. If I also draw the raw points on top, the reader can see how many measurements each group had, which tells them how much to trust the comparison.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • A boxplot is also called a box and whisker plot.
  • The line inside the box is the median, with 50% of the data above and 50% below.
  • The median resists outliers far better than the mean does.
  • The box holds the middle 50%, as two quarters on either side of the median.
  • The whiskers stretch to the remaining ordinary values.
  • Points beyond the whiskers are plotted as dots and are called outliers.
  • Overlaying the original data shows how many measurements each group had.
  • A bar plot of the same data hides the spread, the outliers and the sample size.
