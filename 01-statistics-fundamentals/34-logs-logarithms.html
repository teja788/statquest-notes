Logs (logarithms)

▶ Watch on YouTube

A logarithm does one simple job. It rewrites a number as a power of some base, then hands you back the exponent.

In one breath

Josh takes a number line from zero to eight and rewrites every value as a power of two. The log base two function then isolates the exponent. That turns a crowded number line into an evenly spaced log scale. On it, eight fold up sits the same distance from zero as eight fold down. He also shows that the mean of the logs resists outliers, and that logs turn multiplication into addition.

The main idea

Start with a plain number line running from zero to eight. Eight, four, two and one are easy to rewrite as powers of two. They are two cubed, two squared, two to the first, and two to the zero.

The other numbers work too, just less neatly. Seven, six and five are all powers of two with messy exponents. Even pi can be written as a power of two. Any positive number can.

Now take the log base two of each value. The only real step is to rewrite the number as a power of two. After that, the log function simply isolates the exponent and gives it to you.

Do that for eight and you get three. Do it for one and you get zero, because one is two to the zero. Below one the exponents go negative. One half is two to the minus one, one quarter is two to the minus two, and one eighth is two to the minus three.

The picture below is the payoff. On the ordinary line, one eighth, one quarter and one half are jammed against zero, while eight sits far away. Both ends are the same eight fold change from one. On the log axis the two changes are mirror images. That is why fold changes belong on a log axis.

Step by step

  1. Pick a base that matches your data. Josh uses base two because his example doubles at each step.
  2. Rewrite the number as a power of that base. Eight becomes two cubed; one eighth becomes two to the minus three.
  3. Apply the log. It isolates the exponent, so the log base two of two cubed is simply three.
  4. Read off the sign. Values above one give positive logs, one gives zero, and values below one give negative logs.
  5. Plot fold changes on this axis. Up and down moves of the same size now sit symmetrically around zero.
  6. To multiply two numbers, write both as powers of the base and add the exponents. To divide, subtract them instead.
  7. To return to normal numbers, raise the base to the log value. That undoes the whole thing.

Formulas

What a log does
$$ \log_b\!\left(b^{x}\right) = x $$

Symbols: \(b\) is the base, the number that gets raised to a power. \(x\) is the exponent. What it tells you: the log function isolates the exponent and returns nothing else. This one line is the whole video.

The video's example
$$ \log_2(8) = \log_2\!\left(2^{3}\right) = 3 $$

Symbols: the base is 2 and 8 is the number you feed in. What it tells you: rewrite 8 as two cubed, then read the exponent. Three is the log base two equivalent of eight.

Negative side
$$ \log_2\!\left(\tfrac{1}{8}\right) = \log_2\!\left(2^{-3}\right) = -3 $$

Symbols: the fraction one eighth is the same as two to the minus three. What it tells you: numbers below one give negative logs, and eight fold down lands at minus three, mirroring eight fold up at plus three.

Multiplication becomes addition
$$ \log_b(m \times n) = \log_b(m) + \log_b(n) $$

Symbols: \(m\) and \(n\) are the two positive numbers you multiply, and \(b\) is the base. What it tells you: write both as powers of the base and the exponents just add. This holds even for messy numbers, such as three times five.

Division becomes subtraction
$$ \log_b\!\left(\frac{m}{n}\right) = \log_b(m) - \log_b(n) $$

Symbols: \(m\) is the numerator, \(n\) is the denominator, \(b\) is the base. What it tells you: flipping a number to the bottom of a fraction flips the sign of its exponent, so dividing becomes subtracting. For example \(\log_2(2/4) = 1 - 2 = -1\).

Geometric mean
$$ \text{geometric mean} = b^{\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \log_b(x_i)} $$

Symbols: \(x_i\) is the \(i\)-th measurement, \(n\) is how many you have, and \(b\) is the base. What it tells you: average the logs, then raise the base to that average. A single large value pulls this average around far less.

Worked example from the video

Example

Josh uses qPCR, a lab process where the thing being measured doubles at every cycle. Because of the doubling, base two is the natural choice.

The first run sets the baseline at 1 transcript. The second run finds twice as many, so 2. The third run finds eight times as many, so 8.

On normal numbers the average is \(\frac{1 + 2 + 8}{3} = 3.7\). On the log base two scale the three runs are 0, 1 and 3 cycles, so the average is \(\frac{0 + 1 + 3}{3} = 1.3\).

Convert that back with \(2^{1.3} = 2.5\). The geometric mean of 2.5 is well below the ordinary mean of 3.7, because the third run behaves like an outlier and the log scale refuses to let it dominate.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out There is no log of zero. No exponent \(x\) makes \(2^x\) equal zero. Set \(x\) to minus one thousand and the result is tiny but still above zero. R reports the log base two of zero as negative infinity, which is a convention rather than a real value.
Watch out Fold changes on a linear axis mislead the eye. Eight fold up looks enormous and eight fold down looks like nothing, although both are the same magnitude of change. Only the log axis makes them symmetric.
Watch out The word "log" on its own means different bases in different tools. In R, plain log() gives the natural log, base \(e\), where \(e\) is roughly 2.7. Say the base out loud when you report a result.
Watch out Averaging logs is not the same as logging the average. The mean of the logs, converted back, gives the geometric mean, which is smaller here. Decide which one you want before you compute it.

If they ask you in an interview

What does a logarithm actually do?
It isolates an exponent. You rewrite a number as the base raised to some power, and the log hands you that power back. The log base two of eight is three, because eight is two cubed. Everything else about logs follows from that single fact.
Why do we plot fold change on a log scale?
Because a linear axis treats up and down changes unfairly. A value eight times larger than one sits seven units away, while a value eight times smaller sits less than one unit away. On a log base two axis they land at plus three and minus three, so the picture matches the biology or the business reality.
What is the geometric mean and when would you use it?
It is the mean of the logs, converted back to normal numbers. I would use it whenever the data grows by multiplication rather than addition, such as qPCR cycles, growth rates or returns. It is much less sensitive to a single very large value than the ordinary mean.
What happens if your data contains zeros before you take logs?
The log is undefined, because no exponent gives you zero. Software usually returns negative infinity, which then spoils any average downstream. So I check for zeros first and decide how to handle them.
Does the choice of base matter?
The rules are identical for every base, so it is a readability choice. Base two suits data that doubles. Base ten suits things that move by powers of ten, like decibels or earthquakes. Base \(e\) is the software default.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • A log rewrites a number as a power of a base and returns the exponent.
  • The log base two of eight is three, because eight is two cubed.
  • Values below one give negative logs, and one always gives zero.
  • Log scales are the right home for fold change, since eight fold up and eight fold down sit an equal distance from zero.
  • The mean of the logs, the geometric mean, is more robust to outliers than the ordinary mean.
  • Logs turn multiplication into addition of exponents.
  • Logs turn division into subtraction of exponents.
  • All of this works for any base, so pick the base that matches how your data grows.
