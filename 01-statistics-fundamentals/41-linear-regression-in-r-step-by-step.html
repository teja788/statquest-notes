Linear Regression in R, Step-by-Step

▶ Watch on YouTube Companion to the StatQuest on linear regression

This is the hands-on companion to the linear regression video. Josh fits a line in R, then reads every line of the output.

In one breath

You put your data in a data frame, then call lm(size ~ weight). That gives the least squares intercept and slope. The real work is the summary() output, which has five blocks. You check that the residuals look symmetric, that the p-value for the predictor is below 0.05, and that R-squared is large. Then you draw the line on the plot.

The main idea

Linear regression fits a straight line through the data. R finds the intercept and slope that make the squared vertical distances to the line as small as possible. Those distances are called residuals.

The video uses the nine mice from the linear regression video. The formula size ~ weight reads as "size is explained by weight". So size is the y variable and weight is the x variable. Fitting takes one line of code. Everything after that is interpretation.

Step by step

  1. Put the two columns in a data frame. Typing the name prints it as a neat table.

    mouse.data <- data.frame(
  weight = c(0.9, 1.8, 2.4, 3.5, 3.9, 4.4, 5.1, 5.6, 6.3),
  size   = c(1.4, 2.6, 1.0, 3.7, 5.5, 3.2, 3.0, 4.9, 6.3))
mouse.data

  2. Look at the raw data first, before any model.

    plot(mouse.data$weight, mouse.data$size)

  3. Fit the line with lm(), which stands for linear models. The left side of the tilde is y, the right side is x.

    mouse.regression <- lm(size ~ weight, data = mouse.data)

  4. Ask for the summary. This is where the useful numbers live.

    summary(mouse.regression)

  5. Add the fitted line to the plot you already drew.

    abline(mouse.regression, col = "blue", lwd = 2)

Formulas

Multiple R-squared
$$ R^2 = \frac{SS(\text{mean}) - SS(\text{fit})}{SS(\text{mean})} $$

Symbols: \(SS(\text{mean})\) is the sum of squared distances to a flat line at the mean of y. \(SS(\text{fit})\) is the same sum around the fitted line. What it tells you: the share of the variation in y that x explains.

Residual standard error
$$ RSE = \sqrt{\frac{SS(\text{fit})}{n - p_{fit}}} $$

Symbols: \(n\) is the number of data points. \(p_{fit}\) is the number of parameters in the fitted line, which is 2. The bottom is the residual degrees of freedom. What it tells you: the typical distance from a point to the line. Josh calls it the square root of the denominator of F.

F-statistic
$$ F = \frac{\big(SS(\text{mean}) - SS(\text{fit})\big) / (p_{fit} - p_{mean})}{SS(\text{fit}) / (n - p_{fit})} $$

Symbols: \(p_{mean}=1\) is the single parameter of the flat mean line. The two divisors are the degrees of freedom R prints next to F. What it tells you: whether the gain over the mean line is bigger than chance would give.

Example

Here is the summary for the nine mice, rounded.

    Min      1Q  Median      3Q     Max
-1.5482 -0.8037  0.1186  0.6186  1.8852

Coefficients:
            Estimate Std. Error t value Pr(>|t|)
(Intercept)   0.5813     0.9649   0.602   0.5661
weight        0.7778     0.2334   3.332   0.0126 *

Residual standard error: 1.19 on 7 degrees of freedom
Multiple R-squared: 0.6133,  Adjusted R-squared: 0.5580
F-statistic: 11.1 on 1 and 7 DF,  p-value: 0.01256

The residuals look reasonable. Min and Max sit on opposite sides of zero, 1Q and 3Q are rough mirror images, and the median is close to zero.

The slope is 0.7778, so a mouse one unit heavier is predicted to be 0.7778 units bigger. Its p-value is 0.0126, below 0.05, so weight gives a reliable guess of size. The intercept has a large p-value, but Josh says we usually do not care about the intercept.

R-squared is 0.6133, so weight explains 61% of the variation in size. The F-test says that 61% beats chance. Its p-value matches the one for weight, because there is only one predictor.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out The formula is y ~ x, not the other way round. Writing weight ~ size fits a different line and gives a different slope, even though R-squared stays the same.
Watch out A small p-value does not mean the line fits well. It only says the slope is probably not zero. Look at R-squared and at the scatter plot too.
Watch out Lopsided residuals are a warning. If the median is far from zero, or Min and Max differ a lot, a straight line may be the wrong shape.

If they ask you in an interview

What does the p-value next to a coefficient test?
It tests whether that coefficient is zero. If it is zero, the term adds nothing to the model. A small p-value for the slope means the predictor gives a reliable guess of the outcome. I ignore the intercept's p-value.
Why does the overall F p-value equal the slope p-value here?
With a single predictor, both tests ask the same question. Does adding weight beat a flat line at the mean? With more predictors the F-test becomes a joint test, and the two p-values separate.
What do you check in the residual summary?
I check for symmetry around zero. The median should be near zero. Min and Max should sit about the same distance from zero, and so should 1Q and 3Q. If they are lopsided, a straight line is probably the wrong shape.
R-squared versus adjusted R-squared?
R-squared is the fraction of variation in y that the model explains. It never goes down when you add a predictor, even a useless one. Adjusted R-squared scales it by the number of parameters, so it can go down.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • Put your two variables in a data frame and plot them first.
  • Fit the model with lm(size ~ weight, data = mouse.data), where the left side is y.
  • All the interpretation comes from summary().
  • Check that the residual quantiles are roughly symmetric about zero.
  • Read the slope estimate, and want its p-value below 0.05.
  • R-squared says how much variation the predictor explains; here it is 61%.
  • Finish by drawing the line with abline() so you can see the fit.
