Calculating the Mean, Variance and Standard Deviation

▶ Watch on YouTube

This video shows the exact arithmetic behind the mean, the variance and the standard deviation. It also shows why the formula changes when you only have a small sample instead of the whole population.

In one breath

A normal curve needs two numbers: where it sits and how wide it is. The mean says where it sits. The variance and the standard deviation say how wide it is. If you have every measurement in the population, you calculate these numbers exactly. In real life you almost never have every measurement, so you estimate them from a small sample. The estimate of the mean uses the same formula, but the estimate of the variance divides by \(n-1\) instead of \(n\).

The main idea

Josh keeps the example from the earlier videos. We count mRNA transcripts for gene X inside liver cells. If that means nothing to you, count green apples in grocery stores instead. The population is all 240 billion cells; the sample is the five we measured.

If we measured all 240 billion cells, we could fit a normal curve to their histogram. Two numbers fix that curve. The population mean says where to centre it. The population variance, or its square root the population standard deviation, says how wide to make it. Here the mean is 20 and the standard deviation is 10.

These are not estimates. They came from every measurement, so they are the true values. That is rare and expensive. Almost always we hold a few measurements and make a best guess instead. Josh calls this calculating versus estimating.

The symbols mark the difference. The Greek letter \(\mu\) is the population mean. The symbol \(\bar{x}\), read "x-bar", is the estimated mean, also called the sample mean. With more data, \(\bar{x}\) drifts closer to \(\mu\).

The second half of the video is about the width. The variance is the average of the squared distances between the data and the mean. We square for a simple reason. Measurements left of the mean give negative distances and those on the right give positive ones. Unsquared, they would cancel and the total would collapse to zero.

Squaring has one side effect: the units get squared too. Our variance of 100 means 100 transcripts squared, which we cannot draw on the axis. So we take the square root and get the standard deviation, 10 transcripts. Now we can draw the mean plus or minus 10.

Step by step

  1. Decide what you have. Measure the whole population and you can calculate the true parameters. Measure a sample and you can only estimate them.
  2. Find the mean. Add the measurements and divide by how many there are. Same arithmetic either way; only the symbol changes, \(\mu\) or \(\bar{x}\).
  3. Subtract the mean from every measurement. Each result is a distance from the centre, negative below and positive above.
  4. Square each distance. This kills the minus signs, so left cannot cancel right.
  5. Add up the squared distances. The Greek letter sigma, \(\sum\), means add the terms.
  6. Divide the total by \(n\) for a population, or by \(n-1\) for a sample. The result is the variance.
  7. Take the square root. That is the standard deviation, back in the original units.

Step six is the only place the two situations differ. We measure distances from the sample mean \(\bar{x}\), not the true \(\mu\). Those distances are the smallest possible, so dividing by \(n\) reports too little spread. Dividing by \(n-1\) corrects it.

Formulas

Mean: same formula either way
$$ \mu = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i \qquad\qquad \bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i $$

Symbols: \(x_i\) is the \(i\)-th measurement and \(n\) is how many there are. \(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\) means add the terms from first to last. \(\mu\) uses all 240 billion cells; \(\bar{x}\) uses the 5 you measured. What it tells you: the arithmetic is identical, only the data set differs. The mean is where you place the peak of the curve.

Population variance and standard deviation
$$ \sigma^2 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(x_i - \mu\right)^2}{n}, \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{\sigma^2} $$

Symbols: \(\sigma^2\) is the population variance and \(\sigma\) the population standard deviation. \(x_i - \mu\) is the distance from one measurement to the true mean. \(n\) is the size of the whole population. What it tells you: the variance is the average squared distance from the centre. Its square root returns to the original units, so you can draw \(\mu \pm \sigma\).

Estimated variance and standard deviation — use these
$$ s^2 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(x_i - \bar{x}\right)^2}{n-1}, \qquad s = \sqrt{s^2} $$

Symbols: \(s^2\) is the estimated variance and \(s\) the estimated standard deviation. \(x_i - \bar{x}\) is the distance to the sample mean, because we do not know \(\mu\). \(n\) is the sample size. What it tells you: two things change from the population formula. \(\mu\) becomes \(\bar{x}\), and \(n\) becomes \(n-1\) so the answer is not too small.

Worked example from the video

Example

Five liver cells gave 3, 13, 19, 24 and 29 transcripts. The true population values, which we would never normally see, are \(\mu = 20\) and \(\sigma = 10\).

First the estimated mean. Add the counts and divide by five.

$$ \bar{x} = \frac{3 + 13 + 19 + 24 + 29}{5} = \frac{88}{5} = 17.6 $$

Next, subtract 17.6 from each count and square the result.

$$ s = \sqrt{101.8} \approx 10.1 $$

So five cells give a curve centred on 17.6 with a width of 10.1. The true curve is centred on 20 with a width of 10. On all 240 billion cells the video gets a variance of 100 transcripts squared, so \(\sigma = \sqrt{100} = 10\).

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out

Do not divide by \(n\) when you have a sample. It is the most common slip and it always errs the same way: your reported spread is too small.

Watch out

Only the variance formula changes between calculating and estimating. The mean is the same either way. There is no \(n-1\) anywhere in the mean.

Watch out

The variance is not in the units you measured; it is in those units squared. You cannot plot it on the data axis. Report the standard deviation instead.

Watch out

Check your software's default. Most packages estimate the variance, but Microsoft Excel makes you choose. var.p divides by \(n\); var.s divides by \(n-1\). Since you nearly always hold a sample, var.s is nearly always right.

If they ask you in an interview

Why do we divide by n minus 1 instead of n?
Because we measure distances from the sample mean, not the true mean, which we do not know. The sample mean sits in the middle of our data, so those distances are the smallest possible. That makes the sum of squares too small. Dividing by \(n-1\) corrects the bias.
Why square the differences instead of just adding them?
Points below the mean give negative differences and points above give positive ones. Added raw, they cancel and the total is zero no matter how spread the data are. Squaring makes every term positive, so real spread cannot cancel itself.
Variance or standard deviation: which do you report?
The variance is the average squared distance from the mean, so its units are squared. The standard deviation is its square root, back in the original units. I report the standard deviation, because a reader can compare it directly to the data.
When would you use the population formula that divides by n?
Only when the data really are the entire population. That happens with a closed set, such as every employee on the payroll, when you only describe that set. In practice it is rare, so the estimating formula is the safe default.
How far can you trust an estimate from five measurements?
In the video, five cells gave a mean of 17.6 against a true 20, and a standard deviation of 10.1 against a true 10. Useful, and it saved enormous work. But a small sample can easily land further off, so I would quantify the uncertainty too.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • A normal curve needs a centre and a width: the mean and the standard deviation.
  • The mean is the sum of the measurements divided by how many there are, for population or sample alike.
  • Write \(\mu\) for the true population mean and \(\bar{x}\) for the sample estimate.
  • The variance is the average squared distance from the mean; we square so left cannot cancel right.
  • The standard deviation is its square root, back in the original units, so you can draw it.
  • Divide by \(n\) for a population, but by \(n-1\) for a sample, to avoid underestimating the spread.
  • Five liver cells gave \(\bar{x} = 17.6\) and \(s = 10.1\), close to the true \(\mu = 20\) and \(\sigma = 10\).
  • You almost always hold a sample, so use the estimating formulas and check your software.
