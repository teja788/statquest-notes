Population and Estimated Parameters

▶ Watch on YouTube

This video explains what a population is, what its parameters are, and why we spend our lives estimating them from small samples.

In one breath

A population is every single unit you could measure, such as all 240 billion cells in a liver. Fit a curve to that full set of data, and the numbers defining the curve are the population parameters. In real work you measure only a handful of units, so you estimate those parameters from a small sample. Every repeat gives slightly different estimates, and more data pulls them closer to the truth. Statistics then says how much confidence to place in the estimates, which is what makes results reproducible.

The main idea

Josh counts the number of mRNA transcripts for gene X in five liver cells. An mRNA transcript is a copy of a gene that the cell has made, so the count is a whole number. If genetics means nothing to you, count green apples in five grocery stores instead.

The five cells gave 3, 13, 19, 24 and 29. Now imagine unlimited time and money. You would measure all 240 billion cells in the liver. That complete set is the population. Its histogram shows most cells had between 20 and 30 transcripts, and few had under 10 or over 30.

A histogram of 240 billion cells is clumsy, so we replace it with a smooth curve of the same shape. Here that is a normal distribution with mean 20 and standard deviation 10. The mean sits in the middle. The standard deviation says how far the data spread around the mean. Those two numbers fully define the curve, so they are the population parameters.

The catch is that nobody measures 240 billion cells. We measure five, and use them to estimate the two parameters. The figure below shows the arrangement.

Step by step

  1. Measure five liver cells. Josh counts 3, 13, 19, 24 and 29 transcripts for gene X.
  2. Imagine measuring the whole population instead: all 240 billion cells, drawn as one huge histogram.
  3. Ask the histogram a question. For the chance of 30 or more transcripts, divide 38 billion cells by 240 billion. That gives 0.16.
  4. Replace the histogram with a smooth curve. A normal distribution with mean 20 and standard deviation 10 fits.
  5. Ask the curve the same question. The tail area above 30 is also 0.16. The curve stands in well for the real data.
  6. Learn the words. The full set of cells is the population, and the numbers defining the fitted curve are its parameters. An exponential curve has a rate; a gamma curve has a shape and a rate.
  7. Face reality. You rarely have the population, so you estimate from a sample. The five cells give an estimated mean of 17.6 and an estimated standard deviation of 10.1.
  8. Repeat the experiment. A second set of five cells gives 19.2 and 12.7. Both sets differ from each other and from the true 20 and 10.
  9. Add more data. With two, three and then five measurements the estimates walk towards the truth, as the next figure shows.
  10. Quantify your confidence with p-values and confidence intervals. Here they say the two experiments are not significantly different, so the results should replicate.

This answers the worry about reproducibility. Different answers every time sound alarming. But the estimates tighten around the true parameters as the sample grows.

Formulas

The same probability, two ways
$$ P(X \ge 30) = \frac{38\ \text{billion cells}}{240\ \text{billion cells}} = 0.16 \qquad P(X \ge 30) = \frac{\text{area above } 30}{\text{total area}} = \frac{0.16}{1} = 0.16 $$

Symbols: \(X\) is the count in one cell picked at random. On the left you divide cells by cells. On the right you divide area by area, and the total area is always 1. What it tells you: both routes give 0.16, so the curve replaces the raw data fairly.

The normal curve and its two parameters
$$ X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu = 20,\ \sigma = 10), \qquad f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\, e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}} $$

Symbols: \(\sim\) reads "is distributed as" and \(\mathcal{N}\) is the normal distribution. \(\mu\) (mu) is the population mean, the centre. \(\sigma\) (sigma) is the population standard deviation, the width. \(f(x)\) is the height of the curve above count \(x\). What it tells you: once you know \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) the whole curve is fixed, so those two numbers are the population parameters.

Other shapes, other parameters
$$ f(x) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x},\ \ \lambda = 0.1 \qquad\qquad X \sim \text{Gamma}(\text{shape},\ \text{rate}) $$

Symbols: \(\lambda\) (lambda) is the rate of an exponential distribution; Josh uses 0.1. Gamma needs two numbers, a shape and a rate. What it tells you: "parameter" does not mean mean and standard deviation. It means whichever numbers define the curve you chose.

Parameter versus estimate
$$ \hat{\mu} = 17.6 \ \longrightarrow\ \mu = 20 \qquad\qquad \hat{\sigma} = 10.1 \ \longrightarrow\ \sigma = 10 $$

Symbols: a plain \(\mu\) or \(\sigma\) is the true, fixed population value. The hat marks a value computed from a sample. The arrow means "aims at". What it tells you: hatted numbers change with every repeat; unhatted ones never change.

Worked example from the video

Example

The population of 240 billion liver cells follows a normal curve with \(\mu = 20\) and \(\sigma = 10\). Of those, 38 billion have 30 or more transcripts of gene X, so the probability is \(38/240 = 0.16\).

Now do the experiment. Josh measures five cells and gets 3, 13, 19, 24 and 29, which give an estimated mean of 17.6 and an estimated standard deviation of 10.1. A repeat on five fresh cells gives 19.2 and 12.7. Neither pair equals the truth or each other.

Watch the estimates improve as the sample grows:

Measurements usedEstimated meanEstimated SD
2 cells11.0 (true value 20)11.3 (true value 10)
3 cells15.311.0
5 cells17.610.1

Ten cells would do better still. A p-value or confidence interval says that 17.6 and 19.2 are not significantly different. So both experiments support the same conclusions.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out

A parameter is not the number you calculated. The population mean is 20 and never moves. The 17.6 from your five cells is only an estimate of it. Say "estimated mean" when you mean the sample value.

Watch out

Different estimates on every repeat are normal, not a bug. Josh gets 17.6 and then 19.2 from the same population. What matters is whether the gap is large enough to worry about.

Watch out

The population is fixed by your question, not by its size. It can be every liver cell, or every store in one grocery chain. Change the chain and your estimated parameters no longer describe it.

Watch out

Do not assume a normal curve. Josh trusts it only because the curve and the raw histogram both give 0.16. Other data need an exponential or a gamma curve.

If they ask you in an interview

What is the difference between a population parameter and an estimate?
A population parameter is a fixed number describing the whole population, such as a mean of 20 across every liver cell. An estimate is what you compute from the sample you could afford, such as 17.6 from five cells. The parameter never changes. The estimate changes with every new sample.
Why estimate parameters instead of just describing your data?
Because we want results others can reproduce. A description of my five cells is only about my five cells. If I estimate the parameters of the population they came from, my conclusions carry over to anyone sampling the same population.
Two runs gave different estimates. Is something wrong?
Not by itself. Two samples from the same population almost always give different estimates. The right move is to quantify the difference with a confidence interval or a p-value. In the video 17.6 and 19.2 were not significantly different.
How does sample size affect your estimates?
More data means more accurate estimates and more confidence in them. With two cells the estimated mean was 11 against a true 20. With three it went to 15.3, and with five to 17.6. Larger samples also give narrower confidence intervals.
How does this map onto machine learning?
Josh makes the link directly. The five measurements are the training data. The curve that represents the population is what the method is trying to predict.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • A population is every unit you could measure, such as all 240 billion cells in a liver.
  • Fit a curve to the population histogram, and the numbers defining that curve are the population parameters.
  • For a normal curve those are the mean and the standard deviation, here 20 and 10.
  • Other shapes have other parameters: an exponential has a rate, a gamma has a shape and a rate.
  • We rarely measure a whole population, so we estimate the parameters from a sample.
  • Every repeat gives different estimates, so an estimate is a guess at the parameter, not the parameter.
  • The more data you collect, the closer the estimates sit to the true values.
  • P-values and confidence intervals say how much confidence to place in the estimates, which makes results reproducible.
