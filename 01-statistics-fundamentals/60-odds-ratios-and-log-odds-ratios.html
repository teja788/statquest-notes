Odds Ratios and Log(Odds Ratios)

▶ Watch on YouTube Also in: Machine Learning (video 15)

An odds ratio is a ratio of two odds. It tells you whether one thing raises the odds of another thing. Taking its log makes the scale fair in both directions.

In one breath

The odds are one ratio. The odds ratio compares two odds, one from each group. Its raw scale is lopsided, so we take the log to make it symmetric around zero. The odds ratio measures effect size, like R-squared does. To check if the relationship is real, Josh shows three tests: Fisher's exact test, the chi-square test, and the Wald test.

The main idea

The odds are already a ratio. In the last video, the odds of Josh's team winning were 2 over 4, which is 0.5. So why do people also say "odds ratio"? Because they mean a ratio of odds. You take the odds in one group and divide by the odds in another group.

With the video's simple numbers, one group has odds 2 to 4 and the other has odds 3 to 1. The odds ratio is \((2/4)\) divided by \((3/1)\), which is 0.17. Flip the two groups and you get \((3/1)\) divided by \((2/4)\), which is 6.

Both numbers describe the same comparison, seen from opposite sides. Yet 0.17 and 6 look nothing alike. That is the problem with the raw scale. When the bottom odds are bigger, the answer sits between 0 and 1. When the top odds are bigger, it can run to infinity. Taking the log fixes this. The log of 0.17 is -1.79 and the log of 6 is +1.79.

Step by step

Josh then puts the idea to work on real data: 356 people, some with cancer and some with a mutated gene.

  1. Sort everyone into a two-by-two table. Of the 356 people, 29 have cancer and 327 do not. Also, 140 carry the mutated gene and 216 do not.
  2. Read the odds of cancer inside each group. With the mutated gene the odds are 23 to 117. Without it they are 6 to 210.
  3. Divide the first odds by the second. The odds ratio is 6.88, so the odds of cancer are 6.88 times greater with the gene.
  4. Take the log to get 1.93. We test this value, because logs of odds ratios follow a normal distribution.
  5. Read it as an effect size. Like R-squared, a large value means the gene predicts cancer well.
  6. Now test for significance. Josh gives three options: Fisher's exact test, a chi-square test, and the Wald test.
  7. Report the result. All three tests agree that this relationship is very unlikely to be chance.

The three tests answer the same question in different ways. Fisher's exact test treats the people as a bag of sweets. There are 29 red sweets for cancer and 327 blue ones for no cancer. It asks how surprising a handful of 23 red and 117 blue would be. A computer does the work, and the p-value is 0.00001.

The chi-square test compares what we saw with what we would expect. The expected counts assume the gene and cancer are unrelated. The Wald test uses a different fact. Logs of odds ratios are normally distributed, so it counts how far 1.93 sits from zero.

Formulas

Write the four counts of the table as \(a\), \(b\), \(c\) and \(d\), reading across the rows.

Odds ratio
$$ \mathrm{OR} = \frac{a/b}{c/d} = \frac{a\,d}{b\,c} $$

Symbols: \(a\) and \(b\) are group one with and without the outcome. \(c\) and \(d\) are group two with and without it. What it tells you: how many times greater the odds are in group one.

Why we take the log
$$ \log\!\left(\frac{1}{\mathrm{OR}}\right) = -\log(\mathrm{OR}) $$

Symbols: \(\mathrm{OR}\) is the odds ratio. \(1/\mathrm{OR}\) is the same comparison with the groups swapped. What it tells you: swapping the groups only flips the sign, so zero means no difference.

Expected count for the chi-square test
$$ E = P(\text{outcome}) \times (\text{people in the group}) $$

Symbols: \(E\) is the count you would expect if there were no relationship. \(P(\text{outcome})\) is the overall share of people with the outcome, here \(29/356 = 0.08\). What it tells you: the count each cell should hold if nothing is going on.

Estimated standard deviation of the log odds ratio
$$ s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{a}+\frac{1}{b}+\frac{1}{c}+\frac{1}{d}} $$

Symbols: \(s\) is the estimated standard deviation. \(a, b, c, d\) are the four observed counts. What it tells you: how much the log odds ratio would wobble from sample to sample.

Wald statistic
$$ z = \frac{\log(\mathrm{OR})}{s} $$

Symbols: \(z\) is the number of standard deviations from zero. \(s\) is the estimated standard deviation. What it tells you: how far the result sits from "no relationship". Beyond about 2, the p-value drops below 0.05.

Worked example from the video

Example

Among the 140 people with the mutated gene, 23 have cancer and 117 do not. Among the 216 without it, 6 have cancer and 210 do not. So the odds ratio is \((23/117)\) divided by \((6/210)\), which comes to 6.88. The log of that is 1.93.

Fisher's exact test gives a p-value of 0.00001. For the chi-square test, the overall chance of cancer is \(29/356 = 0.08\). Multiply by the 140 people with the gene. You expect 11.2 cancer cases, leaving 128.8 without. Do the same for the other 216 people. You expect 17.3 cases, leaving 198.7 without. The test compares those numbers with 23, 117, 6 and 210. The p-value is 0.00001 with the continuity correction, and 0.000004 without it.

For the Wald test, \(s = \sqrt{1/23 + 1/117 + 1/6 + 1/210} = 0.47\). Josh also simulated 10,000 log odds ratios with no real relationship. Their standard deviation was 0.43, which is close. Dividing 1.93 by 0.47 gives 4.11 standard deviations. The two-sided p-value is 0.00005.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out The odds and the odds ratio are not the same thing. The odds are one ratio inside one group. The odds ratio divides the odds of one group by the odds of another.
Watch out Do not compare raw odds ratios as if the scale were even. Values below 1 are crammed between 0 and 1. Values above 1 stretch to infinity. Take logs before you compare sizes.
Watch out A large odds ratio is an effect size, not a p-value. Like R-squared, it shows the strength of the relationship. It does not show significance, so you still need a test.
Watch out There is no agreement on which test to use, and people mix them. A p-value from Fisher and a confidence interval from Wald can disagree. Using Wald for both keeps them consistent. Josh's simulation also found the continuity-corrected chi-square test a little conservative: 3 percent significant instead of 5.

If they ask you in an interview

What is an odds ratio, and how is it different from the odds?
The odds count how often something happens against how often it does not, inside one group. An odds ratio divides the odds of one group by the odds of another. So it compares two groups, and a value of 1 means their odds match.
Why do we work with the log of the odds ratio?
The raw scale is not symmetric. Anything that lowers the odds lands between 0 and 1. Anything that raises them can run to infinity. The log centres everything on zero, so swapping the groups only flips the sign. It also makes the values roughly normal, which the standard tests assume.
How would you test whether an odds ratio is significant?
There are three common choices: Fisher's exact test, a chi-square test, and the Wald test. Fisher is exact and suits small counts. The Wald test comes free with logistic regression output. It gives the p-value and the confidence interval from one model, so they always agree. I would check what my field prefers.
How does the Wald test actually work here?
With no relationship, log odds ratios are normal and centred on zero. You estimate their standard deviation as the square root of the sum of one over each count. Then you divide the observed log odds ratio by it. Past about two standard deviations, the p-value falls under 0.05.
What does an odds ratio of 6.88 mean in the gene and cancer example?
The odds of cancer are 6.88 times greater for someone carrying the mutated gene. That is an effect size, so the gene predicts cancer fairly well here. Its log is 1.93, which sits 4.11 standard deviations from zero. The p-value is about 0.00005.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • The odds compare one thing happening to it not happening, inside a single group.
  • An odds ratio divides the odds of one group by the odds of another group.
  • The raw scale is lopsided, because low values are squeezed between 0 and 1.
  • Taking the log makes the scale symmetric, so swapping the groups only changes the sign.
  • For the mutated gene and cancer, the odds ratio is 6.88 and its log is 1.93.
  • The odds ratio measures effect size, like R-squared, so it does not prove significance.
  • Fisher's exact test, the chi-square test and the Wald test each give a p-value.
  • The Wald test divides 1.93 by 0.47, giving 4.11 standard deviations and p = 0.00005.
