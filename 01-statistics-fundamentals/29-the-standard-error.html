The standard error

▶ Watch on YouTube

The standard error tells you how much a sample mean would move if you repeated the same experiment many times. It is the standard deviation of the means, not of the raw data.

In one breath

Josh starts with error bars, because a bar can show a standard deviation, a standard error, or a confidence interval. A standard deviation says how the data points spread around the mean. A standard error says how the means from repeated samples spread. You get it by taking many samples, computing each mean, then taking the standard deviation of those means. For the mean there is a shortcut formula. For every other statistic you can bootstrap it.

The main idea

Imagine you measure three groups, A, B and C, and plot every point on a scatter plot. You then draw a green bar at the mean of each group. You can also draw a red bar one standard deviation above and below each mean. In papers and talks people often hide the raw points and show only these bars. Josh calls that a dynamite plot, because each column looks like the igniter on a stick of dynamite.

There are three common kinds of error bar. Standard deviation bars tell you how the data spread around the mean. Standard error bars tell you how the means spread. Confidence interval bars are built from standard errors. Most of the time you want standard deviation bars, because they describe the data you collected.

Now picture weighing mice. The weights follow a normal curve, so most mice sit close to the average. You cannot weigh every mouse, so you take a sample of five. That sample gives a mean of −0.2 and a standard deviation of 1.923. Remember the rule of thumb: one standard deviation each side of the mean covers about 68% of the data, and two cover about 95%.

Now take a second sample of five, and a third. The individual measurements jump around a lot, but the three means stay close together. For a mean to land far from the centre, almost every point would have to be far out in the same direction. That is rare. Usually one point is extreme, and one extreme point barely moves the mean.

So you can compute a standard deviation of the three means themselves. That number is narrower than the standard deviation of the raw data. It has its own name: the standard error of the mean.

Step by step

  1. Take several samples from the same population. Every sample must have the same number of measurements, n. In this video n = 5.
  2. Compute the mean of each sample. You do not need each sample's standard deviation for this; the means are enough.
  3. Compute the standard deviation of that list of means. That number is the standard error of the mean. Here it came out as 0.86.
  4. The same recipe works for any statistic. Collect many medians and take their standard deviation, and you have the standard error of the median. The same goes for modes, percentiles, or even standard deviations.

Bootstrapping, when there is no formula

Repeating an experiment many times costs time and money. For the mean there is a formula that estimates the standard error from one sample. For almost everything else there is no such formula. That is where bootstrapping helps, and a computer does the work easily.

  1. Start with the one sample you actually collected. Josh notes that bootstrapping works better with 10 or more measurements than with 5.
  2. Pick one measurement at random from that sample and write it down. It is not a new measurement; it is a value you already have.
  3. Repeat until you have written down n values. The same value may come up again, and that is fine. In the video 1.43 is picked twice.
  4. Compute the statistic you care about from those n values. Here it is the mean.
  5. Go back to step 2 and repeat many times, so you build up a long list of means.
  6. Take the standard deviation of that list. That is your standard error.

Formulas

Standard error, by definition
$$ SE_{\bar{x}} \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{1}{M-1}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\left(\bar{x}_i-\bar{\bar{x}}\right)^{2}} $$

Symbols: \(M\) is how many samples you took; \(\bar{x}_i\) is the mean of sample \(i\); \(\bar{\bar{x}}\) is the mean of all those means; the square root and the sum are just the ordinary standard deviation formula. What it tells you: the standard error is nothing special — it is the plain standard deviation applied to a list of means instead of a list of measurements.

Shortcut for the mean
$$ SE_{\bar{x}} \;=\; \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} $$

Symbols: \(s\) is the standard deviation of the one sample you collected; \(n\) is how many measurements are in that sample. What it tells you: you can estimate the standard error from a single sample, without repeating the experiment, and it shrinks as the sample gets bigger. This shortcut exists for the mean; for most other statistics you bootstrap instead.

Rule of thumb for one standard deviation
$$ \bar{x}\pm 1s \approx 68\%\ \text{of the data},\qquad \bar{x}\pm 2s \approx 95\%\ \text{of the data} $$

Symbols: \(\bar{x}\) is the mean; \(s\) is the standard deviation; the percentages are the share of values inside that range for a normal distribution. What it tells you: it fixes what a bar means, so you can read a graph and see that the bar around the means is far shorter than the bars around the data.

Worked example from the video

Example

Josh weighs mice and records the difference from the average weight. One sample of five gives a mean of −0.2 and a standard deviation of \(s = 1.923\). Taking the standard deviation of the sample means gives a standard error of 0.86.

The shortcut formula gives the same answer from that single sample:

$$ SE_{\bar{x}}=\frac{1.923}{\sqrt{5}}=\frac{1.923}{2.236}=0.86 $$

Compare 1.923 with 0.86. The data spread more than twice as wide as the means do.

The bootstrap run uses the same sample. Josh draws 1.43, −1.38, −3.11, 1.43 and −0.10, so 1.43 appears twice. The sum is −1.73, so this bootstrap mean is \(-1.73/5=-0.35\). Repeat that many times, then take the standard deviation of all those means.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out An error bar on its own means nothing. The same drawing can show a standard deviation, a standard error, or a confidence interval. Always say in the caption which one you plotted.
Watch out Do not use the standard error to describe your data. It describes how a mean varies across repeated experiments. If a reader wants to see your data, show the standard deviation.
Watch out Because of the \(\sqrt{n}\), a bigger sample always shrinks the standard error. A tiny standard error may only mean you collected a lot of measurements.
Watch out Bootstrapping must draw with replacement. Without it, every bootstrap sample is your original sample reshuffled, and every mean is identical. Josh also warns that five measurements is thin; ten or more is better.

If they ask you in an interview

What is the difference between a standard deviation and a standard error?
A standard deviation describes the spread of the measurements around their mean. A standard error describes the spread of a statistic, usually the mean, across repeated samples from the same population. Put simply, the standard error is the standard deviation of the means. That is why it is always the narrower bar.
Why is the standard error smaller than the standard deviation?
Averaging cancels extremes. For a sample mean to land far out, nearly every point would have to be far out in the same direction. That is rare. The formula says the same thing: dividing by the square root of n shrinks the spread.
How would you get the standard error of the median?
There is no simple formula for the median, so I would bootstrap it. I resample my data with replacement to the original size, take the median, and repeat many times. The standard deviation of those medians is the answer. The same recipe works for any statistic.
Which error bars would you put on a chart for a stakeholder?
It depends on the question. If they want to understand the data I collected, I plot the standard deviation. If they want to know how reliable my estimate of the mean is, I plot the standard error. Either way I label the bars.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • Error bars come in three common flavours: standard deviation, standard error, and confidence interval.
  • The standard deviation tells you how the data spread around the mean.
  • The standard error tells you how the means from repeated samples spread around the centre.
  • You get it by taking many equally sized samples, computing each mean, and taking the standard deviation of those means.
  • It is always narrower than the standard deviation of the data, because averaging cancels extreme values.
  • For the mean there is a shortcut: the sample standard deviation divided by the square root of the sample size.
  • For any other statistic, bootstrap it by resampling your data with replacement and taking the standard deviation of the results.
