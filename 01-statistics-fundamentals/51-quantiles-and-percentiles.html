Quantiles and Percentiles

▶ Watch on YouTube

Quantiles are the cut points that split a set of numbers into groups of equal size. Percentiles are the same cut points, written on a scale of 0 to 100.

In one breath

Josh sorts the expression values of 15 genes. The median has seven genes below it and seven above, so it is a quantile. Two more cuts give four equal groups: the 0.25, 0.5 and 0.75 quantiles. In practice we call these the 25th, 50th and 75th percentiles. A percentile answers one question: what share of the values are smaller?

The main idea

Josh measures expression for 15 genes. Expression just means how active a gene is. Sort the values from lowest to highest. Now ask where you could cut the list so every piece holds the same count. Those cut points are the quantiles.

The middle cut is the median, at 4.5. Seven genes lie below and seven above, so it splits the data in half. We call it the 0.5 quantile, or the 50% quantile. Both labels are common.

Two more cuts give four equal groups. A quarter of the points are below 2.5, so 2.5 is the 0.25 or 25% quantile. Three quarters are below 7.3, so 7.3 is the 0.75 or 75% quantile.

Strictly, percentiles are quantiles that make 100 equal groups. Fifteen genes cannot make 100 groups. Even so, everyone calls 4.5 the 50th percentile. The word is used loosely, and that is normal.

Step by step

  1. Measure the quantity for every item. Josh measures expression for 15 genes.
  2. Sort the values from lowest to highest.
  3. Choose how many equal groups you want. Two groups give the median, four give quarters, 100 give true percentiles.
  4. Place the cuts so each group holds the same count. Here they land at 2.5, 4.5 and 7.3.
  5. Name each cut by the share of data below it. A quarter below means the 0.25 quantile, or 25th percentile.
  6. For a single point, count the values smaller than it and divide by the total. That fraction is its quantile.

Formulas

Cut points for equal groups
$$ q_j = \frac{j}{g}, \qquad j = 1, 2, \dots, g-1 $$

Symbols: \(g\) is how many equal groups you want, \(j\) counts the cuts, and \(q_j\) labels a cut on a 0 to 1 scale. What it tells you: four groups need three cuts, at 0.25, 0.5 and 0.75.

Quantile and percentile of one point
$$ q = \frac{k}{n}, \qquad \text{percentile} = 100 \times q $$

Symbols: \(k\) is how many values are smaller than your point, \(n\) is the total count of values (15 here), and \(q\) is the quantile from 0 to 1. The 100 rescales \(q\) to a 0 to 100 range. What it tells you: the percentile is the share of the data lying below your value.

Worked example from the video

Example

Take the 15 sorted values. The smallest has nothing below it, so \(k=0\): the 0% quantile, or zeroth percentile.

The next value up has one value below it, so \(k=1\):

$$ q = \frac{1}{15} \approx 0.07 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \text{the 7th percentile} $$

The fourth smallest has three values below it, so \(k=3\):

$$ q = \frac{3}{15} = 0.20 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \text{the 20th percentile} $$

Cut the same 15 genes into four equal groups instead, and you get 2.5, 4.5 and 7.3.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch outThere is no single agreed way to compute a quantile. R's quantile function alone offers nine methods, each giving a slightly different answer.
Watch outDo not put much trust in quantiles from a small data set. They shift from method to method and sample to sample. With large data, all methods agree closely.
Watch outA quantile is a value on the data scale, not a count of points. The 75% quantile here is 7.3, an expression level. Also, 0.75 and 75% name the same cut.

If they ask you in an interview

What is the difference between a quantile and a percentile?
They are the same idea on two scales. A quantile runs from 0 to 1, so the median is the 0.5 quantile. A percentile runs to 100, so the median is the 50th percentile. Strictly a percentile makes 100 groups, but the word is used loosely.
How would you find the percentile of a single value?
I sort the data, count the values smaller than mine, divide by the sample size, and multiply by 100. With 15 genes, a value with three below it sits at the 20th percentile. Conventions differ, so I would state the one I used.
Why can quantiles be unreliable on a small sample?
Two reasons. The methods disagree, and R alone offers nine. Sampling noise also moves the cuts when few points sit near them. With large data the methods converge, so I trust extreme quantiles only with enough data.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • A quantile is a cut point that divides sorted data into groups of equal size.
  • The median is a quantile, and it is labelled 0.5 or 50%.
  • For the 15 genes, the 25%, 50% and 75% quantiles are 2.5, 4.5 and 7.3.
  • For one point, count the values below it and divide by the total.
  • Many methods exist, so quantiles from small samples are unstable.
  • These ideas lead into QQ plots and quantile normalisation.
