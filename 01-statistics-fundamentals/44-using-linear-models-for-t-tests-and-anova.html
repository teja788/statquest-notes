Using Linear Models for t-tests and ANOVA

▶ Watch on YouTube Also in: Machine Learning (video 12)

Part two of the series on general linear models. It shows that a t-test and an ANOVA are the same calculation as linear regression, only with a different fitted line.

In one breath

In linear regression we sum the squared residuals around the mean, then around the fitted line, then turn those two numbers into F and a p-value. A t-test does exactly the same thing. The only change is the line we fit: instead of one sloped line, we fit one horizontal line per group. We write those group lines as a single equation using 0s and 1s that switch each mean on or off, and those 0s and 1s form a design matrix. ANOVA is the same again, with more groups and more columns.

The main idea

In the last video we measured mouse weight and mouse size. R squared told us how useful weight was for predicting size, and the p-value told us whether the relationship could be due to chance. Josh now asks the same machinery to answer a different question.

The new question is a t-test. We compare gene expression in control mice and mutant mice. A mutant mouse is a normal mouse with one gene knocked out, so that gene no longer works. A t-test compares the two means and asks if they differ more than chance would allow.

Here is why this matters. If one method gives a p-value for regression and for a t-test, it gives p-values for much harder situations too. We never invent a new procedure.

Step by step

Josh runs the regression and the t-test side by side, so every step matches.

  1. Ignore the x-axis and find the overall mean. For the t-test that is the mean of all the values, control and mutant together.
  2. Sum the squared residuals around that mean to get SS(mean). A residual is the distance from a data point to the line, and here the line is the overall mean.
  3. Fit a line to the data. Now the x-axis matters again. Regression gives one sloped least squares line. For a group, the least squares fit is simply its mean. So we get a horizontal line at 2.2 for the control data and one at 3.6 for the mutant data.
  4. Combine the two lines into one equation. Each point is one times its own group mean, plus zero times the other group mean, plus a residual. Those 1s and 0s are on and off switches, and stacked together they form the design matrix.
  5. Sum the squared residuals around the fitted lines to get SS(fit). The residuals now run to the group means, not to the overall mean.
  6. Plug SS(mean), SS(fit) and the parameter counts into the F equation, then turn F into a p-value. This last step is identical for all three tests.

Formulas

Combined equation for one point
$$ y = 1 \times 2.2 \;+\; 0 \times 3.6 \;+\; \text{residual} $$

Symbols: \(y\) is the gene expression of one mouse. \(2.2\) is the control mean and \(3.6\) is the mutant mean. The \(1\) and the \(0\) are switches that pick this mouse's group. The residual is the distance from the point to its group mean. What it tells you: multiplied out this is just \(y = 2.2 + \text{residual}\). The odd looking form describes the same line, but a computer can reuse it for any number of groups.

Abstract form with the design matrix
$$ y = \text{mean(control)} + \text{mean(mutant)} $$

Symbols: mean(control) and mean(mutant) are the two parameters we estimate. What it tells you: in practice the role of each design matrix column is assumed, so the model is written this short and the matrix supplies the on and off pattern.

F, the same for regression, t-test and ANOVA
$$ F = \frac{\big(SS(\text{mean}) - SS(\text{fit})\big) \big/ (p_{\text{fit}} - p_{\text{mean}})}{SS(\text{fit}) \big/ (n - p_{\text{fit}})} $$

Symbols: \(SS(\text{mean})\) is the sum of squared residuals around the overall mean. \(SS(\text{fit})\) is the same sum around the fitted lines. \(p_{\text{mean}}\) is the number of parameters in the overall mean equation, always 1. \(p_{\text{fit}}\) is the number of parameters in the fitted model. \(n\) is the number of measurements. What it tells you: F compares the variation the fit explains against the variation it leaves behind, and F leads to the p-value.

Parameter counts
$$ p_{\text{mean}} = 1 \qquad p_{\text{fit}}^{\,\text{regression}} = 2 \qquad p_{\text{fit}}^{\,t\text{-test}} = 2 \qquad p_{\text{fit}}^{\,\text{ANOVA}} = 5 $$

Symbols: for regression the parameters are the intercept and the slope. For the t-test they are the two group means. For the ANOVA here they are the five category means. What it tells you: only the counts change between the three tests, which is why one piece of software runs all of them.

Worked example from the video

Example

We measure gene expression in control mice and mutant mice. The least squares fit for the control group is its mean, which crosses the y-axis at 2.2, so the control line is \(y = 2.2\). The mutant mean is 3.6, so that line is \(y = 3.6\).

A control mouse is written as \(y = 1 \times 2.2 + 0 \times 3.6 + \text{residual}\). A mutant mouse is written as \(y = 0 \times 2.2 + 1 \times 3.6 + \text{residual}\). Inside a group, only the residual changes from point to point.

We then take SS(mean) from step two and SS(fit) from step five. Here \(p_{\text{mean}} = 1\) and \(p_{\text{fit}} = 2\), because we estimated two group means. Those numbers go into the F equation, and F gives the p-value.

The ANOVA repeats this with five categories: control mice, mutant mice, control and mutant mice on a funky diet, and heterozygote mice. It tests whether all five are the same. SS(mean) is found the same way, so \(p_{\text{mean}}\) is still 1. The fitted model now has one mean per category, so \(p_{\text{fit}} = 5\) and the design matrix has five columns.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch outThe design matrices in this video are not the standard ones. Josh says software normally uses a different arrangement. Both versions give the same answer, but you will meet the common form more often. The next video covers it.
Watch outDo not confuse parameters with data points. \(p_{\text{fit}}\) counts the values we estimate, not the number of mice. The number of mice enters F through \(n\).
Watch outThe line fitted to one group is horizontal, so there is no slope to interpret. It still counts as fitting a line, which is why the regression steps carry over unchanged.
Watch outANOVA only tests whether all the categories are the same. A small p-value says at least one differs, but not which one.

If they ask you in an interview

Why is a t-test a special case of a linear model?
Both fit a line by least squares and compare it to the overall mean. In regression the line has an intercept and a slope. In a t-test the fit is one horizontal line per group, sitting at that group's mean. The sums of squared residuals then go into the same F equation and give the same kind of p-value.
What is a design matrix and why do we need one?
A design matrix is a table of 1s and 0s that says which parameters apply to each observation. One column switches the control mean on or off, the other does the same for the mutant mean. It lets us write two fitted lines as one equation, so a computer solves every least squares problem with the same code.
What changes when you move from a t-test to an ANOVA?
Only the number of parameters and the width of the design matrix. The t-test has two group means, so p for the fit is 2. The ANOVA here has five categories, so p for the fit is 5 and the matrix has five columns. Everything else stays the same.
What do SS(mean) and SS(fit) each measure?
SS(mean) is the squared distance from the data to a single overall mean, so it is the variation when we ignore the groups. SS(fit) is the squared distance to the group means, so it is the variation left after the model does its work. F is built from the drop between them, adjusted for the parameters we spent.
Why does it matter that one method covers all these tests?
The same steps then handle harder designs too, such as many categories at once, or categories mixed with continuous predictors. That is the point of a general linear model. You change the design matrix, not the procedure.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • A t-test and an ANOVA use the same steps as linear regression.
  • First find the overall mean and sum the squared residuals around it to get SS(mean).
  • Then fit the data, which for groups means one horizontal line per group at that group's mean, here 2.2 and 3.6.
  • Write those lines as one equation, using 1s and 0s that switch each mean on or off.
  • Stacked together, those 1s and 0s are the design matrix, with one column per group.
  • Sum the squared residuals around the fitted lines to get SS(fit).
  • Plug SS(mean), SS(fit) and the two parameter counts into the F equation, then read the p-value.
  • ANOVA is the same with more columns, and the standard design matrices come in the next video.
