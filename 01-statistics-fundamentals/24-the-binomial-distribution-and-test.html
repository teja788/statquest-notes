The Binomial Distribution and Test

▶ Watch on YouTube

This video shows how to compute the chance of a yes/no result happening by luck, and how to turn that into a p-value.

In one breath

Josh asks people whether they prefer orange Fanta or grape Fanta. If nobody really has a preference, each person is like a coin flip. The binomial distribution gives the probability of every possible survey outcome under that "no preference" model. When you add up the probability of your result plus everything equally likely or rarer, you get a p-value. That calculation is called a binomial test.

The main idea

Four people say they prefer orange Fanta and three say grape. Is that real evidence, or just noise from a small survey? You cannot answer that by staring at the numbers. You need a model of what pure chance looks like.

So Josh builds one. Assume nobody truly prefers either flavour. Then every person has a 50% chance of saying orange and a 50% chance of saying grape. The binomial distribution turns that assumption into a probability for each possible survey result. If the observed result sits comfortably inside that distribution, the "no preference" idea survives.

The key move is counting orders. Take three people, and suppose two choose orange. One specific sequence, orange–orange–grape, has probability \(0.5 \times 0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.125\). But grape could have come first or second instead. There are three sequences, each with the same probability of 0.125. So the chance that any two of three prefer orange is \(3 \times 0.125 = 0.375\).

Step by step

  1. Write down the null idea you want to test. Here it is "people love both flavours equally", which means the probability of choosing orange is \(p = 0.5\).
  2. Compute the probability of one specific sequence of answers. Multiply the per-person probabilities together, because the answers are independent.
  3. Count how many different orders give the same totals. That count is the factorial term in the formula.
  4. Multiply the count by the probability of one sequence. That gives the probability of the whole outcome, such as "any 4 of 7 prefer orange".
  5. Repeat for every possible outcome, from 0 orange up to 7 orange. Together these probabilities form the binomial distribution.
  6. For the p-value, add the probability of the observed outcome plus every outcome that is equally likely or rarer, on both sides. That sum is the binomial test.

Formulas

Binomial probability
$$ p(x \mid n, p) \;=\; \frac{n!}{x!\,(n-x)!}\; p^{\,x}\,(1-p)^{\,n-x} $$

Symbols: \(x\) is the number of people who preferred orange Fanta. \(n\) is the total number of people asked. \(n-x\) is therefore the number who preferred grape. \(p\) is the probability that one person picks orange, and \(1-p\) is the probability that they pick grape. The exclamation mark is a factorial, so \(3! = 3 \times 2 \times 1 = 6\). What it tells you: it gives the probability of seeing exactly \(x\) orange answers out of \(n\) people, if every person answers independently with probability \(p\).

The factorial part is just a count
$$ \frac{n!}{x!\,(n-x)!} \;=\; \frac{3!}{2!\,(3-2)!} \;=\; \frac{6}{2 \times 1} \;=\; 3 $$

Symbols: same \(n\), \(x\) as above, here with \(n = 3\) and \(x = 2\). What it tells you: there are exactly 3 different orders in which 2 of 3 people can say orange. This matches the three rows drawn in the figure above, so the scary looking term is only a counter.

Binomial test p-value (two-sided)
$$ \text{p-value} \;=\; \sum_{\;k \,:\, p(k \mid n,p)\;\le\; p(x \mid n,p)} p(k \mid n, p) $$

Symbols: \(x\) is the observed count, \(k\) runs over every possible count from 0 to \(n\), and \(p(k \mid n,p)\) is the binomial probability of that count. The condition under the sum keeps only the counts that are equally likely or rarer than what you saw. What it tells you: how much of the "no preference" distribution is at least as extreme as your data. Including the low side as well as the high side is what makes it two-sided.

Worked example from the video

Example

Josh asks 7 people. Four prefer orange Fanta and three prefer grape. Under the no-preference model, \(n = 7\), \(x = 4\) and \(p = 0.5\). Plugging in gives \(p(4 \mid 7, 0.5) = 0.273\).

Now build the p-value. Start with the observed outcome and walk outward on the orange side: \(0.273\) for 4 orange, \(0.164\) for 5, \(0.055\) for 6, and \(0.008\) for 7. Those add to \(0.5\).

The grape side is a mirror image. Three orange out of seven has the same probability as four out of seven, so it is equally rare and must be included. Its four probabilities also add to \(0.5\).

The total is \(0.5 + 0.5 = 1\). The p-value is 1, so the no-preference model fits the data well. With only 7 people, we cannot rule out that both flavours are loved equally.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out

One sequence is not the same as one outcome. The value 0.125 is the chance that the first two people say orange and the third says grape, in that exact order. The chance that any two of three say orange is three times larger, at 0.375.

Watch out

A p-value is never a single probability from the distribution. It is the observed probability plus the probability of everything equally likely or rarer. Reporting 0.273 as the p-value for 4 of 7 would be wrong; the correct answer is 1.

Watch out

Because \(p = 0.5\) makes the distribution symmetric, the grape side mirrors the orange side exactly. You must still add that half in. A two-sided p-value counts every outcome that is equally likely or rarer, on both sides.

Watch out

The binomial distribution assumes independence. One person saying they like orange Fanta must not change the probability for the next person. If people influence each other, or you sample the same group repeatedly, the model no longer applies.

If they ask you in an interview

What does the binomial distribution model?
It models the number of successes in a fixed number of independent yes/no trials, where every trial has the same success probability. You give it \(n\), the number of trials, and \(p\), the success probability. It returns the probability of each possible count from 0 to \(n\). In the video the trials are people, success is choosing orange Fanta, and \(p = 0.5\) encodes "no real preference".
Why is there a factorial term in the formula?
Because more than one order of answers can produce the same count. Any one specific order has probability \(p^x (1-p)^{n-x}\), and the factorial term simply counts how many orders share that count. For 2 of 3 people it gives 3, which you can also verify by listing the sequences by hand.
What exactly is a binomial test?
It is a hypothesis test where the binomial distribution supplies the p-value. You fix a null probability, usually 0.5 for "no preference", then sum the probability of the observed count plus every count that is equally likely or rarer. If that sum is small, the null model fits badly and you reject it. If it is large, as in the 4 versus 3 example, you cannot rule the null model out.
Four of seven people preferred orange. Is that evidence of a preference?
No. The binomial test gives a p-value of 1, which is as unconvincing as a result can be. The observed count is the most likely outcome under the no-preference model, so nothing about it is surprising. The honest conclusion is that a sample of seven is too small to detect a preference of that size.
When does the binomial distribution stop being appropriate?
When the trials are not independent, or when the success probability changes between trials. Josh is explicit about this at the end of the video. If one person saying they like orange Fanta changes the chance for the next person, the multiplication of probabilities no longer holds.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • The binomial distribution gives the probability of each possible count of successes in \(n\) independent yes/no trials.
  • Set \(p = 0.5\) to model the idea that people have no real preference between the two flavours.
  • The probability of one specific order of answers is a simple product, such as \(0.5 \times 0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.125\).
  • The factorial term counts how many orders give the same count, so 2 of 3 orange has probability \(3 \times 0.125 = 0.375\).
  • Using the binomial distribution to compute a p-value is called a binomial test.
  • The p-value adds the observed probability plus everything equally likely or rarer, on both sides.
  • For 4 of 7 people preferring orange, the p-value is 1, so the no-preference model fits fine.
  • The model needs independent answers, so one person's choice must not change anyone else's probability.
