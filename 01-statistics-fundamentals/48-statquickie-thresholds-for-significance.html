StatQuickie: Thresholds for Significance

▶ Watch on YouTube The first StatQuickie: a short answer to a question from the comments

People keep asking Josh one question: is 0.05 always the right threshold for significance? This short video says the honest answer is "it depends on what you will do with the result".

In one breath

A threshold for significance is the p-value cut-off you agree on before you call a result real. The usual value, 0.05, was picked by convention, not by nature. It works well when you publish, because editors and reviewers expect it. When you only explore a free data set that you will confirm later, you can relax it. If the effect size is tiny, or the claim is extraordinary, a small p-value is not enough.

The main idea

The threshold, often written \(\alpha\), is the line you draw before the test. If the p-value falls below it, you call the result significant. Josh is clear that 0.05 has no biological or natural reason behind it. It is relatively arbitrary. Science has used it for a long time, and for the most part it does an okay job.

It survives because it hedges the bets. You are wrong about 5% of the time and right about 95%. For most of biological science that trade is cost-effective. The better question is how much evidence your situation needs.

Step by step

  1. Ask what you will do with the result. Publication, private exploration, and a headline claim need different amounts of evidence.
  2. If you plan to publish, use 0.05. That is what editors and reviewers expect. Going below it is even better.
  3. If you are only exploring a data set to generate hypotheses, relax. You will confirm the finding later anyway.
  4. Check the effect size next. Josh uses \(R^2\). If it is near 10%, your variable explains little, so a tiny p-value does not rescue the result.
  5. If the claim is extraordinary, demand extraordinary data. A p-value just under the line does not move a reader who starts out very sceptical.

Formulas

This video gives judgement rules, not derivations. Two expressions state them precisely.

The decision rule
$$ \text{significant} \iff p < \alpha, \qquad \alpha = 0.05 \text{ by convention} $$

Symbols: \(p\) is the p-value, the chance of seeing data at least this extreme if nothing real is going on. \(\alpha\) is the threshold you fix before you look at the result. What it tells you: you choose \(\alpha\), so you choose how often you accept being wrong. At 0.05 that is about 5% of the time.

Effect size, as Josh uses it
$$ R^2 = \frac{\text{variation explained by your variable}}{\text{total variation in the data}} $$

Symbols: \(R^2\) is the share of the variation in the data that your variable accounts for. People often report it as a percentage. What it tells you: \(R^2 = 0.10\) means your variable explains only 10% of what is happening, which is not much, whatever the p-value says.

Worked example from the video

Example

Josh imagines a study that proves extraterrestrials fly around New York City in UFOs. The test returns \(p = 0.0047\). That is below 0.05, so the standard rule calls it significant.

Is that good enough? Josh says absolutely not. The claim is extraordinary, so the data must be extraordinary too. You want a p-value so small that a reader thinks: I cannot explain that away, so I have to believe it.

His second example runs the other way. He has seen publications with \(p = 0.00000001\) and a horrible \(R^2\). The variable explains almost none of the variation, so the finding still does not matter.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out Treating 0.05 as a natural constant. It is a convention that hedges the bets well. It is not the optimal threshold for every question.
Watch out Reporting a tiny p-value with no effect size. A small p-value says something is probably there. \(R^2\) says whether it is big enough to care about.
Watch out Running analysis after analysis until something drops under 0.05. That is p-hacking.

If they ask you in an interview

Why do we use 0.05, and should we always use it?
It is a convention, not a natural constant. It hedges the bets well: wrong about 5% of the time, right about 95%. I keep it for work that will be published, because reviewers expect it. For exploratory work I am looser.
A model gives p = 0.00000001 but R² of 10%. What do you say?
I would say the effect is probably real but almost useless. The p-value only says the effect is unlikely to be noise. The \(R^2\) says my variable explains just a tenth of the variation. I would report both numbers together.
When would you demand a threshold much stricter than 0.05?
When the claim is extraordinary. If a result would overturn something people firmly believe, a p-value just under 0.05 will not convince anyone, and it should not. I would want a p-value small enough that noise is not a credible explanation, plus a solid effect size.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • The threshold for significance is the p-value cut-off you set before the test.
  • 0.05 is arbitrary in origin, but it hedges the bets well.
  • Use 0.05 when you will publish, because that is what editors and reviewers expect.
  • Relax the threshold when you are only exploring data that you will confirm later.
  • A small p-value with a poor \(R^2\) is not an interesting finding.
  • Extraordinary claims need extraordinary data, so a p-value of 0.0047 will not carry a UFO story.
  • Always report the p-value and the effect size together.
