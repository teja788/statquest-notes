In Statistics, Probability is not Likelihood.

▶ Watch on YouTube

In everyday speech, "probability" and "likelihood" mean the same thing. In statistics they mean two different things, and this video shows the difference with one picture each.

In one breath

Probability asks how much of a fixed curve falls inside a range of data. You read it as an area. Likelihood asks how well a curve fits data you already measured. You read it as a height. So in probability the curve is fixed and the data changes. In likelihood the data is fixed and the curve moves.

The main idea

Josh uses one example the whole way through: the weights of mice. The weights follow a normal distribution with mean 32 grams and standard deviation 2.5. The mean is the centre of the bell. The standard deviation says how wide the bell is. The weights run from 24 grams at the low end to 40 grams at the high end.

Now ask a probability question. What is the chance a randomly selected mouse weighs between 32 and 34 grams? You have not weighed any mouse yet. The answer is the area under the curve between those two weights. That area is 0.29, so the chance is 29 percent.

Notice what stays fixed. The curve never moves. You only change the range of weights you ask about. The same idea works for every continuous distribution.

Step by step

  1. Fix the distribution. Here it is a normal curve with mean 32 grams and standard deviation 2.5.
  2. Choose a range of weights, such as 32 to 34 grams. This is the part you may change.
  3. Read the area under the curve for that range. It is 0.29, and that is the probability.
  4. Now switch to likelihood. Weigh the mouse first. It weighs 34 grams, and that value is now fixed.
  5. Read the height of the curve above 34 grams. It is 0.12, the likelihood of this distribution.
  6. Slide the distribution so its mean sits at 34. The height above the same mouse is now 0.21.

Formulas

Probability of data
$$ p(\,32 < \text{weight} < 34 \;\mid\; \mu = 32,\; \sigma = 2.5\,) = 0.29 $$

Symbols: \(p\) is probability. The bar \(\mid\) means "given". \(\mu\) is the mean, the centre of the curve. \(\sigma\) is the standard deviation, the width of the curve. What it tells you: the curve on the right is fixed, and the area over the range on the left is 0.29.

Likelihood of a distribution
$$ L(\,\mu = 32,\; \sigma = 2.5 \;\mid\; \text{weight} = 34\,) = 0.12 $$

Symbols: \(L\) is likelihood. The left side now holds the curve you are testing, and the right side holds the weight you already measured. What it tells you: with the 34 gram mouse held fixed, this particular curve has height 0.12 above it.

The pattern to remember
$$ p(\text{data} \mid \text{distribution}) \qquad\text{versus}\qquad L(\text{distribution} \mid \text{data}) $$

Symbols: "distribution" means the mean and standard deviation. What it tells you: the thing before the bar is what you ask about. The thing after the bar is held fixed.

Worked example from the video

Example

Before weighing anything, the area from 32 to 34 grams is 0.29. Ask instead for "more than 34 grams" and you shade a different piece of the same curve.

Now weigh a mouse. It is 34 grams. The curve with mean 32 has height 0.12 above it. Slide that curve right until its mean is 34, and the height becomes 0.21. The second curve fits this mouse better.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out A likelihood is not a probability. The value 0.12 is a height on the y axis, not a chance of anything. Areas under a continuous curve add up to 1, but heights do not have to.
Watch out Read the bar in the right direction. Whatever sits after the bar is the given. Swapping the two sides is the most common slip, and Josh says even he mixes them up.
Watch out A single point has no area. That is why we do not talk about the probability of a mouse weighing exactly 34 grams, and why likelihood reads the height of the curve instead.

If they ask you in an interview

What is the difference between probability and likelihood?
Probability holds the distribution fixed and asks about the data. It is the area under the curve over a range, so 32 to 34 grams gives 0.29. Likelihood holds the data fixed and asks about the distribution. It is the height above a measurement, so a 34 gram mouse gives 0.12.
Why do we use the height of the curve for likelihood instead of an area?
Likelihood starts after the data is collected, so we look at one exact value, not a range. For a continuous curve the area over a single point is zero, so that would tell us nothing. The height at that point is not zero, and it changes as we move the curve. So it can rank one distribution against another.
How does this connect to maximum likelihood estimation?
Once the data is fixed, we slide and stretch the distribution and watch the likelihood change. Moving the mean from 32 to 34 raised the likelihood from 0.12 to 0.21. Maximum likelihood estimation does this search properly. It picks the mean and standard deviation that make the observed data as likely as possible.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • Probability is an area under a fixed curve, and you change the range of data.
  • With mean 32 and standard deviation 2.5, the area from 32 to 34 grams is 0.29.
  • Likelihood is a height on a curve, read above data you already measured.
  • A 34 gram mouse gives 0.12 under the curve with mean 32, and 0.21 once the mean moves to 34.
  • Write probability as \(p(\text{data} \mid \text{distribution})\) and likelihood as \(L(\text{distribution} \mid \text{data})\).
  • Higher likelihood means the distribution fits the data better, which is the whole basis of maximum likelihood estimation.
