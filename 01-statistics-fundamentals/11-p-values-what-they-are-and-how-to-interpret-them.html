p-values: What they are and how to interpret them

▶ Watch on YouTube

A p-value is a number between 0 and 1. It says how confident you can be that two groups are really different, and not just different by luck.

In one breath

Josh compares drug A with drug B. Testing one person per drug proves nothing, because random things happen to people. With many people, a huge gap between the drugs is clearly real. When the gap is small, a p-value decides. You compare it to a threshold picked in advance, usually 0.05, which is the false positive rate you accept.

The main idea

Imagine two drugs, drug A and drug B. You give each one to a single person. The person on drug A is cured; the person on drug B is not. You cannot conclude that drug A is better.

Drug B could have failed for ordinary reasons: a bad reaction with another medicine, a rare allergy, or a missed dose. Drug A may not even work, with the placebo effect deserving all the credit.

So you redo the experiment with two people per drug. Both people on drug A are cured. On drug B, one is cured and one is not. You still cannot answer, because a drug may have been mislabelled.

Now test many people. Drug A cures 1,043 of its 1,046 people, which is 99.7%. Drug B cures only 2 of its 1,434 people, which is 0.1%. A few placebo cures and a few rare allergies cannot explain a gap that large. Random chance is not a serious explanation here.

Now change the numbers. Drug A cures 37% of its group and drug B cures 31%. Drug A still looks better, but only a little. No study is perfect, so how confident can you be? That is the question a p-value answers. The closer it is to 0, the more confident you are that the drugs differ.

Step by step

  1. Write down the null hypothesis: the claim that drug A and drug B are the same. This is the boring explanation you want to rule out.
  2. Pick a threshold before you look at the results. The common choice is 0.05.
  3. Run the experiment on enough people. With one or two people per group, random events decide the outcome.
  4. Calculate the p-value from the data. It is one number between 0 and 1.
  5. Compare the p-value to the threshold. If it is smaller, reject the null hypothesis and call the drugs different.
  6. If it is larger, say you failed to see a difference. That is not the same as proving the drugs are equal.
  7. Remember the price. A 0.05 threshold means you accept a wrong "different" call 5% of the time.

Step seven deserves a picture. Imagine giving the same drug to two different groups. Any difference now comes only from random things, such as a rare allergy or a strong placebo reaction. Usually the p-value is large, for example 0.9, and you correctly fail to see a difference.

But once in a while all the rare allergies land in one group, and all the strong placebo reactions land in the other. The p-value for that run can be 0.01, so you call the groups different even though they took the same drug. That is a false positive.

The threshold is your choice. If being correct really matters, use 0.00001, which gives one false positive per 100,000 experiments. For a light question, such as when the ice cream truck arrives, 0.2 is fine and accepts two false positives in ten. Most people stay at 0.05, because pushing below 5% usually costs more than it is worth.

The fancy names are simple. Deciding whether the drugs are the same is called hypothesis testing. The claim that they are the same is the null hypothesis. The p-value helps you decide whether to reject it.

Formulas

Josh does not show how a p-value is calculated. He shows how to read one and how to act on it. These short rules capture what he says.

The decision rule
$$ \text{reject } H_0 \quad \text{when} \quad p < \alpha $$

Symbols: \(H_0\) is the null hypothesis, the claim that drug A and drug B are the same. \(p\) is the p-value from your data. \(\alpha\) (alpha) is the threshold you chose before the experiment, usually 0.05. What it tells you: the whole decision is one comparison between two numbers.

What the threshold buys you
$$ P\big(p < \alpha \;\big|\; H_0 \text{ is true}\big) = \alpha $$

Symbols: \(P(\cdot)\) means "the probability that", and the vertical bar means "given that". So the left side is the chance of a small p-value in a world where the drugs really are the same. What it tells you: \(\alpha\) is the false positive rate you accept. Run \(N\) such experiments and expect about \(N \times \alpha\) false positives.

The definition behind Josh's plain words
$$ p = P\big(\text{a result this different or more} \;\big|\; H_0 \text{ is true}\big) $$

Symbols: the same \(P\), bar and \(H_0\) as above. "This different or more" means the gap you saw, or a bigger one. What it tells you: the p-value assumes there is no difference, so it is never the probability that the drugs are the same.

Worked example from the video

Example

Josh's second set of results is the interesting one. Drug A cured 37% of its group, and drug B cured 31%. The gap is 6 percentage points, so drug A looks better.

The threshold was fixed at 0.05 before the experiment. The p-value for this data is 0.24. Now do the one comparison that matters:

$$ p = 0.24 \quad\text{and}\quad \alpha = 0.05 \quad\Rightarrow\quad 0.24 > 0.05 $$

So you do not reject the null hypothesis, and you are not confident that the drugs differ.

Josh then shows the reverse case. A much larger experiment gives a p-value of 0.04, small enough to reject the null hypothesis, even though the gap is only 1 percentage point.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out A small p-value does not tell you how big the difference is. In the video a 6-point gap gives p = 0.24, while a 1-point gap in a larger study gives p = 0.04. Always report the size of the difference next to the p-value.
Watch out A large p-value is not proof that the two groups are the same. Josh is careful to say you "failed to see a difference". A real difference may exist that your study was too small to detect.
Watch out False positives do not disappear with careful work. With a 0.05 threshold, 5% of experiments that have no real difference still give a p-value below 0.05. Run twenty such tests and expect about one to look significant.

If they ask you in an interview

What is a p-value, in plain language?
It is a number between 0 and 1 that measures how surprising my data would be if there were no real difference. A value close to 0 means the data would be surprising, so I have confidence the groups differ. A value close to 1 means the data look like ordinary random variation. It never tells me how large the difference is.
Why 0.05, and what does that threshold actually control?
0.05 is a convention rather than a rule of nature. It controls the false positive rate. If the groups really are the same and I repeat the experiment many times, about 5% of runs still give a p-value below 0.05. I lower the threshold when a wrong claim would be costly, and raise it when the decision is low stakes.
Your p-value is 0.24. Can you say the two drugs are the same?
No. I can only say I failed to see a difference at my threshold. The data are consistent with no difference, but also with a real difference my study was too small to detect. I would report the observed gap alongside the p-value.
A colleague reports p = 0.001 and calls the effect large. What do you say?
I would separate the two questions. The p-value says the difference is unlikely to be pure chance, and that is all it says. In the video a one-point gap gives p = 0.04 while a six-point gap gives p = 0.24. So I would ask for the size of the effect before agreeing it is large.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • Random things happen to individual people, so tiny experiments cannot settle anything.
  • When drug A cures 99.7% and drug B cures 0.1%, chance is not a believable explanation.
  • A p-value sits between 0 and 1, and closer to 0 means more confidence that the groups differ.
  • You compare it to a threshold chosen beforehand, usually 0.05, which is your false positive rate: 5% wrong calls when nothing really differs.
  • The null hypothesis says the groups are the same, and a small p-value lets you reject it.
  • A small p-value never tells you how big the difference is, so always report the effect size too.
