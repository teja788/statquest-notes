Fisher's Exact Test and the Hypergeometric Distribution

▶ Watch on YouTube

This video shows how to get a p-value from a small sample when you keep what you draw. Josh does it with one handful of M&Ms.

In one breath

Josh takes one handful from a bag of 40 M&Ms and gets 7 blue and 1 red. The bag should hold only 8 blue, so the handful looks very blue. He gets its probability by multiplying fractions that shrink after every draw. He then adds every result that is equally rare or rarer. That sum is the p-value, 0.01.

The main idea

Josh assumes the bag holds two servings of 20, so 40 M&Ms in total. He looked up the published colour proportions to build an ideal bag. That ideal bag has 8 blue and 5 red. His handful of 8 holds 7 blue, so a rare colour fills almost the whole hand.

The key point is that M&Ms are not put back. Every draw shrinks the bag and changes the counts. So each probability depends on what you already took. Counting this way is called sampling without replacement, and the hypergeometric distribution describes it.

Step by step

  1. Fix the population. The bag has 40 M&Ms, of which 8 are blue and 5 are red.
  2. Pick one exact order, say seven blues then the red. The first draw is blue with probability 8/40.
  3. Take that blue out. Only 7 blues remain among 39 M&Ms, so the next probability is 7/39, then 6/38, and so on.
  4. After seven blues, 33 M&Ms are left and 5 are red. The last draw is red with probability 5/33.
  5. Multiply the eight fractions. That is the probability of this one exact order.
  6. Order does not matter, so add all 8 orderings of 7 blue and 1 red.
  7. Add the probability of every result that is equally rare or rarer, such as 8 blues, or 7 oranges and 1 blue.
  8. That final sum is 0.01. The whole procedure is Fisher's exact test.

Formulas

One exact order
$$ \frac{8}{40}\cdot\frac{7}{39}\cdot\frac{6}{38}\cdot\frac{5}{37}\cdot\frac{4}{36}\cdot\frac{3}{35}\cdot\frac{2}{34}\cdot\frac{5}{33} \approx 6.5\times10^{-8} $$

Symbols: each top number is how many M&Ms of the wanted colour are left. Each bottom number is how many M&Ms are left in total. What it tells you: the chance of seven blues and then one red, in that exact order.

Hypergeometric probability
$$ P(X=k)=\frac{\binom{K}{k}\binom{N-K}{n-k}}{\binom{N}{n}} $$

Symbols: \(N\) is the bag size, \(K\) is how many in the bag are your colour, \(n\) is how many you draw, \(k\) is how many of that colour you got. \(\binom{a}{b}\) counts the ways to choose \(b\) things from \(a\). What it tells you: the same answer as the chain of fractions, with the orderings already counted.

The p-value
$$ p=\sum_{\text{outcomes as rare or rarer}} P(\text{outcome}) = 0.01 $$

Symbols: the sum runs over every handful whose probability is at most the probability of the handful we saw. What it tells you: how often a normal bag looks this surprising, so 0.01 means about one bag in a hundred.

Worked example from the video

Example

The bag holds 40 M&Ms: 8 blue and 5 red. Josh draws 8 and gets 7 blue and 1 red.

For one exact order he multiplies 8/40, 7/39, 6/38, 5/37, 4/36, 3/35 and 2/34 for the blues, then 5/33 for the red. That gives about \(6.5\times10^{-8}\). The 8 orderings together give about \(5.2\times10^{-7}\).

That is still one outcome. The p-value also needs 8 blues, 7 oranges with 1 blue, and everything equally rare or rarer. A computer adds them all and returns 0.01, so the bag is special.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out A very small probability is not a p-value. This handful had probability \(5\times10^{-7}\), but the p-value was 0.01. The p-value is a sum over many outcomes, so it is always larger.
Watch out "Equally rare or rarer" is not the same as "more blue". It includes odd outcomes such as 7 oranges and 1 blue, because those are just as surprising.
Watch out The answer depends on the assumed bag. Josh assumed 40 M&Ms and used published colour proportions. Change either one and the p-value moves.

If they ask you in an interview

What is the hypergeometric distribution?
It is the distribution of how many "successes" you get when you draw a fixed number of items from a finite group without replacement. In the M&M example it gives the chance of 7 blue in a handful of 8, from a bag of 40 with 8 blue.
How do you get from a probability to a p-value here?
I compute the probability of the result I saw. Then I add every other outcome that is equally likely or less likely. That sum is the p-value. It says how often a normal bag looks at least this strange.
Where does this show up in real data science work?
Enrichment analysis is the case Josh names. I ask whether my gene list has more metabolism genes than normal. The arithmetic is the M&M calculation, with genes instead of colours.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • Josh assumes a bag of 40 M&Ms with 8 blue and 5 red, then draws a handful of 8.
  • His handful has 7 blue and 1 red, far more blue than the bag should give.
  • Because he keeps each M&M, the probabilities shrink from 8/40 down to 5/33.
  • The p-value adds every outcome that is equally rare or rarer, and it comes to 0.01.
  • That procedure is Fisher's exact test, and it rests on the hypergeometric distribution.
  • The same calculation answers enrichment questions about gene lists.
