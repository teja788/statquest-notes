Quantile-Quantile Plots (QQ plots)

▶ Watch on YouTube Assumes you know what a quantile is (see video 51).

A QQ plot is a picture that answers one question: does my data follow a given distribution? It compares the quantiles of the data with the quantiles of a reference distribution.

In one breath

Josh measures expression in 15 genes and asks if the data is normally distributed. He cuts the data into 15 quantiles, then cuts a normal curve into 15 groups of equal probability. He pairs the quantiles in rank order and draws each pair as a dot. Dots on a straight line mean the two shapes match. Here the normal curve fits poorly, but a uniform distribution fits well.

The main idea

A quantile is a cut point that splits data into groups of the same size. The clever part is that the same idea works on a smooth curve. For a normal curve, "equal-sized groups" means groups with equal probability.

Values near the middle of a normal curve are common, so the middle groups come out narrow. Values far out in the tails are rare, so the edge groups must be wide to hold the same probability. That is why the cut lines are not evenly spaced.

You now have two lists of the same length. A QQ plot puts one list on each axis and draws them as pairs. If the two shapes agree, the pairs climb together in a straight line.

Step by step

  1. Give each data point its own quantile. With 15 measurements you get 15 cut lines that split the data into equal-sized groups.
  2. Get yourself a reference distribution. Any normal curve will do; its centre and width do not matter.
  3. Add the same number of cut lines to the curve, each group holding equal probability.
  4. Plot the QQ graph. Data quantiles go on the Y axis and reference quantiles on the X axis. Put a dot where each horizontal and vertical line meet.

When every dot is on the graph, draw a straight line through them and look. In the video the dots do not sit well on the line, so Josh runs the same four steps again with a uniform distribution. This time the dots hug the line, so the uniform distribution describes the data better.

The same method also compares two data sets. Josh compares the gene data with a much smaller second one. That set gives only four quartiles. Quartiles are just quantiles when you have four of them. So he takes four quartiles from the original data too, and plots the four pairs. With so few dots the answer stays weak.

Formulas

The video does all its work by drawing, not by algebra. One standard rule still tells you where the reference quantiles sit, and it lands close to Josh's numbers.

Reference quantile position
$$ x_i = \Phi^{-1}\!\left(\frac{i}{n+1}\right) $$

Symbols: \(n\) is how many data points you have, 15 here. \(i\) is the rank of a point, so \(i=1\) is the smallest. \(\Phi^{-1}\) is the inverse normal CDF. You give it a probability, and it returns the value on the standard normal curve with that much area to its left. What it tells you: the \(i\)-th reference quantile cuts off a fraction \(i/(n+1)\) of the curve's area. That is what "equal-probability groups" means.

Worked example from the video

Example

Josh reads the first three normal quantiles off the curve as -1.5, -1.2 and -0.89. The first three quantiles in the data are 0.6, 1.1 and 1.9.

So the first three dots sit at \((-1.5,\,0.6)\), \((-1.2,\,1.1)\) and \((-0.89,\,1.9)\). Repeat until all 15 pairs are drawn. Then judge how straight the cloud of dots looks.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out A QQ plot checks shape, not values. The dots only need to lie on a straight line, not on the 45-degree line. That is why any normal curve works: a different centre or width just changes the line's intercept and slope.
Watch out Few quantiles means a weak answer. In the four-quartile comparison Josh cannot decide anything firm. Judge a QQ plot with the sample size in mind.
Watch out A bad fit does not mean your data is broken. It only means this reference distribution is wrong. Josh simply tries a uniform distribution next, and it fits.

If they ask you in an interview

What does a QQ plot actually show?
It plots the quantiles of my data against the quantiles of a reference distribution, one pair per dot. If the two have the same shape, the dots fall on a straight line. So a QQ plot is a visual test of distributional shape, not of the raw values.
Why are the cut lines on the normal curve unevenly spaced?
Each group has to hold the same probability, not the same width. The middle of the curve is dense, so those groups are narrow. The tails are sparse, so the edge groups have to be wide to collect the same probability.
The dots are on a straight line, but not the diagonal. Is that a problem?
No. Straightness is the thing that matters, because it means the shapes agree. The slope reflects a difference in spread and the intercept a difference in centre, and both are easy to correct. I would only worry if the dots curved or bent.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • A QQ plot asks whether your data follows a chosen distribution.
  • Cut your data into quantiles, then cut the reference distribution into the same number of equal-probability groups.
  • Equal probability makes the middle groups narrow and the edge groups wide.
  • Plot data quantiles on Y against reference quantiles on X, one dot per pair.
  • Dots on a straight line mean the two shapes match; curving dots mean they do not.
  • The gene data fits a uniform distribution far better than a normal curve.
  • The same recipe compares two data sets, but few quantiles give a weak answer.
