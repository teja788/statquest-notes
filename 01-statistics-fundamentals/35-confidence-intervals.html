Confidence Intervals

▶ Watch on YouTube

A confidence interval is simply the range that covers 95% of the means you get when you bootstrap your sample. Once you see it that way, it stops being mysterious.

In one breath

You measure a small sample, such as 12 female mice. You bootstrap that sample many times and collect thousands of means. The interval that covers the middle 95% of those means is the 95% confidence interval. Anything outside it happens less than 5% of the time, so a confidence interval works as a statistical test you can do with your eyes.

The main idea

Josh starts with a bootstrapping refresher, because that is what makes confidence intervals click. Bootstrapping means you draw a new sample of the same size from your original sample, at random. The same measurement may be picked more than once. That is called sampling with replacement.

Each bootstrapped sample gives you one mean. Repeat this many times, sometimes more than 10,000 times. You end up with a big pile of means. That pile shows which values are reasonable for the true mean of all female mice.

A confidence interval is just a bar drawn across that pile. A 95% interval covers 95% of the bootstrapped means. A 99% interval covers 99% of them, so it is wider. That is all it is.

Step by step

  1. Weigh your sample. Josh weighs 12 female mice, not every mouse on the planet.
  2. Calculate the sample mean. It is only an estimate of the true mean.
  3. Bootstrap once. Pick 12 weights at random from the original 12, with replacement, then take the mean.
  4. Repeat step 3 many times. Josh mentions more than 10,000 repeats.
  5. Draw the bar that covers 95% of those means. That is your 95% confidence interval.

Formulas

The video builds the interval by counting, not by algebra. These two lines write that rule down.

Bootstrap percentile interval
$$ \text{CI}_{95\%} = \big[\; q_{2.5}(\bar{x}^{*}),\;\; q_{97.5}(\bar{x}^{*}) \;\big] $$

Symbols: \(\bar{x}^{*}\) is the pile of means from all the bootstrapped samples. \(q_{2.5}\) is the value below which 2.5% of them fall. \(q_{97.5}\) is the value below which 97.5% of them fall. What it tells you: drop the lowest 2.5% and the highest 2.5%, and the middle 95% is left.

The usual formula you meet in the wild
$$ \bar{x} \;\pm\; 1.96 \cdot \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} $$

Symbols: \(\bar{x}\) is the sample mean, \(s\) is the sample standard deviation, and \(n\) is the sample size. The term \(s/\sqrt{n}\) is the standard error of the mean. What it tells you: Josh says intervals are usually built by a formula, not by bootstrapping. Both routes are read the same way.

Worked example from the video

Example

Josh asks whether the true mean weight of all female mice could be less than 20. He draws the 95% confidence interval across the bootstrapped means from the 12 mice.

The region left of 20 sits outside the interval. The interval holds 95% of the means, so that outside region holds less than 5% of them. The probability that the true mean lands there is below 0.05.

So \(p < 0.05\), and the true mean is significantly different from any value below 20. No test was calculated; the picture did the work.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out Overlapping intervals do not mean "no difference". Josh is explicit here. The two means may still be significantly different, so you have to run the t-test.
Watch out The interval covers plausible values for the true mean, not the spread of individual mice. It is built from means, so it is narrow.
Watch out Wider is not better. A 99% interval is wider only because it must cover more of the means.

If they ask you in an interview

What is a 95% confidence interval, in plain words?
If I bootstrap my sample thousands of times and record the mean each time, the 95% confidence interval is the range that covers 95% of those means. It tells me which values are reasonable for the true mean of the whole population. Values outside it turn up less than 5% of the time.
How can a confidence interval act as a statistical test?
The interval covers 95% of the means, so anything outside it happens less than 5% of the time. If a value I care about falls outside, its p-value is below 0.05. I read significance straight off the plot.
Two groups have overlapping confidence intervals. Are their means the same?
I cannot say that. Overlap only means the picture is not conclusive, so I would run a t-test. If the intervals do not overlap, the means are significantly different.
Do you have to bootstrap to get a confidence interval?
No. Bootstrapping is one route, and it is the easiest one to picture. Most intervals come from a formula instead, and you read the result the same way.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • Bootstrapping means resampling your data with replacement and taking the mean each time.
  • Repeat it thousands of times and you get a large pile of plausible means.
  • A 95% confidence interval covers 95% of those means; a 99% interval covers 99% and is wider.
  • Anything outside a 95% interval happens less than 5% of the time, so its p-value is below 0.05.
  • That makes a confidence interval a statistical test you can perform by eye.
  • If two intervals do not overlap, the means are significantly different.
  • If they do overlap, the answer is still open, so run the t-test.
