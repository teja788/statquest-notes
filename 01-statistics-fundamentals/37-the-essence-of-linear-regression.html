The Essence of Linear Regression

▶ Watch on YouTube

Linear regression fits a straight line to data so you can predict one thing from another. This video also shows how to say how much confidence you should have in that prediction.

In one breath

Josh works for a food store chain. The boss asks whether the company should build three new stores, and how confident the answer is. Josh fits a line to store counts and revenue, and picks the line that makes the sum of the squared residuals as small as possible. He then measures accuracy with \(R^2\), and measures luck with a p value. The line predicts higher revenue, but the p value is 0.53, so the honest answer is to collect more data first.

The main idea

Three companies are in the data. Squatch owns 2 stores and makes 3 units of revenue. Norm owns 12 stores and makes 12.5. Gamma owns 15 stores and makes 7. More stores seem to bring more revenue, but the trend is not perfect. Norm has the most revenue with fewer stores than Gamma.

Because the trend is not perfect, no line goes through all three points. A line through Norm and Squatch predicts 15.3 for Gamma, whose real revenue is 7. A line through Norm and Gamma sends Squatch's prediction off the screen. So we need a score that says how good a line is, instead of guessing by eye.

That score starts with a residual. A residual is the observed value minus the value the line predicts. We draw it as a vertical gap between a point and the line. A line that fits well has small residuals everywhere.

Step by step

  1. Plot the data and draw a candidate line. For each point, read the predicted revenue off the line at the same number of stores.
  2. Calculate each residual as observed minus predicted. For one early line in the video the residuals are −4.5 for Gamma, +2 for Norm and −3 for Squatch.
  3. Do not simply add the residuals. Their sum is −5.5, and a much worse black line gives the same sum, because negatives cancel positives.
  4. Square each residual first, then add them. Squaring makes every term positive, and it keeps the later calculus easy. The blue line scores 33.25 and the black line scores 355.125, so the blue line wins.
  5. Now search for the best line. Keep the slope fixed, move the y-axis intercept up in small steps, and plot the sum of squared residuals for each intercept. The scores fall, reach a bottom, then rise again.
  6. Find the bottom of that curve by taking the derivative and solving for derivative equal to zero. Do the same for the slope. This method is called least squares.
  7. The solutions are exact formulas. They look messy, so a computer plugs the data in for you. Here they give intercept 3 and slope 0.5.
  8. Judge the line. Use \(R^2\) to measure how much the line improves on the mean, and a p value to measure how often random data would do as well.

Formulas

The fitted line
$$ \hat{y} = b + m\,x \qquad\text{here}\qquad \hat{y} = 3 + 0.5\,x $$

Symbols: \(x\) is the number of stores, the input. \(\hat{y}\) is the revenue the line predicts. \(b\) is the y-axis intercept, the predicted revenue when \(x\) is zero. \(m\) is the slope, the extra revenue for one more store. What it tells you: once you know \(b\) and \(m\), any store count gives a revenue prediction.

Residual and sum of squared residuals
$$ r_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i \qquad\qquad \text{SSR} = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_i - \hat{y}_i\right)^2 $$

Symbols: \(y_i\) is the observed revenue of company \(i\). \(\hat{y}_i\) is the revenue the line predicts for it. \(r_i\) is the residual, the vertical gap. \(n\) is the number of data points, three here. The \(\sum\) sign means add over all points. What it tells you: SSR is one number that scores a whole line, and a smaller SSR means better predictions.

Least squares condition
$$ \frac{d\,\text{SSR}}{d\,b} = 0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac{d\,\text{SSR}}{d\,m} = 0 $$

Symbols: the derivative \(d\,\text{SSR}/d\,b\) is the slope of the score curve as the intercept \(b\) changes. The second one is the same idea for the slope \(m\). What it tells you: the score curve is flat at its lowest point, so setting both derivatives to zero gives the best intercept and slope. The answers are exact formulas, and a computer evaluates them for you.

R squared
$$ R^2 = \frac{\text{SSR}_{\text{mean}} - \text{SSR}_{\text{line}}}{\text{SSR}_{\text{mean}}} $$

Symbols: \(\text{SSR}_{\text{mean}}\) is the sum of squared residuals around the mean revenue, that is, predictions made without the store count. \(\text{SSR}_{\text{line}}\) is the same sum around the fitted line. What it tells you: the share of the error that disappears when you use the store count. It runs from 0, when the line is no better than the mean, to 1, when the line fits perfectly.

The p value, as Josh builds it
$$ p = \Pr\!\left(R^2_{\text{random}} \ge R^2_{\text{observed}}\right) $$

Symbols: \(R^2_{\text{observed}}\) is the value from your real data. \(R^2_{\text{random}}\) is the value from the same number of random points, repeated thousands of times to make a histogram. What it tells you: the share of random data sets that match or beat your fit. A large p value means luck alone often does as well, so trust the line less.

Worked example from the video

Example

Least squares gives the line revenue = 3 + 0.5 × stores. The predictions are 4 for Squatch, 9 for Norm and 10.5 for Gamma. The residuals are −1, +3.5 and −3.5, so \(\text{SSR}_{\text{line}} = 1 + 12.25 + 12.25 = 25.5\).

The mean revenue is 7.5. Around the mean the residuals are −4.5, +5 and −0.5, so \(\text{SSR}_{\text{mean}} = 20.25 + 25 + 0.25 = 45.5\). Then \(R^2 = (45.5 - 25.5)/45.5 = 0.44\). Using the store count removes 44% of the error.

Josh then fits lines to three random points thousands of times. In that histogram, 53% of the random \(R^2\) values reach 0.44 or more, so the p value is 0.53.

The business answer: with 4 stores now and 3 more, the company would have 7 stores, and the line predicts \(3 + 0.5 \times 7 = 6.5\) revenue. But \(R^2\) is only middling and the p value is high. So tell the boss that revenue may rise, and that you should gather more data before you build.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out Never score a line by adding raw residuals. A negative residual can cancel a positive one. In the video a good line and a clearly bad line both sum to −5.5. Square first, then add.
Watch out Residuals are vertical, not perpendicular to the line. The vertical gap compares the observed and predicted revenue at the same number of stores, which is the comparison you care about.
Watch out A perfect \(R^2\) can be meaningless. Any two random points sit on a line exactly, so their \(R^2\) is always 1 and the p value is 1.0. \(R^2\) alone says nothing about confidence, so always read it with the p value.
Watch out Do not memorise the least squares formulas. Josh is clear that you only need to know they exist and that software evaluates them. Spend your effort on reading \(R^2\) and the p value instead.

If they ask you in an interview

Why square the residuals instead of taking absolute values?
Both tricks stop positive and negative errors from cancelling. Squaring wins because the maths is easier later. A squared term has a clean derivative, so least squares has an exact formula. Absolute values give a kink that is harder to differentiate.
What does R squared actually measure, and why compare against the mean?
R squared is the fraction of the squared error that disappears when you use the predictor instead of ignoring it. We compare against the mean because the mean is the best possible prediction when you have no predictor. It is the horizontal line with the smallest sum of squared residuals, so it is a fair baseline.
The R squared is high. Why do you still report a p value?
Because a high R squared can come from luck, especially with few points. Two random points always give an R squared of 1. The p value asks how often random data of the same size would match or beat your fit. Only a small p value lets you say the relationship is more than chance.
How would you explain the p value here to a non technical manager?
I would say we shuffled the world thousands of times and fitted a line to random points each time. Then we counted how often the random fit was as good as ours. In this example that happened 53% of the time, so our result is well inside what chance produces, and we should collect more data before we act on it.
Your model says revenue rises with stores. Do you recommend building?
Not yet on this evidence. The line predicts revenue of 6.5 at seven stores, which is encouraging. But R squared is 0.44, so the predictions are only moderately accurate, and the p value of 0.53 means chance explains the fit just as well. I would recommend more data, or more predictors, before we commit capital.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • Linear regression fits a straight line so one variable can predict another.
  • A residual is the observed value minus the value the line predicts, measured vertically.
  • Raw residuals cancel each other, so we square them and add them to get the SSR.
  • Least squares is the method that picks the intercept and slope with the smallest SSR, found where the derivative is zero.
  • R squared compares the SSR around the line with the SSR around the mean, and runs from 0 to 1.
  • The p value tells you how often random data of the same size would fit at least as well.
  • In the store example the fit is revenue = 3 + 0.5 × stores, with R squared 0.44 and a p value of 0.53.
  • Those numbers together say the trend may be real but is not yet trustworthy, so gather more data.
