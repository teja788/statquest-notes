How to calculate p-values

▶ Watch on YouTube

A p-value is not the probability of the thing you saw. It is a sum of three probabilities. This video shows how to build that sum, first by counting coin flips and then by adding areas under a curve.

In one breath

Josh flips a coin and gets two heads. The null hypothesis says the coin is still a normal coin. To test it, you add three parts: the chance of the observation, the chance of anything equally rare, and the chance of anything rarer or more extreme. For coins you count outcomes, and for continuous data like height you add areas under a distribution. If the total is below 0.05, you reject the null hypothesis.

The main idea

There are two kinds of p-value: one-sided and two-sided. Two-sided p-values are the common kind, and Josh calculates those all the way through. He returns to one-sided p-values at the end, to show why they are risky.

You want to know if your coin is special. Statistics asks the opposite question. The null hypothesis is the boring claim that your coin is no different from a normal coin. A small p-value tells you to reject that claim. Only then can you say the coin is special.

A p-value has three parts, and this is the core of the whole video. Part one is the probability that random chance alone gives your observation. Part two is the probability of seeing something else that is equally rare. Part three is the probability of seeing something rarer or more extreme. You add all three together.

Josh explains parts two and three with flowers. If you give someone the rarest flower of its species, it feels special. If you say many other flowers are equally rare, it feels much less special. If you say many flowers are rarer still, it feels ordinary. So equally rare things and rarer things both reduce how surprising your result is, and both belong in the p-value.

Step by step

  1. Write the null hypothesis. It always says the boring thing, for example "my coin is no different from a normal coin".
  2. List every possible outcome and how likely each one is. Two flips give four outcomes. Five flips give 32 outcomes.
  3. Take part one: the probability that random chance gives exactly what you observed.
  4. Add part two: the probability of every other outcome that is equally rare.
  5. Add part three: the probability of every outcome that is rarer or more extreme.
  6. Compare the total with 0.05. If it is smaller, reject the null hypothesis. If not, you failed to reject it.

Counting works for coins because the list of outcomes is short. Height does not work that way, since a person can be any value in a range. For continuous measurements you use a distribution instead, which is a curve whose area gives probability. Then part one, part two and part three become areas under that curve, and "more extreme" means "further from the mean".

Formulas

The three parts of a p-value
$$ p = P_{\text{observed}} + P_{\text{equally rare}} + P_{\text{rarer or more extreme}} $$

Symbols: \(p\) is the p-value. \(P_{\text{observed}}\) is the probability that random chance alone produces the result you saw. \(P_{\text{equally rare}}\) is the probability of any other result that is just as unlikely. \(P_{\text{rarer or more extreme}}\) is the probability of every result that is even less likely, or further from the mean. What it tells you: a p-value measures how ordinary your result is inside a crowd of equally surprising and more surprising results, not how likely your result is on its own.

Probability when all outcomes are equally likely
$$ P(\text{outcome}) = \frac{\text{number of ways to get that outcome}}{\text{total number of outcomes}} $$

Symbols: the numerator counts the arrangements that give the result, and the denominator counts every arrangement that could happen. What it tells you: with coins you can get every probability by counting, because order does not change the chance of heads on the next flip, so all arrangements are equally likely.

The decision rule
$$ \text{reject the null hypothesis if } p < 0.05 $$

Symbols: \(p\) is the p-value you calculated, and 0.05 is the usual threshold for calling a result significant. What it tells you: below the threshold you say the data are surprising enough to abandon the boring explanation. Above it you simply failed to reject that explanation.

Worked example from the video

Example

Flip a coin twice and get two heads. The four outcomes are HH, HT, TH and TT, so \(P(\text{two heads}) = 1/4 = 0.25\). Part one is 0.25. Two tails is equally rare, so part two is 0.25. Nothing is rarer than two heads or two tails, so part three is 0.

$$ p = 0.25 + 0.25 + 0 = 0.5 $$

The p-value 0.5 is much larger than 0.25, and much larger than 0.05. Two heads in a row does not convince us the coin is special.

Example

Now flip five times and get four heads and one tails. There are 32 outcomes in total. Five of them give four heads and one tails, five give one heads and four tails, and one each gives five heads or five tails.

$$ p = \frac{5}{32} + \frac{5}{32} + \frac{2}{32} = \frac{12}{32} = 0.375 $$

Again 0.375 is above 0.05, so we fail to reject the null hypothesis.

Example

Now use the height curve. You measure a woman who is 142 cm tall. You ask whether she could come from this Brazilian distribution, whose mean is 155.7 cm.

The area at or below 142 cm is 0.025. Every height at least as far from the mean counts too, so the area at or above 169 cm adds another 0.025.

$$ p = 0.025 + 0.025 = 0.05 $$

That sits exactly on the threshold, so the data are inconclusive. At 141 cm each tail shrinks to 0.016, so \(p = 0.03\). Now you reject the null hypothesis, and some other distribution explains that height better.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out The p-value is not the probability of your observation. Two heads has probability 0.25 but a p-value of 0.5. The extra comes from parts two and three, which people forget most often.
Watch out A large p-value does not prove the null hypothesis is true. You only failed to reject it. With 142 cm the p-value lands exactly on 0.05, and the honest answer is that the data are inconclusive.
Watch out A value close to the mean can have a tiny probability and still give a p-value of 1. A height between 155.4 and 156 cm has probability 0.04, but 48% of the curve lies on each side and is more extreme, so \(0.04 + 0.48 + 0.48 = 1\). Nothing unusual happened.
Watch out One-sided p-values only look for change in the direction you chose, so they are blind to harm in the other direction. Josh calls them tricky and says to avoid them unless you really know what you are doing. The figure below shows why.

If they ask you in an interview

What exactly goes into a p-value?
Three probabilities added together. The probability of the observation itself, the probability of anything else equally rare, and the probability of anything rarer or more extreme. That is why the p-value is usually larger than the probability of the observation alone.
Why do we include outcomes we did not observe?
Because a result only feels special when few things are as surprising as it. If many other outcomes are equally rare, or even rarer, then yours is not remarkable. Adding those probabilities is how we measure that.
How does the calculation change for continuous data?
You cannot list every possible value, so you use a distribution and add areas under the curve instead of counting outcomes. More extreme now means further from the mean, on either side. The three parts are the same, just measured as area.
Why avoid one-sided p-values?
A one-sided p-value only counts extreme values in the direction you hoped for. If the effect goes the other way, it can return a huge p-value and report nothing unusual. In the drug example the recovery time got worse, and the one-sided p-value was 0.98 while the two-sided one was 0.03.
What does a p-value of 0.5 mean about your coin?
It means you failed to reject the null hypothesis, so the data are consistent with a normal coin. It does not prove the coin is fair. With only two flips there is simply not enough evidence either way.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • The null hypothesis is the boring claim, and a small p-value tells you to reject it.
  • A p-value adds three parts: the observation, anything equally rare, and anything rarer or more extreme.
  • Two heads in a row gives a p-value of 0.5, not 0.25, because of parts two and three.
  • Four heads and one tails out of five flips gives 5/32 + 5/32 + 2/32 = 0.375.
  • For continuous data you add areas under a distribution, and more extreme means further from the mean.
  • A height of 142 cm gives 0.025 + 0.025 = 0.05, which is inconclusive, while 141 cm gives 0.03 and is rejected.
  • A value near the mean can give a p-value of 1, so a small probability is not the same as a small p-value.
  • One-sided p-values ignore surprises in the other direction, so use two-sided p-values unless you are an expert.
