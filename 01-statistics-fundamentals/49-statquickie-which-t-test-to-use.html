StatQuickie: Which t test to use

▶ Watch on YouTube

Josh answers the two questions he hears most in his genetics lab: which t-test to run, and one-tailed or two-tailed.

In one breath

A t-test compares two sets of measurements. If both come from the same subject, such as blood pressure before and after a drug, use a paired t-test. If they come from separate groups, use an unpaired t-test. Then pick the version that does not assume equal variance, and use two tails. Both choices are conservative, so passing them means the result is solid.

The main idea

The first decision is simple. Ask whether each number in one set has a partner in the other set. Josh's example is a blood pressure drug. You measure each person, give the drug, then measure again. Every person now has a before value and an after value. That data is paired, so use a paired t-test.

Now change the study. You measure the heights of one group, A, and the heights of a different group, B. Nobody is in both groups, so no number has a partner. That data is unpaired, so use an unpaired t-test.

Unpaired t-tests come in two versions. One assumes the spread in group A equals the spread in group B. Spread here means variance: how far measurements scatter around their own average. The other version lets the groups differ. Josh recommends the second, because it is slightly more conservative. Data that passes it is rock solid.

Step by step

  1. Look at how the data was collected. If each subject gave a before and an after value, the data is paired.
  2. For paired data, run a paired t-test. It works on the difference inside each pair.
  3. For two separate groups with no shared members, run an unpaired t-test.
  4. For the unpaired case, take the version that does not assume equal variance.
  5. Finally choose the number of tails. Use two tails.

A two-tailed test asks whether group A is taller than group B, and also whether it is shorter. It carries no preconceived idea. A one-tailed test asks only one of those questions. It is less conservative, and you must claim the direction in advance. Josh always recommends two tails.

Formulas

Josh shows no maths here. The two formulas below sit behind the boxes in the diagram.

Paired t-test
$$ t = \frac{\bar{d}}{s_d / \sqrt{n}} $$

Symbols: \(d\) is one subject's difference, after minus before; \(\bar{d}\) is the average of those differences; \(s_d\) is their standard deviation, a measure of spread; \(n\) is the number of subjects. What it tells you: it weighs the average within-person change against how much that change varies.

Unpaired t-test that does not assume equal variance
$$ t = \frac{\bar{x}_A - \bar{x}_B}{\sqrt{\dfrac{s_A^2}{n_A} + \dfrac{s_B^2}{n_B}}} $$

Symbols: \(\bar{x}_A\) and \(\bar{x}_B\) are the group averages; \(s_A^2\) and \(s_B^2\) are the group variances; \(n_A\) and \(n_B\) are the group sizes. What it tells you: each group keeps its own variance, so equal spread is never assumed.

Worked example from the video

Example

Blood pressure drug. You measure everyone, give the drug, then measure again. Each person has a before and an after value, so the data is paired. Choose a paired t-test.

Heights. You measure one group and call it A, then measure a different group and call it B. Nobody appears in both, so the data is unpaired. Choose an unpaired t-test, take the version that does not assume equal variance, and run it two-tailed.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out Pairing comes from the study design, not from the numbers. Two groups of equal size are still unpaired if no person appears in both.
Watch out Assuming equal variance is a real assumption about your groups. Josh advises against it. The version that allows different variances is more conservative, so a result that survives it is stronger.
Watch out A one-tailed test is tempting because significance is easier to reach. That is the problem. You must declare the direction before you look at the data.

If they ask you in an interview

When would you use a paired t-test instead of an unpaired one?
I use a paired t-test when each subject gives two measurements, such as blood pressure before and after a drug. Every value has a natural partner, so the test works on the within-person differences. If the two sets come from separate groups, there are no pairs, so I use an unpaired test.
Should an unpaired t-test assume equal variance?
I default to the version that does not assume equal variance. Real groups rarely have identical spread, and that version is slightly more conservative. It is harder to reach significance, so a result that passes is solid.
One-tailed or two-tailed, and why?
Two-tailed, almost always. It checks both directions, so it carries no preconceived idea about which group is bigger. A one-tailed test is less conservative and makes me commit to a direction first. Journals expect two tails, and I would rather let the data speak for itself.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • T-tests split into two main families, paired and unpaired.
  • Use a paired t-test when the same subject gives you a before and an after measurement.
  • Use an unpaired t-test when two separate groups are compared, like heights in group A and group B.
  • Unpaired tests come in two versions; prefer the one that does not assume equal variance.
  • Prefer a two-tailed test, because it checks both directions and lets the data speak for itself.
  • Conservative choices mean a significant result is worth more.
