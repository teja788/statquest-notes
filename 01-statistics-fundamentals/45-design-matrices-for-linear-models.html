Design Matrices For Linear Models

▶ Watch on YouTube Also in: Machine Learning (video 13)

A design matrix is the small table of numbers that tells a linear model which parts of its equation to use for each measurement. This video shows that one simple table can run a t-test, a regression, or both at the same time.

In one breath

Part three of the general linear models series starts with the standard design matrix for a t-test. It has a column of ones plus a column that switches a mutant offset on or off. Josh then shows that the numbers do not have to be ones and zeros. A column can hold the actual x-axis values, which turns the same machinery into linear regression. Finally he combines both ideas in one model, compares it to simpler models with an F test, and uses the same trick to remove a batch effect.

The main idea

A linear model is just an equation with a few terms. Each term has a parameter, such as a mean, an offset, or a slope. The design matrix holds one row for every measurement you collected. Each column of that row is a number that multiplies one term of the equation.

The standard design matrix for a t-test uses only ones and zeros. The first column is all ones, so every mouse, control and mutant, turns on the term for the mean of the control values. The second column is zero for control mice and one for mutant mice. That second term is the difference between the mutant mean and the control mean, so it acts as an offset that only the mutants receive.

Multiplying a term by one keeps it, which turns it on. Multiplying a term by zero removes it, which turns it off. In part two the same t-test was written with two separate mean terms. Both versions give the same residuals and both have two parameters, so they give the same F value and the same p-value.

Step by step

  1. Write the equation you want to fit. Each term needs one parameter, such as a mean, an offset, or a slope.
  2. Build the design matrix with one row per measurement and one column per term. The first column is usually all ones.
  3. Use ones and zeros when a term should switch on or off. This is what a t-test or an ANOVA needs, because the data fall into categories.
  4. Use real numbers in a column when a term should be scaled instead. For linear regression the second column holds the x-axis value of each point.
  5. Plug each row into the equation, one row at a time. Each row gives one predicted point on the fitted line, so you can measure the residuals.
  6. Compare the fitted model with a simpler model using the F statistic, and turn that F value into a p-value.

The interesting step is number four. Once a column can hold any number, a single framework covers categories, continuous predictors, and both together.

Formulas

Standard t-test model
$$ \text{size} = \mu_{c}\cdot x_{1} \;+\; (\mu_{m}-\mu_{c})\cdot x_{2} $$

Symbols: \(\mu_{c}\) is the mean of the control measurements. \(\mu_{m}\) is the mean of the mutant measurements, so \(\mu_{m}-\mu_{c}\) is the mutant offset. \(x_{1}\) is the value in column one, always 1. \(x_{2}\) is the value in column two, 0 for a control mouse and 1 for a mutant mouse. What it tells you: every mouse gets the control mean, and mutants get an extra offset on top.

Regression model, one row at a time
$$ \text{size} = b\cdot 1 \;+\; m\cdot \text{weight} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{size} = 0.01\cdot 1 + 0.8\cdot \text{weight} $$

Symbols: \(b\) is the y-intercept, which the video fits at 0.01. \(m\) is the slope, fitted at 0.8. The column of ones turns the intercept on, and the weight column scales the slope. What it tells you: each row of the design matrix returns one point on the least squares line, and those points give you the residuals.

The combined model
$$ \text{size} = b \;+\; d\cdot x_{\text{mutant}} \;+\; m\cdot \text{weight} $$

Symbols: \(b\) is the y-intercept for the control mice. \(d\) is the mutant offset, the vertical gap between the two lines. \(x_{\text{mutant}}\) is 0 for control mice and 1 for mutant mice. \(m\) is the slope, shared by both groups. What it tells you: three parameters describe two parallel lines, so the model uses weight and mouse type at once.

F statistic for comparing two models
$$ F=\frac{\bigl(SS_{\text{simple}}-SS_{\text{fancy}}\bigr)\big/\bigl(p_{\text{fancy}}-p_{\text{simple}}\bigr)}{SS_{\text{fancy}}\big/\bigl(n-p_{\text{fancy}}\bigr)} $$

Symbols: \(SS_{\text{simple}}\) and \(SS_{\text{fancy}}\) are the sums of squared residuals for the simple and the fancy model. \(p_{\text{simple}}\) and \(p_{\text{fancy}}\) are the numbers of parameters in each model. \(n\) is the number of measurements. What it tells you: how much the extra parameters reduce the leftover error, per extra parameter. A large F gives a small p-value.

Worked example from the video

Example

Josh measures the weight and the size of eight mice. Four are normal controls and four are mutants that grow tall and thin. By eye the mutants are larger even at the same weight. A plain regression would fit one line and ignore the mouse type. A plain t-test would compare the two mean sizes and ignore weight, and here that gives a p-value above 0.05.

So he builds the fancy model with three columns: ones, the mutant switch, and the weight values. Rows with the switch off land on the control line. Rows with the switch on land on the mutant line. The residuals turn out to be very small.

He then compares this fancy model, with \(p_{\text{fancy}}=3\), against three different simple models.

  • Against the overall mean alone, \(p_{\text{simple}}=1\), the F value is 21.88 and the p-value is 0.003.
  • Against regression on weight alone, \(p_{\text{simple}}=2\), the p-value is 0.0023.
  • Against a plain t-test on mouse type alone, \(p_{\text{simple}}=2\), the p-value is 0.0025.

All three p-values are small. Using weight and mouse type together predicts size better than the mean alone, better than weight alone, and better than type alone.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out Two design matrices can look different and still be the same model. The two t-test versions have the same residuals and the same parameter count. So they give the same F and the same p-value.
Watch out A design matrix is not restricted to ones and zeros. A column of ones and zeros switches a term on or off, while a column of measured values scales a term. Mixing both kinds of column in one matrix is what lets you test categories and a continuous predictor together.
Watch out The p-value depends on which simple model you choose. Comparing against the overall mean, against weight alone, and against type alone answers three different questions. Pick the simple model that matches the question you actually care about.
Watch out Count the parameters carefully, because they sit in the F formula. The combined model has three, the simple regression has two, and the mean-only model has one.

If they ask you in an interview

What is a design matrix?
It is a table with one row for each measurement and one column for each term in the model equation. The number in a cell multiplies that term for that measurement. A one keeps the term, a zero removes it, and any other number scales it. Plugging a row into the equation gives the model's prediction for that measurement.
Why does a t-test design matrix usually have a column of ones and an offset column?
The column of ones gives every measurement a common baseline, which here is the control mean. The second column is one only for the treated group, so it adds the difference between the two group means. Writing it this way keeps the same form as regression. The version with two separate mean terms returns the same p-value.
How would you test whether two groups have different lines rather than different means?
I would fit one model with three terms: an intercept, a group offset, and a shared slope on the continuous predictor. The design matrix has a column of ones, a zero-one group column, and a column of predictor values. Then I compare that model to a simpler one with an F test.
How do you handle a batch effect with a design matrix?
In the video, Lab B repeats Lab A's experiment but its measurements are lower overall. You add a term for the Lab A control mean, a term for the Lab B offset, and a term for the mutant to control difference. The Lab B offset absorbs the batch shift. You then test the last term by comparing against a model that drops that column.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • A design matrix holds one row per measurement and one column per term in the model equation.
  • The standard t-test matrix uses a column of ones plus a zero-one column that switches a group offset on.
  • Different but equivalent design matrices give the same residuals, the same F, and the same p-value.
  • Columns do not have to be ones and zeros; a column of x-axis values turns the same machinery into linear regression.
  • Adding a ones column, a group switch column, and a predictor column fits two parallel lines with three parameters.
  • In the mouse example that combined model beat the mean-only model with F equal to 21.88 and a p-value of 0.003.
  • It also beat weight alone, p equal to 0.0023, and type alone, p equal to 0.0025.
  • The same idea removes a batch effect by giving the second lab its own offset column.
