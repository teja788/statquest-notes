The Main Ideas behind Probability Distributions

▶ Watch on YouTube Also in: —

This video explains what a statistical distribution is. It shows how likely each measurement is, in two forms: a histogram and a smooth curve.

In one breath

Josh measures the height of many people and drops each value into a bin. The stack of bins is a histogram, and it shows which heights are common and which are rare. Narrower bins and more people give a sharper picture. You can then draw a smooth curve with the same shape. Both are distributions: both show how probabilities are spread out.

The main idea

Josh measures the height of many people, one after the other. Each measurement goes into the bin that covers its value. A bin is a small range of heights, for example 5 feet up to 5.5 feet.

The first person is 5.2 feet tall, so that goes in the bin from 5 to 5.5. The next people are 5.8, 5.6, 5.9, 5.1 and 6.3 feet tall. Each is stacked on whatever is already in its bin. Do this for many people and the bins form a histogram.

The shape is the useful part. Most measurements sit between 5 and 6 feet. Shorter and taller people are both rare. So a random measurement will probably land in the middle. That is what a distribution tells you.

Step by step

  1. Measure many people and choose a bin width. Josh starts half a foot wide.
  2. Put each measurement in its bin and stack the blocks. That is the histogram.
  3. Read the shape. The tall part shows likely values, the low parts unlikely ones.
  4. Halve the bin width and measure more people. Josh can now say half the people are between 5.25 and 5.75 feet.
  5. Draw a smooth curve along the top. It says the same things.

Josh gives three reasons why the curve is often better. First, a bin can be empty just because nobody landed there. The histogram reports zero, which is wrong. The curve stays above zero.

Second, the curve is not tied to bin width. Say you want the probability of a height between 5.021 and 5.317 feet. No bin has those edges, but the curve answers without rounding. Third, a curve built from the mean and standard deviation of a small sample is usually good enough.

Formulas

The video shows pictures, not algebra. These two formulas state what Josh describes.

Probability from a histogram
$$ \hat{P}(a \le X < b) = \frac{n_{[a,b)}}{N} $$

Symbols: \(X\) is the measurement, here a height. \(a\) and \(b\) are the edges of one bin. \(n_{[a,b)}\) is how many measurements landed in it. \(N\) is the total count. The hat means estimated from data. What it tells you: a histogram becomes a probability distribution once you divide each count by the total.

Probability from a curve
$$ P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx $$

Symbols: \(f(x)\) is the height of the curve at value \(x\), called the probability density. \(a\) and \(b\) are any two heights, such as 5.021 and 5.317. The integral sign means area under the curve. What it tells you: probability is area, so any interval works.

Worked example from the video

Example

Josh measures six people: 5.2, 5.8, 5.6, 5.9, 5.1 and 6.3 feet tall. The bins are half a foot wide.

The bin 5.0 to 5.5 holds 5.2 and 5.1, so 2 people. The bin 5.5 to 6.0 holds 5.8, 5.6 and 5.9, so 3 people. The bin 6.0 to 6.5 holds 6.3 alone.

Divide each count by \(N = 6\):

$$ \hat{P}(5.0 \le X < 5.5) = \tfrac{2}{6} = 0.33, \quad \hat{P}(5.5 \le X < 6.0) = \tfrac{3}{6} = 0.50, \quad \hat{P}(6.0 \le X < 6.5) = \tfrac{1}{6} = 0.17 $$

Five of the six people are between 5 and 6 feet tall, about 83 percent.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out Narrow bins show more detail only if you have enough measurements to fill them.
Watch out A bar height is a count, not a probability. Divide by the total first. On a curve, the height at one point is not a probability either. Only area is.
Watch out An empty bin does not mean the value is impossible. It means nobody in this sample landed there.

If they ask you in an interview

What is a probability distribution, in plain words?
It describes how likely each measurement is. Stack many heights into bins and that histogram already is a distribution. A smooth curve over the same data is the continuous version.
When would you use a histogram, and when a curve?
A histogram when I want the raw shape with no assumptions. A curve when I need arbitrary ranges, when small samples leave gaps, or when measurements are expensive.
Why can a curve give a probability for an empty bin?
Because the curve models the whole population, not just my sample. An empty bin forces the histogram estimate to zero. The curve smooths across that gap.
What is the risk of replacing a histogram with a curve?
The curve carries assumptions the histogram does not. Fit a bell curve to skewed or two-peaked data and the probabilities will be wrong, especially in the tails.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • A distribution shows how the probabilities of measurements are spread out.
  • Put each measurement in a bin and stack the bins to build a histogram.
  • The tall part marks likely values, the low parts unlikely ones.
  • Smaller bins plus more measurements give a more accurate and precise picture.
  • A smooth curve approximates the histogram and tells the same story.
  • The curve fills empty bins, works for any interval, and needs only two numbers.
