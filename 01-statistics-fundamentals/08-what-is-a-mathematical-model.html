What is a (mathematical) model?

▶ Watch on YouTube

A model describes the relationship between two or more things. This video shows why a fitted line, and its equation, is exactly that.

In one breath

Josh uses mouse weight and mouse size. He plots the data, fits a line, and calls that line the model. The equation of the line is the mathematical model, and it predicts the size of a mouse he never measured. The model is only an approximation, so gaps remain to the real points. Most of statistics exists to say how good it is.

The main idea

The word "model" means many things. As a child it was a toy Josh glued together. In statistics it means a relationship between measured things.

His example is mouse weight and mouse size. He models size with weight, describing how the two move together. The relationship is obvious: heavier mice are bigger.

A model can also be an equation. Josh fits a straight line through the mice, then writes its equation. That equation is the mathematical model. Picture and equation are the same thing written two ways.

The equation is useful because it talks about mice you never measured. Put in a weight of four and it returns 3.3.

The model is an approximation, not a copy. Josh draws dotted lines to each real point. Those gaps are what it misses.

Step by step

  1. Choose the relationship you care about. Josh models mouse size using mouse weight.
  2. Plot the measurements. Each dot is one mouse, and heavier mice sit higher.
  3. Fit a line through the dots, then write it as an equation. That equation is the model.
  4. Use the equation to predict. Weight four gives size 3.3.
  5. Look at the gaps between the model and each real point.
  6. Judge it with statistics. That is what linear regression, t-tests, ANOVA and F-tests are for.
  7. Change the model's shape when the data asks for it.

Josh ends with two other shapes. Drug dosage against hair growth rises, then flattens: past a point, more drug grows no hair. He also uses genes X and Z together to model mouse size.

Formulas

The video shows an equation but never reads its numbers. These are the general forms.

The straight-line model, and Josh's prediction
$$ \widehat{\text{size}} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \times \text{weight}, \qquad \text{weight} = 4 \;\longrightarrow\; \widehat{\text{size}} = 3.3 $$

Symbols: the hat means predicted, not measured. \(\beta_0\) is the intercept, the predicted size at zero weight. \(\beta_1\) is the slope, the size change per extra unit of weight. What it tells you: this equation is the model, and it turns any weight into a predicted size.

The gap between model and data
$$ e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i $$

Symbols: \(y_i\) is the size measured for mouse \(i\) and \(\hat{y}_i\) the size predicted. \(e_i\) is the difference, the length of one dotted line, known as the residual. What it tells you: each residual says how much the model missed.

A model with two inputs
$$ \widehat{\text{size}} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \times \text{gene X} + \beta_2 \times \text{gene Z} $$

Symbols: \(\beta_1\) and \(\beta_2\) are the slopes of Josh's two gene measurements. What it tells you: a model is not limited to one input. Adding inputs is what makes it complex.

Worked example from the video

Example

Josh has a graph of mice, weight across and size up. He fits one straight line, and its equation is the model.

Someone asks how big a mouse would be at four units of weight. Josh puts four in and gets 3.3.

$$ \widehat{\text{size}} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \times 4 = 3.3 $$

That is a prediction, not a fact; no such mouse was measured. If the dots sit close to the line, the model approximates the data well.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out A predicted value is not a measurement. The 3.3 comes from the line, not from a mouse.
Watch out A straight line is a choice, not a rule. Hair growth rises then flattens, so a line would keep promising more hair from more drug.
Watch out Building a model and judging it are different jobs. Josh says a lot of statistics exists just to check the approximation.

If they ask you in an interview

What is a statistical model?
A description of the relationship between variables, usually an equation. You model mouse size using mouse weight, and the fitted line is the model. It summarises the pattern and predicts unmeasured cases.
Why is a model only an approximation?
Because the real points almost never sit exactly on it. The gaps between observations and predictions are called residuals. Small, patternless residuals mean a good approximation.
How do you decide whether a model is any good?
Look at how far the data sit from the model, then judge it formally. That is the point of linear regression, general linear models, t-tests, ANOVA and F-tests.
When would a straight line be the wrong model?
Whenever the relationship changes shape across the input range. Drug dosage against hair growth rises then levels off, so a line would keep predicting growth forever.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • In statistics, a model is a relationship between measured things.
  • Josh models mouse size using mouse weight: heavier mice are bigger.
  • The fitted line and its equation are the same model.
  • It answers questions about unmeasured cases. At weight 4 it predicts size 3.3.
  • The model is an approximation, and the dotted gaps show what it misses.
  • Statistics judges whether those gaps are small enough.
  • Models are not always straight lines, and not limited to one input.
