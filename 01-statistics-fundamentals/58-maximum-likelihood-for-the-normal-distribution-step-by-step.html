Maximum Likelihood For the Normal Distribution, step-by-step

▶ Watch on YouTube

This video proves where two familiar formulas come from. It finds the normal curve that makes the observed data most likely.

In one breath

A normal curve has two knobs: μ moves its centre and σ sets its width. The likelihood of a curve, given one measurement, is the height of the curve above that measurement. With several measurements the likelihoods multiply, because the measurements are independent. Josh takes the log of that product, which turns it into a sum and makes the derivatives easy. The μ derivative gives the mean of the data, and the σ derivative gives the standard deviation of the data.

The main idea

The equation for the normal curve has two parameters. The first is μ, the Greek letter mu, which sets the location of the mean. A smaller μ moves the curve left, and a larger μ moves it right. The second is σ, the Greek letter sigma, the standard deviation, which sets the width. A larger σ makes the curve shorter and wider.

We want the μ and σ that fit the data best. Josh starts with the smallest data set possible: one mouse that weighs 32 grams. Lay a curve with μ = 28 and σ = 2 over that point and plug x = 32 into the equation. The likelihood is 0.03, which is exactly the height of the curve above 32 grams. Slide the centre to μ = 30 and the likelihood rises to 0.12, so that curve fits better.

So fix σ at 2 and treat it as a given, like the data. Try many values of μ and plot each likelihood against the μ that produced it. The result is a curve with one clear peak, at the place where its slope is zero. Here that peak sits at μ = 32. The same trick then finds σ: fix μ, try many widths, and look for the peak again.

Step by step

  1. Move to two mice, \(x_1 = 32\) and \(x_2 = 34\) grams. They are independent, because weighing the first did not change the second.
  2. So the likelihood given both mice is the likelihood given \(x_1\) times the likelihood given \(x_2\). With n mice you multiply all n individual likelihoods.
  3. Take the log of that product. The log does not move the peak, but it turns multiplication into addition, which is far easier to differentiate.
  4. Simplify each term. The log splits the fraction from the exponential, ln(e) = 1 removes the exponential, and the log of 2πσ² splits into the log of 2π plus twice the log of σ.
  5. Combine the n simplified terms into one tidy log-likelihood. That is the function we differentiate.
  6. Differentiate with respect to μ, treating σ as a constant. The first two terms contain no μ, so they vanish, and the chain rule handles each squared term.
  7. Differentiate with respect to σ, treating μ as a constant. Rewrite 1/σ² as σ to the power −2 first, because that makes the derivative straightforward.
  8. Set each derivative to zero and solve. The μ equation gives the mean of the data, and the σ equation gives the standard deviation of the data.

Formulas

Likelihood for one measurement
$$ L(\mu,\sigma \mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\; e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}} $$

Symbols: \(x\) is the measurement; \(\mu\) is the centre of the curve; \(\sigma\) is the standard deviation, so \(\sigma^{2}\) is the variance; \(\pi\) is 3.14159 and \(e\) is 2.71828. What it tells you: the height of this curve above the measurement, which is the likelihood.

Likelihood for n independent measurements
$$ L(\mu,\sigma \mid x_1,\dots,x_n) \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\; e^{-\frac{(x_i-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}} $$

Symbols: \(x_1\) to \(x_n\) are the n measurements; the \(\prod\) sign means multiply all n terms. What it tells you: the likelihood of the whole data set is the product of the individual likelihoods.

Taking the log, one term at a time
$$ \ln\!\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\,e^{-\frac{(x_1-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\right] $$

Start with the log of one likelihood term. The log of a product is the sum of the logs, so it splits in two.

$$ =\; \ln\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}} \;+\; \ln e^{-\frac{(x_1-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}} $$

One over a square root is the power \(-\tfrac{1}{2}\), and \(\ln(e)=1\) makes the exponential disappear.

$$ =\; -\tfrac{1}{2}\ln\!\left(2\pi\sigma^{2}\right) \;-\; \frac{(x_1-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}} $$

The log splits \(2\pi\sigma^{2}\) into \(2\pi\) plus \(\sigma^{2}\), the exponent 2 comes out in front of \(\ln\sigma\), and then the two halves cancel.

$$ =\; -\tfrac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi) \;-\; \ln(\sigma) \;-\; \frac{(x_1-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}} $$

Symbols: \(\ln\) is the natural log. What it tells you: every piece now adds instead of multiplies, so the derivative becomes easy.

The simplified log-likelihood
$$ \ln L \;=\; -\frac{n}{2}\ln(2\pi) \;-\; n\ln(\sigma) \;-\; \frac{(x_1-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}} \;-\;\cdots\;-\; \frac{(x_n-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}} $$

Symbols: \(n\) is how many measurements we have; the first two terms collect the n copies of the constant pieces. What it tells you: this is the function we differentiate, and it peaks at the same μ and σ as the likelihood.

Derivative with respect to μ
$$ \frac{\partial \ln L}{\partial \mu} \;=\; \frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\Big[(x_1+\cdots+x_n) \;-\; n\mu\Big] $$

Symbols: \(\partial\) marks a derivative taken while the other parameter is held fixed. What it tells you: the slope as we slide the curve left and right, so the best μ is where it equals zero.

Derivative with respect to σ
$$ \frac{\partial \ln L}{\partial \sigma} \;=\; -\frac{n}{\sigma} \;+\; \frac{1}{\sigma^{3}}\Big[(x_1-\mu)^{2}+\cdots+(x_n-\mu)^{2}\Big] $$

Symbols: the same as above, with μ now a fixed number. What it tells you: the slope as we make the curve wider or narrower, so the best σ is where it equals zero.

Setting each slope to zero and solving
$$ \frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\Big[(x_1+\cdots+x_n) - n\mu\Big] = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; x_1+\cdots+x_n = n\mu $$

Multiply both sides by \(\sigma^{2}\), which makes it disappear. Then add \(n\mu\) to both sides. Divide by \(n\) and μ is alone.

$$ -\frac{n}{\sigma} + \frac{1}{\sigma^{3}}\Big[(x_1-\mu)^{2}+\cdots+(x_n-\mu)^{2}\Big] = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; (x_1-\mu)^{2}+\cdots+(x_n-\mu)^{2} = n\sigma^{2} $$

Multiply both sides by \(\sigma\), then add \(n\) to both sides. Multiply by \(\sigma^{2}\), divide by \(n\), and take the square root.

The two answers
$$ \mu \;=\; \frac{x_1+\cdots+x_n}{n} \qquad\qquad \sigma \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{(x_1-\mu)^{2}+\cdots+(x_n-\mu)^{2}}{n}} $$

Symbols: the same n measurements, and the μ on the right is the value we just solved for. What it tells you: the best centre is the mean of the data, and the best width is the standard deviation of the data.

Worked example from the video

Example

One mouse weighs 32 grams. Put a curve with μ = 28 and σ = 2 over it. The front part is \(1/\sqrt{2\pi\cdot 4} = 0.199\) and the exponential is \(e^{-(32-28)^{2}/8} = e^{-2} = 0.135\), so the likelihood is 0.03. Shift the centre to μ = 30 and the exponential becomes \(e^{-0.5} = 0.607\), so the likelihood rises to 0.12.

Add a second mouse at 34 grams, still with μ = 28 and σ = 2. Its own likelihood is \(0.199 \times e^{-36/8} = 0.0022\), so the likelihood given both mice is about 0.00006. Josh notes that this is a really small number.

Now use the solved formulas on those two mice. The mean is \((32+34)/2 = 33\), so the best curve is centred at 33. The standard deviation is \(\sqrt{((32-33)^{2}+(34-33)^{2})/2} = 1\), so that is its width.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out Likelihood is not probability. Here it is the height of a fixed curve above a fixed data point. Probability comes from areas under the curve, while likelihood comes from heights.
Watch out Taking the log is a convenience, not a change of problem. The log is an increasing function, so the likelihood and the log-likelihood peak at exactly the same μ and σ.
Watch out Solve one parameter at a time, holding the other one constant. The σ formula also uses μ, so you must find μ first.
Watch out This derivation divides the squared differences by n, not by n − 1. That is the maximum likelihood answer. Also, one measurement can give you μ but never σ, as Josh points out.

If they ask you in an interview

What are the maximum likelihood estimates for a normal distribution?
The estimate for the centre is the mean of the data, and the estimate for the width is the standard deviation. They look obvious, but the calculus proves the intuition. You write the likelihood, take its log, differentiate with respect to each parameter, and set each derivative to zero.
Why maximise the log-likelihood instead of the likelihood?
The likelihood is a product of n terms, and differentiating a long product is painful. The log turns it into a sum, so each term differentiates on its own. The log is increasing, so the peak does not move and the answer is the same. It also avoids numerical underflow, because a product of many small likelihoods shrinks very fast.
Why can you multiply the individual likelihoods together?
Because the measurements are independent, so weighing one mouse said nothing about the next. Under independence the likelihood of the whole data set is the product of the individual likelihoods. If the observations were correlated, that product would be the wrong model.
Why does the maximum likelihood variance divide by n rather than n − 1?
Dividing by n is what falls out of setting the σ derivative to zero. It is slightly biased downward, because it uses the estimated mean rather than the true mean. Dividing by n − 1 corrects that bias, but unbiasedness and maximum likelihood are different criteria.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • A normal curve has two parameters: μ sets where it sits, and σ sets how wide it is.
  • The likelihood of a curve, given one measurement, is the height of that curve above the measurement.
  • Because the measurements are independent, the likelihood given n of them is the product of the n individual likelihoods.
  • Taking the log turns that product into a sum without moving the peak, which makes the derivatives easy.
  • Differentiate with respect to μ while holding σ fixed, set the result to zero, and μ becomes the mean of the data.
  • Differentiate with respect to σ while holding μ fixed, set that to zero, and σ becomes the standard deviation of the data.
  • So the familiar formulas are not just intuition; they are the values that make the observed data most likely.
