Alternative Hypotheses: Main Ideas

▶ Watch on YouTube Also in: —

This video explains the alternative hypothesis: the rival description of the data that a statistical test compares against the null hypothesis.

In one breath

A statistical test needs three things: data, a null hypothesis, and an alternative hypothesis. The null says the groups are the same, so one mean describes all the data. The alternative says the groups differ, so separate means describe the data better. The test looks at how far the observations sit from each set of means, and rejects the null when the separate means fit much better. With three or more groups there are several possible alternatives, and different alternatives can lead to different decisions.

The main idea

Josh reviews the previous video. Two drugs, C and D, and we measure recovery from a virus. We test the null hypothesis: there is no difference. A gap too big to blame on diet or exercise means we reject the null. If small random things could flip the result, we fail to reject.

Now the new part. To make that decision we run the data through a statistical test, a procedure that returns one verdict: reject, or fail to reject. It needs three ingredients: the data, the null or primary hypothesis, and an alternative hypothesis, which is the competing story about the data.

With two drugs the alternative is obvious. The null says no difference, so the alternative is simply the opposite. Josh warns that the next part is hand-wavy. The goal is to feel why the alternative matters, not to learn how a test works in detail.

Here is the picture. Compute one mean from all the data. Measure the distance from every observation to it. Those distances stand for the null. Then compute a separate mean per drug and measure again. If those distances are much shorter, two means describe the data better, so we reject the null.

Now suppose the distances around the single mean were not much longer. Then the small gap between the group means probably reflects random things. Josh's example: perhaps one person got less exercise. Had he exercised more, the difference would disappear. Then we fail to reject the null.

Josh adds a note for readers who know machine learning. Failing to reject is the same as noticing that two averages have overfit the data.

Step by step

  1. Collect the data: how quickly each person recovered while taking drug C or drug D.
  2. State the null hypothesis. There is no difference, so a single mean describes everybody.
  3. State the alternative hypothesis. With two groups it is the opposite: each drug gets its own mean.
  4. Measure the distances from every observation to the single mean. These stand for the null.
  5. Measure the distances to each observation's own group mean. These stand for the alternative.
  6. Compare the two sets. If the group means shrink the distances a lot, reject the null. If the shrinkage could be random noise, fail to reject.
  7. Report the verdict about the null. Never report that you accepted the alternative.

When there are three or more groups

With two groups the alternative is obvious, so it is easy to forget. Add a third drug and it becomes interesting. The null now says there is no difference between C, D and E, again pictured as distances to a single mean. But the alternative is no longer unique.

One alternative is that all three drugs differ, so each gets its own mean. Another is that C and D are the same while E does its own thing, so C and D share one mean and E keeps another. Both are legitimate.

This is the heart of the video. Depending on which alternative goes into the test, we can reach a different decision about the null. So state clearly which alternative you use before you run the test.

Whichever alternative we choose, the verdict is still about the null. We may say we rejected the null in favour of that alternative. We never accept the alternative, because too many others exist to test.

Formulas

The video stays hand-wavy and gives no formula. It talks about "distances" from the observations to the means. Real tests add those up as squared distances, because squaring removes the sign.

Distances for the null and for the alternative
$$ SS_{\text{null}} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left( x_i - \bar{x} \right)^2, \qquad SS_{\text{alt}} = \sum_{g=1}^{G} \; \sum_{i \in g} \left( x_i - \bar{x}_g \right)^2 $$

Symbols: \(x_i\) is person \(i\)'s recovery time and \(n\) is the total number of people. \(\bar{x}\) is the single pooled mean. \(g\) labels one group of the alternative, \(G\) is how many groups it uses, and \(\bar{x}_g\) is that group's mean. What it tells you: the first number says how badly one mean describes everybody. The second says how well the alternative's grouping fits. A different alternative changes who shares a mean.

Rule of thumb for the comparison
$$ \text{drop} = SS_{\text{null}} - SS_{\text{alt}} $$

Symbols: the drop is how much distance the extra means removed. What it tells you: a large drop, relative to the number of extra means used, says the separate means are worth it, so reject the null. A small drop says they chased random noise. Turning that judgement into a number is the job of the p-value.

Worked example from the video

Example

The video draws the three drugs without printing values. These numbers match the figure above. Recovery in days: drug C gives 3 and 4, drug D gives 4 and 5, drug E gives 9 and 10.

The null says everyone shares one mean, which is \((3+4+4+5+9+10)/6 = 5.83\) days. The squared distances to it add up to:

$$ SS_{\text{null}} = 2.83^2 + 1.83^2 + 1.83^2 + 0.83^2 + 3.17^2 + 4.17^2 = 42.8 $$

The first alternative says all three drugs differ. The means become 3.5 for C, 4.5 for D and 9.5 for E, and every observation sits half a day from its own mean:

$$ SS_{\text{alt-1}} = 6 \times 0.5^2 = 1.5 $$

The second alternative says C and D are the same and E does its own thing. Then C and D share the mean \((3+4+4+5)/4 = 4\) days, while E keeps 9.5:

$$ SS_{\text{alt-2}} = \left(1^2 + 0^2 + 0^2 + 1^2\right) + 2 \times 0.5^2 = 2.5 $$

Both alternatives cut the distances from 42.8 to under 3, so both say reject the null. Josh's warning is that the two will not always agree. When they disagree, the alternative you chose decides the verdict.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out

Do not say "we accept the alternative hypothesis". The test only rejects or fails to reject the null. Some other alternative you never tested might describe the data better.

Watch out

Choose the alternative before you run the test. With three or more groups, different alternatives can give different decisions about the same null and the same data.

Watch out

"Fail to reject" is not "there is no difference". It means the extra means did not shrink the distances enough to rule out random things.

Watch out

Extra means always fit at least as well, so shorter distances alone prove nothing. That is Josh's overfitting remark.

If they ask you in an interview

What is the alternative hypothesis, in plain words?
It is the rival description of the data that the test weighs against the null. The null says one mean describes everybody; the alternative says the groups need their own means. A test cannot work with the null alone.
Why do we never accept the alternative hypothesis?
Rejecting the null only says one mean fits poorly. It does not say our particular alternative is right, and there are far too many others we never tested. The honest wording is that we rejected the null in favour of a stated alternative.
With three groups, what alternatives could you have, and why does it matter?
With drugs C, D and E, one alternative is that all three differ, so each gets its own mean. Another is that C and D are the same while E differs. Each groups the data differently, and the test can reach a different verdict, so state it up front.
How does a test actually use the alternative hypothesis?
It measures how far each observation sits from a single pooled mean, then from the means the alternative implies. If the alternative's means shrink those distances a lot, we reject the null.
How is "fail to reject" related to overfitting?
Failing to reject means the extra means bought very little. They moved apart only because of random noise, such as one person exercising less. In machine learning language, that is overfitting.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • A statistical test needs three things: the data, a null or primary hypothesis, and an alternative hypothesis.
  • The null hypothesis says the groups are the same, which we picture as one mean for all the data.
  • The alternative hypothesis says the groups differ, which we picture as separate means.
  • The test compares the distances from the observations to each set of means.
  • Much shorter distances around the separate means mean we reject the null hypothesis.
  • Similar distances mean we fail to reject it, because random things could explain the small gap.
  • With three or more groups several alternatives exist, and each can change the decision.
  • Whatever alternative we use, we only reject or fail to reject the null.
