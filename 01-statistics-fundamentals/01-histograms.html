Histograms

▶ Watch on YouTube Also in: —

A histogram turns overlapping measurements into a shape you can read. Josh shows why bins fix the overlap, and why bin width matters.

In one breath

Plot many measurements as dots on a line and they overlap, so some are hidden. Stacking only the exact matches does not help, because exact ties are rare. So you split the range of values into bins, and stack the measurements in each bin. Tall stacks mark common values, and the shape hints where future measurements will land. The setting you must tune is the bin width.

The main idea

Imagine you measure how tall someone is. You draw a dot on a line at that height. Then you measure a whole crowd. Soon so many dots overlap that some sit hidden behind others.

The obvious fix is to stack dots with the exact same value. Josh shows why this fails. Measurements are continuous, so exactly equal values are rare. Most hidden dots stay hidden.

The fix that works is to stop asking for exact matches. Divide the range of values into bins. A bin is a small interval, such as 170 to 180 centimetres. Now stack every measurement in the same bin. That picture is a histogram.

The taller a stack, the more measurements fell in that bin. Josh uses the shape to predict. The next measurement will probably land in the tall middle range. The shape also justifies a distribution, a smooth curve that stands in for your data. A symmetric hump suggests a normal distribution. A fast drop suggests an exponential one.

Step by step

  1. Plot each measurement as a dot on a number line. With enough data they overlap.
  2. Cut the whole range, smallest to largest, into bins of equal width.
  3. Put each measurement in its bin and stack them. That is your histogram.
  4. Read the heights. Use the shape to judge future measurements and pick a distribution.
  5. Redraw with several bin widths. Keep the one that shows the shape most clearly.

Formulas

The video gives no formula. Josh says to try a few bin widths and look. These two rules make the first try less blind.

Bin count, then bin width
$$ k = \left\lceil \sqrt{n} \right\rceil, \qquad w = \frac{x_{\max} - x_{\min}}{k} $$

Symbols: \(n\) is how many measurements you have. \(k\) is the number of equal bins, and the brackets mean round up. \(x_{\max}\) and \(x_{\min}\) are the largest and smallest values. \(w\) is one bin's width. What it tells you: the square root of the sample size is a fair first guess for the bin count, and the second part turns it into a width.

Probability estimate from a bar
$$ \hat{P}(x \in \text{bin } j) = \frac{c_j}{n} $$

Symbols: the hat means estimated from data. \(c_j\) is the count in bin \(j\), that bar's height. \(n\) is the total count. What it tells you: the share of past measurements in a bin estimates the chance the next one lands there.

Worked example from the video

Example

The video shows no numbers, so here are counts matching the figures above. Suppose you measure \(n = 16\) people, from 152 cm to 197 cm.

The square root rule gives \(k = 4\) bins. The range is 45 cm, so \(w = 11.25\) cm. That is awkward, so round to 10 cm bins covering 150 cm to 200 cm.

Sorting the heights gives counts of 1, 3, 6, 4 and 2, which add to 16. The tallest bin is 170 to 180 cm, so \(\hat{P} = 6/16 = 0.375\).

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out Do not accept your tool's default bin width. Josh is explicit about this. Draw the histogram at several widths first.
Watch out Narrow bins are not more accurate. When every measurement gets its own bin, you are back to raw dots.
Watch out Wide bins hide the shape. In the video, two wide bins split the data 50/50, which only says how many sit above the average.

If they ask you in an interview

What problem does a histogram actually solve?
Plotted as points on a line, many measurements overlap and some become invisible. A histogram cuts the range into bins and stacks everything in the same bin. The bar heights then show how common each range of values is.
Why not stack measurements with the same value?
With continuous measurements, exactly equal values are rare. Binning groups values that are near each other, not identical, and that is what makes the stacks informative.
How do you choose the bin width?
I start from a rule of thumb, usually the square root of the sample size, then get the width from the range. Then I redraw at two or three other widths and compare. I never trust the library default.
How does a histogram help you pick a distribution?
The histogram is the observed shape of the data, so it is evidence for the distribution you propose. A symmetric hump supports a normal distribution. A fast decay supports an exponential one.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • Many measurements plotted as dots on a line overlap, and some hide completely.
  • Stacking only exact duplicates fails, because exact ties are rare.
  • A histogram cuts the range into bins and stacks the measurements in each bin.
  • The taller a bin's stack, the more measurements landed in that range.
  • The shape predicts future measurements and justifies a normal or exponential distribution.
  • Bins that are too narrow show nothing new; bins that are too wide split the data 50/50.
  • Try several widths, and never trust the default.
