Bar Charts Are Better than Pie Charts

▶ Watch on YouTube

Josh takes the same numbers and draws them two ways. He shows why a bar chart answers questions that a pie chart hides.

In one breath

A pie chart cuts a circle into slices, one slice per category. A bar chart draws one bar per category on a shared baseline. Our eyes read bar heights well and slice angles badly. Josh shows one person's day, then two people's days, and the pie chart fails both times. His advice is simple: when you plan a pie chart, draw a bar chart instead.

The main idea

Both charts hold the same numbers. The difference is the work your eye has to do. In a pie chart you compare wedges that point in different directions. Two slices that differ by a real amount can still look the same size. In a bar chart every bar starts on one baseline, so you compare heights on a single scale and a small gap stays visible.

Josh's first example is Herman's day. Herman sleeps, works, eats and rests. In the pie chart, sleeping and working look about equal. In the bar chart you see at once that Herman works one hour more than he sleeps. Resting beats eating by one hour too, and that is just as obvious.

Step by step

  1. List the categories you want to compare. Josh uses four parts of Herman's day: sleep, work, eat and rest.
  2. Put the categories on one axis and the amount on the other. Every bar then shares the same baseline and the same scale.
  3. Read the differences straight off the chart. A one-hour gap is a clear step in height, not a small change in angle.
  4. For a second person, add a second bar beside each first bar. Two heights side by side are far easier to compare than two circles.

Formulas

This video has no formula. The useful rule of thumb is about how we read pictures: we judge position on a common scale accurately, and we judge angle and area poorly. So put whatever you want compared on a shared baseline. A pie chart also forces the parts to add up to one whole, while a bar chart has no such rule.

Worked example from the video

Example

Josh then puts Herman next to Earl. With two pie charts he asks one simple question: does Earl work more than Herman, and if so, how much more? Two circles make that surprisingly hard. The bar chart answers it in seconds. Herman sleeps about 30 minutes longer than Earl. Herman works about 40 minutes longer. Earl eats one hour longer, and Earl rests one hour longer. Four answers, all read off the same picture.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out A pie chart is not always wrong. It can work when you only need a rough sense of one big slice against the rest. It fails as soon as the slices are close in size.
Watch out Two pie charts side by side are the worst case. Your eye must compare angles across two circles, so real differences vanish. One bar chart with paired bars solves it.
Watch out Pie charts also cost space. As Josh says, a bar chart saves space and still leaves room for clear labels and values.

If they ask you in an interview

Why do you prefer bar charts to pie charts?
Because a bar chart puts every value on one shared baseline. We compare lengths on a common scale well, and angles poorly. In a pie chart two categories that differ by a full hour can look identical. On bars that hour is a visible step.
Would you ever still use a pie chart?
Only when the question is rough and the parts are few, for example: is this slice about half of the total? If the differences are large and the audience expects a share-of-whole picture, a pie is readable. The moment someone asks how much more, I move to bars.
How would you compare two groups across the same categories?
I would use one bar chart with the two groups side by side inside each category, on a shared scale. Then every comparison is a height difference the reader can see. I would avoid two separate pie charts, because comparing across circles hides the differences.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • A pie chart shows parts of a whole as slices of a circle.
  • A bar chart shows the same values as bars on one shared baseline.
  • People read lengths on a common scale much better than angles.
  • In Herman's day, the bars show the one-hour gaps that the pie hides.
  • With two people, two pie charts make the comparison very hard.
  • Paired bars answer four questions about Herman and Earl at a glance.
  • A bar chart also saves space, so plan bars where you were going to draw a pie.
