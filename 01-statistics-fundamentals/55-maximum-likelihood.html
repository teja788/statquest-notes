Maximum Likelihood

▶ Watch on YouTube

Maximum likelihood is how we fit a distribution to data. We pick the distribution settings that make our observed data as likely as possible.

In one breath

Josh weighs a bunch of mice and describes the weights with a curve. He picks a normal curve, because most weights sit near the average. He then slides that curve along the axis. At each position he asks how likely the measured weights would be. The best position is the maximum likelihood estimate of the mean. He repeats the search for the standard deviation.

The main idea

A distribution is a smooth curve that says which values are common and which are rare. Fitting one to your data is useful because a curve is easier to work with than a pile of numbers. It is also more general, so it applies to every experiment of the same type.

There are many shapes to choose from: normal, exponential, gamma, and more. Josh chooses the normal shape, because most mice weigh close to the average and the values are not badly skewed. Choosing the shape is only half the job. A normal curve can be skinny or wide, and it can sit anywhere on the axis. Maximum likelihood picks those settings for you.

Step by step

  1. Look at the data and choose a family of distributions. Josh chooses the normal family, because the weights cluster near the average and are roughly symmetrical.
  2. Pick any centre for the curve and check the fit. With the centre far from the data, most measured weights fall in the thin tail, so their likelihood is low.
  3. Shift the centre until it matches the average weight. Now the measurements sit under the tall part of the curve and the likelihood is high.
  4. Keep shifting past the data and the likelihood drops again. Plot likelihood against the centre position and take the peak. That position is the maximum likelihood estimate of the mean.
  5. Repeat the search for the standard deviation, which sets the width. The two winning values give the fitted distribution.

Formulas

The video stays graphical. The likelihood is the product of the curve heights at your data points.

Likelihood of the data
$$ L(\mu,\sigma \mid x_1,\dots,x_n) \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\, e^{-\frac{(x_i-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}} $$

Symbols: \(x_1,\dots,x_n\) are the measured mouse weights; \(n\) is how many mice; \(\mu\) is the candidate centre; \(\sigma\) is the candidate width; \(\prod\) means multiply the terms together. What it tells you: it scores one candidate curve by multiplying its height above every data point.

The values that win, for a normal curve
$$ \hat{\mu} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i \qquad\qquad \hat{\sigma} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(x_i-\hat{\mu}\right)^2} $$

Symbols: the hat means "the estimate that maximises the likelihood"; \(\sum\) means add up over all the mice. What it tells you: for a normal curve the search lands on the average of the data. That is why Josh says the two means agree here.

Worked example from the video

Example

Josh lays the mouse weights on one axis and drops a normal curve far to the left. The score for that curve is the height above the first mouse, times the height above the second, and so on. Out in the tail every height is tiny, so the product is tiny too.

He then slides the curve until its centre matches the average weight. Now the mice sit under the peak, so the heights are large and the product is as big as it gets.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out In everyday speech, probability and likelihood mean the same thing. In statistics they do not. Likelihood scores a candidate curve against data you already observed.
Watch out Josh estimates the mean of the distribution, not the mean of the data. Those are different ideas. They happen to give the same number for a normal curve, which hides the distinction.
Watch out Maximum likelihood does not choose the shape for you. You choose normal, exponential, or gamma first. Maximum likelihood only tunes the settings inside that choice.

If they ask you in an interview

What is maximum likelihood in plain words?
It is a way to fit a distribution to data. You try different values for the settings of the distribution, such as the mean and the standard deviation. For each set of values you ask how likely your observed data would be. You keep the values that make the observed data most likely.
How is likelihood different from probability?
In casual speech they are the same word. In statistics, probability describes data given fixed settings for the distribution. Likelihood flips it around: the data is fixed because you already measured it, and you vary the settings to see which ones explain it best.
For a normal distribution, what does maximum likelihood give you?
It gives the average of the data as the estimate of the mean. For the standard deviation it gives the root mean squared distance from that average. So the fitted centre lands exactly on the average of the measurements.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • Fitting a distribution turns a pile of measurements into one curve that is easier to use.
  • You first choose the family, such as normal, based on the shape of your data.
  • Likelihood scores a candidate curve by how plausible it makes the measured data.
  • Sliding the curve too far left or right makes the likelihood small.
  • The position that maximises the likelihood is the maximum likelihood estimate of the mean.
  • You then run the same search for the standard deviation, which sets the width.
  • For a normal curve the estimated mean equals the average of the data.
