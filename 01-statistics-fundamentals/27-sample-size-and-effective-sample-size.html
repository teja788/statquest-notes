Sample Size and Effective Sample Size

▶ Watch on YouTube

Sample size is not the number of tubes you measured. It is the number of independent pieces of information you hold.

In one breath

The letter \(n\) is shorthand for sample size. The question you want to answer decides what counts as one sample. Measuring the same individual twice does not give you two individuals. Two individuals who are very alike, such as twins, do not give you two separate pieces of information either. The effective sample size puts a number on that overlap.

The main idea

Josh starts with blue dudes. A blue dude can be a strain of mouse, or a type of tree, or any group you like. He takes a blood sample from each of three blue dudes and measures gene expression, which means how active a gene is. He wants to report on blue dudes, so each dude is one sample and \(n = 3\).

Now he measures each blue dude twice. The claim is still about dudes, so \(n\) is still three. The repeated pairs are technical replicates. They tell you how accurate your measuring method is. They do not tell you how blue dudes differ. They count as samples only when the claim is about the method, and there \(n = 4\).

The same picture shows the limit of any study. If you want to report on blue ladies, then \(n = 0\).

Step by step

  1. Write down the claim first. A claim about a method and a claim about dudes need different counts.
  2. Count the independent sources that match that claim. Repeated measurements of one individual stay one individual.
  3. To speak about dudes in general, widen the study. Josh adds an orange dude and a green dude, so \(n = 3\).
  4. Now add the blue dude's twin. He is a fourth person, but the twins share a genome, so their gene expression is highly correlated.
  5. Estimate that correlation and put it into the effective sample size formula. The pair then counts as between one and two people.
  6. Add that effective count to the independent individuals. That gives the effective sample size of the whole study.

Formulas

Effective sample size
$$ n_{\text{eff}} \;=\; \frac{n}{1 + (n-1)\,r} $$

Symbols: \(n\) is how many measurements are in the correlated group, so two for twins. \(r\) is the correlation between them, a number from 0 to 1 saying how much they move together. \(n_{\text{eff}}\) is how many truly separate samples that group is worth. What it tells you: correlated samples repeat information, so the group is worth less than its head count.

The two ends of the scale
$$ r = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; n_{\text{eff}} = \frac{n}{1} = n \qquad\qquad r = 1 \;\Rightarrow\; n_{\text{eff}} = \frac{n}{n} = 1 $$

Symbols: the same \(n\) and \(r\) as above. What it tells you: with no correlation everybody counts fully. With perfect correlation the whole group counts as one sample.

Worked example from the video

Example

The study has an orange dude, a green dude, a blue dude, and the blue dude's twin. The twins are the correlated pair, so \(n = 2\) goes into the formula.

First Josh sets the correlation between the twins to 0.7, which is high:

$$ n_{\text{eff}} = \frac{2}{1 + (2-1)\times 0.7} = \frac{2}{1.7} = 1.18 $$

The twins count as 1.18 people instead of two. With the orange and green dudes added, the study is worth \(2 + 1.18 = 3.18\) samples.

Then he lowers the correlation to 0.1, which is low:

$$ n_{\text{eff}} = \frac{2}{1 + (2-1)\times 0.1} = \frac{2}{1.1} = 1.82 $$

Now the twins count as 1.82 people, almost the full two. The study is worth \(2 + 1.82 = 3.82\) samples.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out Repeated measurements are not extra subjects. Measuring three blue dudes twice each still gives you three blue dudes when the claim is about dudes.
Watch out Technical replicates do count, but only for a claim about the method. Decide the claim first, then count, never the other way round.
Watch out The effective sample size is not a rounding rule. It is a smooth number between one and the head count. It falls as the correlation rises.
Watch out Josh warns that real calculations of effective sample size get more complicated. The idea stays the same: correlated samples do not count as separate samples.

If they ask you in an interview

What does sample size actually count?
It counts the independent units that match the claim I want to make. If I report on blue dudes, each dude is one unit, however often I measure him. If I report on my method, each measurement is a unit.
What is effective sample size, in one sentence?
It is how many truly independent samples my data are worth after I allow for correlation. For \(n\) correlated measurements with correlation \(r\), it is \(n\) divided by \(1 + (n-1)r\). It always sits between one and \(n\).
Twins in a study, correlation 0.7. How many samples is that pair worth?
Two divided by 1.7, so about 1.18. The twins share a genome, so their measurements repeat the same information. At a correlation of 0.1 the pair is worth about 1.82, which is nearly two full people.
Why does it matter if you overstate the sample size?
An inflated count makes your uncertainty look smaller than it really is. You then report a finding as solid when the data cannot support it. The effective sample size keeps that uncertainty honest.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • The letter \(n\) is shorthand for sample size, and the claim you want to make decides what one sample is.
  • Three blue dudes give \(n = 3\), even if you measure each one twice.
  • Technical replicates count as samples only when the claim is about the method.
  • You can say nothing about a group you never measured, so blue ladies give \(n = 0\).
  • The effective sample size is \(n\) divided by \(1 + (n-1)r\), where \(r\) is the correlation.
  • Twins with correlation 0.7 count as 1.18 people, and with correlation 0.1 as 1.82 people.
  • The four-person study is therefore worth 3.18 or 3.82 samples, not a flat four.
