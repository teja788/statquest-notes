The Difference Between Technical and Biological Replicates

▶ Watch on YouTube

Repeating a measurement is easy. Knowing what your repeats actually prove is the hard part.

In one breath

A replicate is one repeat of a measurement. Technical replicates repeat the experiment on the same individual. They describe that individual and show how reliable your method is. Biological replicates measure different individuals, so they let you talk about a group. The story you want to tell decides which kind you need.

The main idea

Josh starts with some dude. He takes a blood sample and measures gene expression, which means how active a gene is. Then he measures the same sample again, and again. Those three numbers are technical replicates, because every measurement came from the exact same sample.

Technical replicates do two jobs. They give an accurate value for this dude, and they show how accurately the method measures. If the three numbers came out far apart, no single measurement could be trusted.

Next Josh takes three separate samples from the same dude. These are still technical replicates, because they still describe one individual. Finally he samples three different dudes. Those are biological replicates, because each measurement comes from a different biological source.

Step by step

  1. Decide the story first. Is it about your method, one individual, or a group?
  2. For the method, measure one sample several times. The spread is measurement error.
  3. For one individual, take several samples from that person. This adds sample handling variation, but it is still one person.
  4. For a group, sample different individuals. Only biological replicates carry information about the population.
  5. Check who is inside your group. Three dudes support claims about dudes, not about ladies.
  6. Choose the mix by budget. For RNA-Seq, Josh says extra biological replicates buy more.

Formulas

The video has no formula, because it is a lesson about study design. The rule of thumb below shows why the two kinds of replicate are not interchangeable.

Variance of a group average
$$ \operatorname{Var}(\bar{y}) \;=\; \frac{\sigma^2_{\text{bio}}}{n} \;+\; \frac{\sigma^2_{\text{tech}}}{n\,k} $$

Symbols: \( \bar{y} \) is the study average. \( n \) is the number of dudes, the biological replicates. \( k \) is the technical replicates per dude. \( \sigma^2_{\text{bio}} \) is the spread from one dude to the next, and \( \sigma^2_{\text{tech}} \) is the spread the measurement adds. What it tells you: raising \( k \) shrinks only the second term. Only a larger \( n \) shrinks both.

Worked example from the video

Example

Josh runs the same gene-expression experiment three ways. First, one sample from one dude, measured three times. The spread shows how trustworthy the method is, so this design suits a paper about a new method.

Second, three samples from that same dude. These are still technical replicates. The extra spread now includes sample differences the method did not cause, so it says more about the dude than the method.

Third, one sample from each of three dudes. These are biological replicates. Only here can you speak about dudes as a group.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out More tubes from the same person are not biological replicates. What counts is the number of different sources, so three tubes from one dude still give you one dude.
Watch out Counting technical replicates as independent data points inflates your sample size. Your statistics then look far more confident than the evidence deserves.
Watch out Conclusions cannot travel further than your samples. Sampling only blue dudes supports claims about blue dudes, not orange or green ones, and not ladies.
Watch out Mixing both kinds is allowed, but the right mix depends on the experiment. For RNA-Seq, Josh says spend the budget on biological replicates.

If they ask you in an interview

What is the difference between a technical and a biological replicate?
A technical replicate repeats the measurement on the same individual, either on one sample or on several samples from that person. A biological replicate measures a different individual or cell line. Technical replicates describe one subject and your method. Biological replicates let you talk about a population.
Why can technical replicates make results look better than they are?
Because people count them as independent observations. If I measure one person ten times, I have ten numbers but still one person. The standard error shrinks as if I had ten subjects, so p-values look strong while the evidence has not changed.
How do you decide which population your result applies to?
I look at who my biological replicates came from. The result generalises only to that kind of subject. If every subject was male, I say nothing about women. The same holds for ethnicity or cell line.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • Technical replicates repeat the experiment on the same person, from one sample or from several samples.
  • They give an accurate value for that individual and show how reliable the method is.
  • Biological replicates come from different sources, such as different people, plants, or cell lines.
  • Only biological replicates support a claim about a group.
  • Counting technical replicates as independent subjects makes results look stronger than they are.
  • Your conclusion reaches only as far as the subjects you sampled.
  • Pick the design by asking which story you want to tell: an individual and a method, or a group.
