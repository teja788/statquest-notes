The Central Limit Theorem

▶ Watch on YouTube

Josh opens with the line that carries the whole lesson: even if you are not normal, the average is normal. This video shows why that one fact supports most standard statistical tests.

In one breath

Josh draws 20 random values from a flat uniform distribution and takes their mean. He plots that single number in a histogram, then repeats until he has 100 means. The histogram of means looks normal. He repeats everything with a lopsided exponential distribution and gets a normal histogram again. Whatever shape the data has, the sample means are normally distributed.

The main idea

The word "normal" here means the bell shaped curve. Many statistical tests want that shape, but real data often does not have it.

Josh starts with a uniform distribution from zero to one. Uniform means every value in that range is equally likely, so the picture is a flat rectangle.

Then he runs a loop. Take 20 random values, calculate their mean, and add that one number to a histogram. Repeat with fresh values until the histogram holds 100 means.

One mean tells you nothing. By 100 the histogram is clearly a bell, and Josh lays a normal curve on top to prove it. The data was flat, but the means are normal.

Step by step

  1. Pick a distribution to sample from. Josh picks a uniform distribution from zero to one.
  2. Collect 20 random values from it. Those 20 values are one sample.
  3. Calculate the mean of the sample. One sample gives exactly one mean.
  4. Add that mean to a histogram, then repeat until you have 100 means. A bell shape appears.
  5. Repeat the whole experiment with an exponential distribution. The 100 means are normal again, so the starting shape does not matter.

Formulas

The video is built from pictures, not algebra. Josh writes no equations on screen. These two lines are the usual written form of what the pictures show.

The sample mean
$$ \bar{X} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i $$

Symbols: \(X_i\) is one value in a sample; \(n\) is the sample size, 20 in both examples; \(\sum\) means add up; \(\bar{X}\) is the mean of that sample. What it tells you: a whole sample collapses into one number, and the histogram of many such numbers is what turns out normal.

The central limit theorem
$$ \bar{X} \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu,\ \frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\right) \quad \text{approximately} $$

Symbols: \(\mathcal{N}\) is the normal distribution; \(\mu\) is the mean of the source distribution; \(\sigma^{2}\) is its variance, a measure of spread; \(n\) is the sample size; \(\sim\) means "is distributed as". What it tells you: sample means are normal whatever shape \(X\) has, and they cluster more tightly as \(n\) grows.

Worked example from the video

Example

The source is a uniform distribution from 0 to 1. Josh draws 20 values, averages them, and plots that mean. He builds the histogram up to 10 means, then 20, 30, and on to 100. At 100 the histogram is clearly bell shaped, and an overlaid normal curve fits it.

He then swaps the source for an exponential distribution, which piles most of its probability near zero. The procedure is identical: 20 values, one mean, one bar, 100 times. The result is normal again.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out The theorem is about the means, not your data. Your 20 raw values still come from the original distribution. Only the histogram of many means becomes normal.
Watch out Do not treat a sample size of 30 as a law. Josh calls it a rule of thumb, and he breaks it here with samples of 20.
Watch out There is fine print. The distribution must have a mean you can calculate. The Cauchy distribution is the one case Josh can name, and he never met it in 20 years of biostatistics.

If they ask you in an interview

What does the central limit theorem say?
Take samples from a distribution and calculate each sample mean. Those means are normally distributed, whatever the original shape was. Josh shows it with a flat uniform distribution and with a skewed exponential one.
Why does it matter in practice?
We rarely know the distribution our data came from. The theorem says we do not need to, as long as we work with sample means. That is what supports confidence intervals, t-tests, ANOVA, and any test built on the mean.
Do you always need 30 observations per sample?
No. Thirty is a rule of thumb people quote because it is generally safe. Here the sample size is 20 and the means still look normal. How much you need depends on how skewed the source is.
When does the theorem fail?
Only when the distribution has no mean to calculate. The Cauchy distribution is the classic case. In practice it almost never comes up.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • Sample means are normally distributed, whatever the source data looks like.
  • Josh takes 20 random values from a flat uniform distribution and records the mean.
  • After 100 means the histogram forms a clear bell shape.
  • An exponential source gives the same bell shape.
  • So we do not need to know the distribution our data came from.
  • That is why confidence intervals, t-tests, and ANOVA work so widely.
  • The sample size of 30 is only a rule of thumb, and 20 works fine here.
