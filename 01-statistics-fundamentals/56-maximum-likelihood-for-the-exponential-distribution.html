Maximum Likelihood for the Exponential Distribution

▶ Watch on YouTube

The exponential distribution models the time between events. This video shows how to pick the one rate parameter that best fits your measured waiting times.

In one breath

An exponential distribution has a single knob, the rate \(\lambda\). Josh collects the gaps in time between views of his video and asks which \(\lambda\) makes that data most likely. He multiplies the individual likelihoods together, takes the log to make the algebra easy, then sets the derivative to zero. The answer is simple: \(\lambda\) equals the number of measurements divided by their sum. That is just one over the average waiting time.

The main idea

The exponential distribution is a statistical distribution that models the time between events. How long will you wait before the next text message arrives? How much time passes before the next person views this video? Those waiting times follow this shape.

The x-axis is the amount of time between events. The y-axis is scaled so the whole area under the curve equals 1. To get the probability that an event happens within 0 to 5 seconds, you find the area under the curve between \(x = 0\) and \(x = 5\).

The curve has one parameter, \(\lambda\), called the rate parameter. It is proportional to how quickly things happen. When \(\lambda = 1\), events happen on average once every second. When \(\lambda = 2\), events happen on average twice every second. When \(\lambda = 0.5\), events happen on average once every two seconds. Maximum likelihood is how we choose \(\lambda\) from real data.

Step by step

  1. Collect the data. \(x_1\) is the time that passed between the first and second views. \(x_2\) is the time between the second and third views, and so on up to \(x_n\).
  2. Write the likelihood for one measurement. For a value of \(\lambda\) we plug \(x_1\) into the curve's equation. The height of the curve at that point is the likelihood of \(\lambda\) given \(x_1\).
  3. Combine two measurements. Because we care about \(x_1\) and \(x_2\), we multiply the two likelihoods. Pull the two \(\lambda\) terms out and add the exponents together.
  4. Combine all the measurements. The full likelihood is the product of all \(n\) individual likelihoods. It collapses to \(\lambda^n\) times \(e\) raised to minus \(\lambda\) times the sum of every measurement.
  5. Look at the likelihood as a function of \(\lambda\). It rises, reaches a peak, and falls. At the peak the slope, and so the derivative, equals zero.
  6. Take the log first. Any function with an exponential in it is almost always easier to differentiate after taking the log. The log turns the product into a sum, moves the exponent \(n\) in front, and reduces the exponential term to just its exponent.
  7. Take the derivative with respect to \(\lambda\), set it to zero, and solve. That gives the maximum likelihood estimate.

Formulas

The exponential distribution
$$ y = \lambda\, e^{-\lambda x} $$

Symbols: \(x\) is the time between two events. \(y\) is the height of the curve at that time. \(\lambda\) is the rate parameter, which is proportional to how quickly events happen. \(e\) is Euler's number, about 2.718. What it tells you: you plug in a value for \(x\) and out comes a value for \(y\); the area under this curve over a range of times is the probability of waiting that long.

Likelihood of λ given two measurements
$$ L(\lambda \mid x_1, x_2) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x_1} \times \lambda e^{-\lambda x_2} = \lambda^{2} e^{-\lambda (x_1 + x_2)} $$

Symbols: \(L\) is the likelihood, read as "the likelihood of \(\lambda\) given the data". \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) are the first two measured waiting times. What it tells you: because we want both measurements at once, we multiply their likelihoods; the two \(\lambda\) terms combine into \(\lambda^2\) and the two exponents add together.

Likelihood of λ given all the data
$$ L(\lambda \mid x_1 \ldots x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} \lambda e^{-\lambda x_i} = \lambda^{n} e^{-\lambda (x_1 + x_2 + \cdots + x_n)} $$

Symbols: \(n\) is how many measurements you have. \(\prod\) means multiply every term together. What it tells you: the same trick scales to \(n\) points; all the \(\lambda\) terms pull out as \(\lambda^n\) and all the exponents add into one sum.

Step 1 — take the log, then the derivative
$$ \ln L = n \ln(\lambda) - \lambda (x_1 + x_2 + \cdots + x_n) $$

The log splits the multiplication into addition. The exponent \(n\) moves in front of the log, and the log of the exponential term reduces to just its exponent. Now differentiate with respect to \(\lambda\):

$$ \frac{d\,\ln L}{d\lambda} = \frac{n}{\lambda} - (x_1 + x_2 + \cdots + x_n) $$

Symbols: \(\ln\) is the natural log. \(\tfrac{d}{d\lambda}\) means the slope as \(\lambda\) changes. What it tells you: the log is safe to use here because a function and its log have a derivative of zero at the same place, so the peak does not move.

Step 2 — set the derivative to zero and solve
$$ \frac{n}{\lambda} - (x_1 + \cdots + x_n) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{n}{\lambda} = x_1 + \cdots + x_n \;\Rightarrow\; n = \lambda (x_1 + \cdots + x_n) $$

First add the sum to both sides, so that term cancels and leaves \(n/\lambda\) alone. Then multiply both sides by \(\lambda\). Finally divide both sides by the sum.

$$ \hat{\lambda} = \frac{n}{x_1 + x_2 + \cdots + x_n} $$

Symbols: \(\hat{\lambda}\) is the maximum likelihood estimate of the rate. What it tells you: the best rate is the count of measurements divided by their total; that is one divided by the mean waiting time.

Worked example from the video

Example

Josh measures the gaps between views of this video. Two seconds passed between the first and second views, so \(x_1 = 2\). Two and a half seconds passed between the second and third views, so \(x_2 = 2.5\). One and a half seconds passed between the third and fourth views, so \(x_3 = 1.5\).

That is all the data, so \(n = 3\). Plug the three values into the estimate:

$$ \hat{\lambda} = \frac{3}{2 + 2.5 + 1.5} = \frac{3}{6} = 0.5 $$

Given this data, the maximum likelihood estimate for \(\lambda\) is 0.5. We can now draw the exponential distribution with \(\lambda = 0.5\) and use it as the fitted model. It says an event happens on average once every two seconds.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out The likelihood is a function of \(\lambda\), not of \(x\). When you plug a measurement into the curve you get one number. You then vary \(\lambda\) and watch that number change. The data stays fixed the whole time.
Watch out Taking the log is a convenience, not a different problem. A function and the log of that function have a derivative of zero in the same place. So for finding the peak they are interchangeable, and the log turns a hard product into an easy sum.
Watch out Multiplying the individual likelihoods assumes the measurements are independent. The video does exactly this when it writes the combined likelihood as a product. If the waiting times influence each other, that product no longer holds.
Watch out Do not mix up the rate and the mean. \(\lambda\) is events per unit of time. The average waiting time is \(1/\lambda\). In the example \(\lambda = 0.5\) per second, which is an average wait of 2 seconds.

If they ask you in an interview

What does the exponential distribution model, and what is its parameter?
It models the time between events, like the gap before the next text message arrives. It has one parameter, the rate \(\lambda\), which is proportional to how quickly events happen. The density is \(\lambda e^{-\lambda x}\), and the area under the curve equals 1.
Derive the maximum likelihood estimate for λ.
Write the likelihood as the product of \(\lambda e^{-\lambda x_i}\) over all \(n\) points, which simplifies to \(\lambda^n e^{-\lambda \sum x_i}\). Take the log to get \(n\ln\lambda - \lambda \sum x_i\). Differentiate to get \(n/\lambda - \sum x_i\), set it to zero, and solve. You get \(\hat{\lambda} = n / \sum x_i\).
Why take the log of the likelihood before differentiating?
Because the likelihood is a product with an exponential in it, and that is awkward to differentiate. The log turns the product into a sum and reduces the exponential to its exponent. It is valid because the derivative of a function and the derivative of its log are zero in the same place, so the location of the peak does not change.
How do you interpret the estimate in plain terms?
The estimate is the number of measurements divided by their total time, so it is one over the average waiting time. If your three gaps are 2, 2.5 and 1.5 seconds, the estimate is 3 divided by 6, which is 0.5. That means one event on average every two seconds.
How do you know the solution is a maximum and not a minimum?
If you plot the likelihood against candidate values of \(\lambda\), it rises from zero, reaches a single peak, then decays. The derivative is zero only at that peak. So the single solution of the derivative equation is the maximum likelihood estimate.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • The exponential distribution models the time between events and has the density \(\lambda e^{-\lambda x}\).
  • The single parameter \(\lambda\) is the rate, and it is proportional to how quickly events happen.
  • The likelihood of \(\lambda\) given one measurement is just the height of the curve at that measurement.
  • For several measurements you multiply the individual likelihoods, which gives \(\lambda^n e^{-\lambda \sum x_i}\).
  • Take the log first, because it turns the product into a sum without moving the peak.
  • Differentiate the log likelihood, set it to zero, and solve for \(\lambda\).
  • The maximum likelihood estimate is \(\hat{\lambda} = n / (x_1 + \cdots + x_n)\), which is one over the mean waiting time.
  • With gaps of 2, 2.5 and 1.5 seconds the estimate is 0.5, so an event happens on average every two seconds.
