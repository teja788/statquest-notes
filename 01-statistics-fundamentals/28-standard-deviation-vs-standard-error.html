Standard Deviation vs Standard Error

▶ Watch on YouTube

The standard deviation describes your measurements. The standard error describes your averages. Swapping them makes weak results look tidy.

In one breath

Josh weighs five mice and gets one mean and one standard deviation. The standard deviation says how spread out those five weights are. Then he repeats the experiment five times with new mice, so he gets five means. Those five means have their own spread. That spread is the standard error.

The main idea

Imagine you weigh five mice. The mean is their average, and it sits in the middle of the data. The standard deviation is a distance on both sides of the mean. It quantifies how much the five weights are spread out.

Now run the exact same experiment five separate times, with different mice each time. Every set gives you its own mean and its own standard deviation. Each standard deviation describes the spread inside one set only.

Finally, plot only the five means on a single number line. Those means have a mean of their own, called the mean of the means. They also have a standard deviation of their own. That standard deviation of the means is the standard error.

Step by step

  1. Weigh five mice and write down the five weights. This is one set of measurements.
  2. Calculate the mean of that set. It marks the middle of those five points.
  3. Calculate the standard deviation of that set. It quantifies the variation inside this one set.
  4. Repeat the experiment four more times with different mice. You now hold five means and five standard deviations.
  5. Plot the five means on one number line. Treat them as a small data set. Their standard deviation is the standard error.

Josh names the confusing part directly. The standard error can be estimated from a single set of measurements, even though it describes the means from many sets. That is why software offers you the standard error when you measured only once.

Formulas

The video shows no formulas. These two are the standard ones behind the pictures.

Standard deviation of one set
$$ s=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(x_i-\bar{x}\right)^{2}} $$

Symbols: \(x_i\) is one measurement, such as the weight of one mouse. \(\bar{x}\) is the mean of that set. \(n\) is how many measurements the set contains. \(s\) is the standard deviation. What it tells you: the typical distance from a single measurement to the mean of its own set.

Standard error of the mean
$$ SE=\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} $$

Symbols: \(s\) is the standard deviation of your one set. \(n\) is the number of measurements in it. \(SE\) is the estimated standard deviation of the means. What it tells you: how far the mean would move if you repeated the experiment. A larger set gives a smaller standard error.

Worked example from the video

Example

Josh shows no numbers on screen. These weights follow his setup of five mice, weighed five times over.

Set 1: 18, 20, 21, 22, 24 grams. The mean is \(105/5 = 21\) g. The deviations are −3, −1, 0, 1 and 3, and their squares add to 20. Divide by \(n-1 = 4\), then take the square root: \(s = 2.24\) g. So \(SE = 2.24/\sqrt{5} = 1.00\) g.

All five sets: the means are 21.0, 20.2, 22.2, 19.8 and 21.6 grams. Their own standard deviation is 0.98 g. That is the standard error measured the long way. It nearly matches the 1.00 g estimated from set 1 alone.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out The standard error is smaller than the standard deviation whenever \(n\) is above one. Smaller bars look like cleaner data, but the data did not change.
Watch out The standard error describes means from many sets, yet you can estimate it from one set. That mismatch is exactly what makes the two terms so easy to confuse.
Watch out Josh's default is clear: in almost all cases plot the standard deviation, because a graph is usually meant to describe the data you actually measured.

If they ask you in an interview

What is the difference between standard deviation and standard error?
The standard deviation quantifies the variation within one set of measurements. The standard error quantifies the variation in the means from multiple sets. Put simply, the standard error is the standard deviation of the means, so it describes the estimate rather than the data.
Why is the standard error smaller than the standard deviation?
Averaging cancels some of the noise, so means sit closer together than individual measurements. The formula shows it: we divide by the square root of the sample size. Four times more data halves the standard error.
Which one should you plot on a chart?
In almost all cases the standard deviation, because the chart describes the data you measured. I would use the standard error only when the point is the precision of the estimate. Either way I label the bars.
How can you get a standard error when you ran the experiment only once?
You estimate it rather than observe it. You take the standard deviation of your single set and divide by the square root of the sample size. It still describes how the mean would move across repeats you never ran.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • The standard deviation quantifies the variation within one set of measurements.
  • The standard error quantifies the variation in the means from multiple sets.
  • So the standard error is nothing more than the standard deviation of the means.
  • Five sets of five mice give five means, and the spread of those means is the standard error.
  • You can estimate the standard error from one set by dividing \(s\) by \(\sqrt{n}\).
  • Plot the standard deviation by default, because graphs are meant to describe your data.
