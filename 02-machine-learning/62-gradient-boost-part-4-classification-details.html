Gradient Boost Part 4 (of 4): Classification Details

▶ Watch on YouTube

This video walks through the original gradient boost algorithm for classification, line by line. It shows where the strange looking leaf output formula comes from.

In one breath

Gradient boost for classification predicts log odds, not probabilities. The loss function is the negative log likelihood, rewritten as a function of the predicted log odds. Its negative first derivative is the pseudo residual, which is simply observed minus predicted probability. Its second derivative is \(p(1-p)\). A second order Taylor approximation of the loss then gives each leaf the output value \(\sum r \,/\, \sum p(1-p)\).

The main idea

Josh uses a very small training set: three people. We know whether each person likes popcorn, their age, their favourite colour, and whether they love the movie Troll 2. Two people love Troll 2 and one does not. The set is too small for real trees, so the video uses stumps. In practice gradient boost uses trees with 8 to 32 leaves.

The algorithm is the same as for regression, with one twist. The predictions are on the log odds scale, as in logistic regression. The log odds is the log of the probability of yes divided by the probability of no. Every prediction is a log odds value, and we convert it to a probability when we need one.

Josh moves between log odds and probability again and again, because some steps are easier in one form. The first derivative of the loss gives the residual. The second derivative, \(p(1-p)\), goes in the denominator of the leaf output. That is the whole story of this video.

Step by step

  1. Write the log likelihood of the data given a predicted probability, the same one used in logistic regression. Multiply it by \(-1\) so that smaller means better. This is now a loss function.
  2. Rewrite that loss as a function of the predicted log odds instead of the predicted probability. This takes a few algebra steps, shown in the Formulas section.
  3. Take the derivative of the loss with respect to the predicted log odds. It is differentiable, which is what gradient boost needs.
  4. Step 1 of the algorithm. Find the constant log odds value that minimises the total loss. Setting the sum of derivatives to zero gives \(p = 2/3\), so the first leaf is \(F_0(x) = \log(2/1) = 0.69\).
  5. Step 2A. Compute the pseudo residuals. The negative derivative of the loss is exactly observed minus predicted probability, so with \(p = 0.67\) the residuals are 0.33, 0.33 and −0.67.
  6. Step 2B. Fit a regression tree to those residuals using the features. Name the leaves \(R_{j,m}\), where \(j\) is the leaf and \(m\) is the tree.
  7. Step 2C. Compute an output value \(\gamma_{j,m}\) for each leaf. Solving directly is a mess, so approximate the loss with a second order Taylor polynomial and solve that instead.
  8. Step 2D. Update the prediction: \(F_m(x) = F_{m-1}(x) + \nu \gamma_{j,m}\). Then set \(m = m+1\) and repeat from 2A.
  9. Step 3. Output \(F_M(x)\). Convert the final log odds to a probability and apply a threshold, usually 0.5.

Formulas

Loss function, in probability
$$ L = -\left[\,\text{obs}\cdot\log(p) + (1-\text{obs})\cdot\log(1-p)\,\right] $$

Symbols: \(\text{obs}\) is the observed label, 1 for yes and 0 for no. \(p\) is the predicted probability of yes. \(\log\) is the natural log. What it tells you: this is the log likelihood from logistic regression, flipped by the minus sign so that a smaller value means a better fit.

Derivation, step 1 of 3
$$ L = -\text{obs}\left[\log(p) - \log(1-p)\right] - \log(1-p) $$

We distribute the minus sign and group the two \(\log\) terms that contain \(\text{obs}\). This grouping lets the log odds appear in the next step.

Derivation, step 2 of 3
$$ \log(p) - \log(1-p) = \log\!\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right) = \log(\text{odds}) $$

The bracket is exactly the definition of the log odds, so the first term becomes \(-\text{obs}\cdot\log(\text{odds})\).

Derivation, step 3 of 3
$$ \log(1-p) = \log\!\left(\frac{1}{1+e^{\log(\text{odds})}}\right) = -\log\!\left(1+e^{\log(\text{odds})}\right) $$

We write \(1-p\) in terms of the log odds, and \(\log(1) = 0\) drops out. So the second term of the loss changes sign from minus to plus.

Loss function, in log odds
$$ L = -\,\text{obs}\cdot\log(\text{odds}) + \log\!\left(1+e^{\log(\text{odds})}\right) $$

Symbols: \(\text{obs}\) is 1 or 0, and \(\log(\text{odds})\) is the current prediction. What it tells you: this is the version gradient boost actually uses, because the trees add up log odds values.

First derivative (the pseudo residual)
$$ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \log(\text{odds})} = -\,\text{obs} + \frac{e^{\log(\text{odds})}}{1+e^{\log(\text{odds})}} = -(\text{obs} - p) $$

Symbols: the fraction is the standard conversion from log odds to the predicted probability \(p\). What it tells you: the negative derivative, which gradient boost calls the pseudo residual, is simply observed minus predicted probability.

Second derivative
$$ \frac{\partial^2 L}{\partial \log(\text{odds})^2} = \frac{\partial}{\partial \log(\text{odds})}\left[-\text{obs} + e^{\log(\text{odds})}\left(1+e^{\log(\text{odds})}\right)^{-1}\right] $$

The second derivative is the derivative of the first one. Josh writes the fraction as a product, so he can use the product rule.

$$ = \frac{e^{\log(\text{odds})}}{1+e^{\log(\text{odds})}} - \frac{e^{2\log(\text{odds})}}{\left(1+e^{\log(\text{odds})}\right)^{2}} = \frac{e^{\log(\text{odds})}}{\left(1+e^{\log(\text{odds})}\right)^{2}} $$

The \(-\text{obs}\) term has no log odds in it, so its derivative is zero. Over a common denominator, two terms in the numerator cancel.

$$ = \frac{e^{\log(\text{odds})}}{1+e^{\log(\text{odds})}} \times \frac{1}{1+e^{\log(\text{odds})}} = p\,(1-p) $$

Split the fraction into two factors. The first factor is \(p\), and the second is \(1-p\) from derivation step 3. Symbols: \(p\) is the current predicted probability. What it tells you: the messy second derivative is a simple product of two probabilities.

Second order Taylor approximation
$$ L\!\left(\text{obs},\, F_{m-1}+\gamma\right) \approx L + \frac{\partial L}{\partial F}\,\gamma + \frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^2 L}{\partial F^2}\,\gamma^{2} $$

Symbols: \(\gamma\) is the value we add to the current prediction, and \(F\) is short for the current log odds. What it tells you: the true loss is hard to minimise for \(\gamma\) directly, but this quadratic stand in is easy.

$$ \frac{d}{d\gamma}: \quad \frac{\partial L}{\partial F} + \frac{\partial^2 L}{\partial F^2}\,\gamma = 0 $$

Take the derivative with respect to \(\gamma\) and set it to zero. The first term has no \(\gamma\), so it drops out, and the square cancels the 1/2.

Leaf output value
$$ \gamma_{j,m} = \frac{-\sum \partial L/\partial F}{\sum \partial^2 L/\partial F^2} = \frac{\sum_i r_{i,m}}{\sum_i p_i\,(1-p_i)} $$

Symbols: the sums run over the samples in leaf \(j\) of tree \(m\). \(r_{i,m}\) is that sample's residual and \(p_i\) its most recent predicted probability. What it tells you: solve for \(\gamma\), then plug in \(-(\text{obs}-p)\) and \(p(1-p)\). With two samples in a leaf, Josh adds their Taylor polynomials, so each part becomes a sum.

Update rule
$$ F_m(x) = F_{m-1}(x) + \nu \sum_{j} \gamma_{j,m}\,\mathbf{1}\!\left(x \in R_{j,m}\right) $$

Symbols: \(\nu\) is the learning rate, \(R_{j,m}\) is leaf \(j\) of tree \(m\), and the indicator is 1 when the sample lands in that leaf. What it tells you: each new prediction is the old one plus a scaled leaf value. The sum is there only in case a sample reaches more than one leaf.

Worked example from the video

Example

Step 1, the initial leaf. Two of the three people love Troll 2, so the probability that minimises the total loss is \(p = 2/3 = 0.67\). Converting gives \(F_0(x) = \log(2/1) = 0.69\). Every sample starts with this same log odds.

Step 2A, residuals. With \(p = 0.67\) for everyone, the residuals are \(1-0.67=0.33\), \(1-0.67=0.33\) and \(0-0.67=-0.67\).

Step 2B, the tree. The stump splits on Likes Popcorn. Sample \(x_1\) goes to leaf \(R_{1,1}\). Samples \(x_2\) and \(x_3\) go to leaf \(R_{2,1}\).

Step 2C, leaf outputs. For the leaf with one sample: \(\gamma_{1,1} = 0.33 \,/\, (0.67 \times 0.33) = 1.5\). For the leaf with two samples: \(\gamma_{2,1} = (0.33 - 0.67) \,/\, (0.67 \times 0.33 + 0.67 \times 0.33) = -0.77\).

Step 2D, new predictions. The learning rate is \(\nu = 0.8\), which is deliberately large here. So \(F_1(x_1) = 0.69 + 0.8 \times 1.5 = 1.89\), and \(F_1(x_2) = F_1(x_3) = 0.69 + 0.8 \times (-0.77) = 0.07\).

Samples 1 and 3 improved, but sample 2 got worse. The video then builds a second tree and stops at \(M = 2\). In practice \(M\) is 100 or more.

Step 3, prediction for a new person. \(F_2(x) = 3.4\) in log odds. The probability is \(e^{3.4}/(1+e^{3.4}) = 0.97\). Since 0.97 is above the 0.5 threshold, we predict that this person loves Troll 2.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out The leaf output is not the average of the residuals in the leaf. The residuals live in probability space, but the prediction lives in log odds space. Dividing by \(\sum p(1-p)\) is the conversion between them.
Watch out Each \(p_i\) in the denominator is that sample's own most recent predicted probability, not a shared value. They happen to be equal for the first tree because every sample starts at the same initial leaf. After the first tree they differ.
Watch out A single tree can make one sample worse, as sample 2 shows here. Josh says this is why we build more than one tree. Judge the whole model, not any one tree.

If they ask you in an interview

Why does gradient boost for classification work with log odds instead of probabilities?
The trees add their outputs to the previous prediction. Probabilities are bounded between 0 and 1, so adding numbers to them can go out of range. Log odds are unbounded, so addition is always valid. We apply the logistic conversion at the end to get a probability back.
What is the loss function, and what is the pseudo residual?
The loss is the negative log likelihood, written as a function of the predicted log odds. Its negative first derivative is the pseudo residual, and that works out to be observed minus predicted probability. So the residual is simple even though the loss looks intimidating.
Why do we need a Taylor approximation to get the leaf output?
Taking the derivative of the real loss with respect to gamma and solving in closed form is not practical. So we replace the loss with its second order Taylor polynomial around the current prediction. That is a quadratic in gamma, so setting its derivative to zero gives gamma as minus the first derivative divided by the second derivative.
Explain the leaf output formula in one sentence.
The output of a leaf is the sum of the residuals of the samples in that leaf, divided by the sum of \(p(1-p)\) for those same samples. The numerator says which direction to move. The denominator is the sum of second derivatives of the loss, which puts the step on the log odds scale.
How does the classification version differ from the regression version?
The loop is identical: initial guess, residuals, tree on the residuals, leaf outputs, scaled update. Only two pieces change. The loss becomes the negative log likelihood instead of squared error, and the leaf output becomes \(\sum r / \sum p(1-p)\) instead of the plain average of the residuals.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • Gradient boost for classification predicts log odds, then converts to a probability at the end.
  • The loss function is the negative log likelihood, rewritten as a function of the predicted log odds.
  • Its first derivative gives the pseudo residual, which equals observed minus predicted probability.
  • Its second derivative simplifies to \(p(1-p)\).
  • The initial leaf is the log odds of the overall class balance, here \(\log(2/1) = 0.69\).
  • Each leaf output is the sum of residuals divided by the sum of \(p(1-p)\), found with a second order Taylor approximation.
  • Each new prediction adds the learning rate times the leaf output to the previous prediction.
  • After \(M\) trees, convert the final log odds to a probability and compare it to a threshold such as 0.5.
