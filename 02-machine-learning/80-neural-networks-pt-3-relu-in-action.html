Neural Networks Pt. 3: ReLU In Action

▶ Watch on YouTube Also in: Neural Networks / Deep Learning (video 8)

This video swaps the curved soft plus activation function for ReLU. Then it runs the drug dosage data through the network by hand. It shows that simple bent lines can still combine into a shape that fits the data.

In one breath

ReLU, short for rectified linear unit, outputs whichever value is larger: zero or its input. Each hidden node turns dosages into a bent or straight line, and the weights stretch and flip those lines. The network adds the lines, adds a final bias, and passes the sum through one more ReLU at the output. The result is a pointy shape that is zero for low and high dosages and positive for medium dosages. The bend in ReLU has no derivative, but we simply define it as zero or one.

The main idea

The data set is the one from part one. Low and high drug dosages do not work against the virus, but a medium dosage does. To keep the math simple, dosage goes from 0 (low) to 1 (high).

An activation function is the bent or curved function inside each node. In part one, the hidden layer used soft plus, and the network fit a green squiggle to the data. Here Josh replaces soft plus with ReLU. ReLU is one of the most popular activation functions for deep learning and convolutional neural networks. Because it is common to put an activation function before the final output, this network also has a ReLU right before the output.

ReLU is not curvy, and its equation is very simple. That can make it look too weak to fit anything interesting. But the weights and biases on the connections slice, flip, and stretch the ReLU into new shapes. The network then adds these shapes together to get an entirely new shape that fits the data.

Step by step

  1. Plug a dosage into the connection to the top hidden node. The connection multiplies the dosage by 1.70 and adds −0.85. The result is an x-axis coordinate for the activation function.
  2. Pass that value through ReLU. For dosages 0, 0.2, and 0.4 the value is negative, so ReLU outputs 0. At dosage 0.6 the value is 0.17, and ReLU outputs 0.17.
  3. Repeat for all dosages from 0 to 1. The outputs form a bent blue line: flat at zero, then rising.
  4. Multiply the bent blue line by −40.8. This flips it upside down and makes it much steeper.
  5. Run the dosages through the connection to the bottom hidden node. This gives a straight orange line. Multiply it by 2.70.
  6. Add the bent blue line and the straight orange line. The result is a green wedge.
  7. Add the final bias, −16, to every point on the green wedge.
  8. Pass each point of the green wedge through the ReLU in front of the output. Negative values become zero, and positive values stay the same. The result is the green pointy thing that fits the data.

Formulas

ReLU activation function
$$ f(x) = \max(0,\, x) $$

Symbols: \(x\) is the input to the node, which is the x-axis coordinate for the activation function. \(\max\) picks the larger of the two values. What it tells you: negative inputs become zero, and positive inputs pass through unchanged.

Top hidden node, scaled
$$ b(d) = -40.8 \cdot \max(0,\; 1.70\,d - 0.85) $$

Symbols: \(d\) is the dosage, from 0 to 1. \(1.70\) is the weight and \(-0.85\) is the bias on the input connection. \(-40.8\) is the weight on the output connection. \(b(d)\) is the final bent blue line. What it tells you: the blue line is zero for low dosages, then drops steeply once \(1.70\,d - 0.85\) turns positive.

Network output
$$ \text{output}(d) = \max\big(0,\; b(d) + 2.70 \cdot o(d) - 16\big) $$

Symbols: \(b(d)\) is the bent blue line from above. \(o(d)\) is the straight orange line from the bottom hidden node; the video does not read out its weight and bias. \(2.70\) is the weight on the bottom node's output connection. \(-16\) is the final bias. The outer \(\max\) is the ReLU in front of the output. What it tells you: the output is the sum of the two lines plus the bias, with every negative value set to zero.

Slope of ReLU for gradient descent
$$ f'(x) = \begin{cases} 0 & x < 0 \\ 1 & x > 0 \\ \text{define as } 0 \text{ or } 1 & x = 0 \end{cases} $$

Symbols: \(f'(x)\) is the derivative, or slope, of ReLU at input \(x\). What it tells you: the slope is zero on the flat part and one on the rising part. At the bend it is not defined, so we pick zero or one, and the choice does not really matter.

Next, add the scaled orange line and the final bias, −16, to get the green wedge. Then apply the output ReLU to each point.

  • The first point on the green wedge, at the lowest dosage, is −16. Because 0 is greater than −16, the output is 0.
  • The next point on the green wedge is −9.2. The output is again 0.
  • For one point near the middle, the green wedge is positive, 0.49. ReLU keeps it, so the output is 0.49.

All other positive values stay the same, and all negative values become zero. The final output is zero for low and high dosages and positive for medium dosages, which matches the data.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out "Not curvy" does not mean "cannot fit curves." Each ReLU is only a bent line, but after the weights flip and stretch them and the network adds them, the sum can take a new shape that fits the data.
Watch out ReLU has no derivative at the bend. Gradient descent, which estimates the weights and biases, needs a derivative at every point. The fix is simple: define the derivative at the bend as zero or one.
Watch out The ReLU before the output changes the answer. Without it, the output would be the green wedge, which is negative almost everywhere. With it, every negative value is set to zero.
Watch out Josh says his drawings are not to scale. If you draw this at home, the bent blue line will have a steeper slope than in the video.

If they ask you in an interview

What is the ReLU activation function?
ReLU stands for rectified linear unit. It outputs whichever value is larger, zero or its input. So negative inputs become zero and positive inputs pass through unchanged. It is one of the most popular activation functions in deep learning and convolutional neural networks.
ReLU is just two straight pieces. How can a network of ReLUs fit a curved pattern?
The weights and biases slice, flip, and stretch each ReLU into a new bent line. The network then adds these lines together. In the dosage example, a flipped bent line plus a straight line, shifted down by a bias, gives a wedge. A final ReLU turns that wedge into a peak at medium dosages.
ReLU is not differentiable at zero. How does gradient descent handle that?
Gradient descent needs a derivative at every point, and ReLU has none at the bend. In practice we just define the derivative there as zero or one. The choice does not really matter, so it is not a big problem.
What does a ReLU right before the output do?
It sets every negative value of the summed shape to zero and keeps the positive values. In the dosage example, the green wedge was −16 and −9.2 at low dosages, and the ReLU turned both into zero. Only the part above zero survived, which gave the final pointy shape.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • ReLU outputs the larger of zero and its input.
  • The top hidden node computes 1.70 × dosage − 0.85 and applies ReLU, giving a bent blue line.
  • Multiplying by −40.8 flips that line and makes it steep; the bottom node gives a straight orange line scaled by 2.70.
  • Adding the two lines and the final bias of −16 gives a green wedge.
  • A ReLU before the output sets negative values to zero, leaving a pointy shape that fits the medium dosages.
  • Weights and biases turn simple bent lines into a new shape that fits the data.
