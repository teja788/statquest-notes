Naive Bayes

▶ Watch on YouTube Also in: —

This video builds a spam filter by counting words. It is the multinomial Naive Bayes classifier, the version most people mean when they say Naive Bayes.

In one breath

Josh takes a small pile of messages and splits them into normal messages and spam. For each group he counts the words and turns those counts into probabilities. To classify a new message, he starts with an initial guess for each group and multiplies it by the probability of every word in the message. The group with the bigger result wins. Two fixes make it work in practice: add a small count to every word so nothing is zero, and accept that word order is ignored.

The main idea

Imagine you receive normal messages from friends and family, and you also receive spam. Spam is unwanted mail, usually a scam or an advertisement. You want to sort new messages into the right pile automatically.

The first step is a histogram of every word in the normal messages. A histogram here is just a bar chart of word counts. From the counts you get the probability of each word, given that the message is normal. The word dear occurred 8 times, and there are 17 words in all the normal messages, so the probability is 8/17 = 0.47. The same counting on the spam gives the probability of dear in spam: 2 out of 7 words, which is 0.29.

These numbers are probabilities of discrete words, not of something continuous like height or weight. Because of that, many people call them likelihoods instead. Here the two words mean the same thing, so do not worry about the label.

Step by step

  1. Count every word in the normal messages, and count every word in the spam. Keep the two counts separate.
  2. Turn each count into a probability. Divide the count of the word by the total number of words in that group.
  3. Make an initial guess that a message is normal. Josh uses the training data: 8 of the 12 messages are normal, so the guess is 0.67. This initial guess is called the prior probability.
  4. Make the same guess for spam. Four of the 12 messages are spam, so that prior is 0.33.
  5. For a new message, multiply the prior for normal by the probability of each word in the message, given a normal message. Repeat the multiplication with the spam numbers.
  6. Compare the two results. The bigger one wins, and that is the predicted class.

Formulas

Word probability
$$ P(w \mid C) = \frac{\text{count}(w, C)}{N_C} $$

Symbols: \(w\) is one word, such as dear. \(C\) is the class, either normal or spam. \(\text{count}(w, C)\) is how many times that word appears in that class. \(N_C\) is the total number of words in that class. What it tells you: how common a word is inside one class, which is the only thing the model learns from the text.

Score for a class
$$ \text{score}(C) = P(C) \times \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(w_i \mid C) $$

Symbols: \(P(C)\) is the prior, the initial guess that a message belongs to class \(C\). \(w_1 \dots w_n\) are the words of the new message, in any order. The symbol \(\prod\) means multiply all of these together. What it tells you: how well the class explains the message. The value is proportional to \(P(C \mid \text{message})\), so it is a score, not a real probability.

Decision rule
$$ \hat{C} = \arg\max_{C} \; \text{score}(C) $$

Symbols: \(\hat{C}\) is the predicted class. \(\arg\max\) means "the class that gives the largest value". What it tells you: only the ranking of the scores matters, not their size.

Adding counts (alpha)
$$ P(w \mid C) = \frac{\text{count}(w, C) + \alpha}{N_C + \alpha V} $$

Symbols: \(\alpha\) is the number of extra counts added to every word, usually 1. \(V\) is the number of different words in the histogram, which is 4 here. What it tells you: no word can have probability zero any more. For lunch in spam this gives \(\frac{0+1}{7+4} = 0.09\) instead of 0.

Worked example from the video

Example

Message 1: "Dear Friend". The word probabilities come from the two histograms. In normal messages, dear is 8/17 = 0.47 and friend is 5/17 = 0.29. In spam, dear is 2/7 = 0.29 and friend is 1/7 = 0.14. The priors are 8/12 = 0.67 for normal and 4/12 = 0.33 for spam.

$$ \text{score}(\text{normal}) = 0.67 \times 0.47 \times 0.29 = 0.09 $$
$$ \text{score}(\text{spam}) = 0.33 \times 0.29 \times 0.14 = 0.01 $$

0.09 is larger than 0.01, so the message is classified as normal.

Example

Message 2: "Lunch Money Money Money Money". The word money is much more likely in spam, so spam looks like the sensible answer. The normal score is the prior times the probability of lunch, times the probability of money four times over. That gives a tiny number, but a positive one.

$$ \text{score}(\text{normal}) = 0.67 \times 0.18 \times 0.06^4 $$

The spam score collapses to zero. The word lunch never appeared in the spam, so its probability is 0, and anything multiplied by zero is zero. The number of times money appears makes no difference, and the prior makes no difference either.

$$ \text{score}(\text{spam}) = 0.33 \times 0 \times 0.57^4 = 0 $$

So any message containing lunch would always be called normal. To fix this, add one count to every word in both histograms. The probability of lunch in spam becomes 1/(7+4) = 0.09. Now the spam score is above zero, it beats the normal score, and the message is correctly classified as spam.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out

A single unseen word can veto a whole class. One zero in the chain of multiplication sets the score to zero, whatever the other words say. Always add \(\alpha\) counts, and remember that \(\alpha\) is a choice you make, not a value learned from data.

Watch out

Adding counts to the words does not touch the priors. The extra counts change word histograms only. The number of normal messages and spam messages in the training data stays the same, so 0.67 and 0.33 do not move.

Watch out

The values 0.09 and 0.01 are not probabilities. They are proportional to the probability of the class given the message. Do not report them as "a 9% chance of being normal". Use them only to compare classes.

Watch out

This is why the method is called naive. It treats all word orders as the same. So "Dear Friend" and "Friend Dear" get the same score. Every language has grammar and common phrases, and the model throws all of that away. It treats a message as a bag of words, picked at random.

If they ask you in an interview

What exactly is naive about Naive Bayes?
It assumes the words are independent of each other, given the class. In practice that means word order and phrases are ignored completely, so "dear friend" and "friend dear" score the same. Tracking every reasonable phrase in a language would be impossible, so the model drops the whole idea. It is a strong, clearly wrong assumption that still works well.
Why do you add one count to every word?
Because a word that never appeared in a class gets probability zero, and zero wipes out the entire product. Then that class can never win, no matter how strong the other evidence is. Adding \(\alpha\) counts to every word, usually one, keeps every probability above zero. It changes only the word probabilities, never the priors.
Where do the priors come from?
They are the initial guess that a message belongs to a class, before you read a single word. You can pick any value between zero and one. The common choice is the proportion in the training data, which here was 8 of 12 messages normal and 4 of 12 spam.
How does Naive Bayes sit on the bias-variance trade-off?
It has high bias, because it ignores the relationships between words. But it works well in practice, so Josh says it has low variance. That is why it separates normal messages from spam surprisingly well.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • Count the words separately for each class, then divide by the total words in that class to get probabilities.
  • Pick a prior for each class, usually the share of that class in the training data.
  • To classify a message, multiply the prior by the probability of each word in the message.
  • Do this once per class and predict the class with the larger result.
  • Those results are scores, proportional to probabilities, not probabilities themselves.
  • A word never seen in a class makes its score zero, which breaks the comparison.
  • Add \(\alpha\) counts, usually one, to every word so that no probability is ever zero.
  • Word order is ignored, which is high bias, but the low variance makes it work well in practice.
