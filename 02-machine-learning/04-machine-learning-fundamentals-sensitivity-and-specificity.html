Machine Learning Fundamentals: Sensitivity and Specificity

▶ Watch on YouTube

A confusion matrix holds a lot of counts. Sensitivity and specificity squeeze those counts into two numbers that tell you how well a model finds positives and how well it finds negatives.

In one breath

Sensitivity is the share of true positives that the model actually caught. Specificity is the share of true negatives that the model actually caught. Josh computes both for a heart disease confusion matrix, once for logistic regression and once for a random forest. The two models score almost the same, so you pick the one whose strength matters more for your problem. When the matrix has three or more categories, you get one sensitivity and one specificity per category, not one pair for the whole table.

The main idea

This video follows on from the confusion matrix video. Remember the layout: the rows are what the model predicted, and the columns are the known truth. With two categories, here "has heart disease" and "does not have heart disease", the table has four cells.

The top left cell holds the true positives. These are patients who had heart disease and were predicted to have it. The bottom right cell holds the true negatives: patients without heart disease who were predicted not to have it. The bottom left cell holds the false negatives, meaning the patient had the disease but the model said no. The top right cell holds the false positives, meaning the patient was healthy but the model said yes.

Now look at the columns one at a time. The left column contains every patient who truly has the disease. Sensitivity asks what fraction of that column the model got right. The right column contains every healthy patient. Specificity asks what fraction of that column the model got right. Each metric lives inside one column of truth, so neither one is disturbed by the other.

Step by step

  1. Build the confusion matrix on your testing data. Rows are the predictions, columns are the known truth.
  2. Name the four cells: true positives, false positives, false negatives, true negatives. Everything after this is arithmetic on those four counts.
  3. Compute sensitivity. Divide the true positives by the whole "truly positive" column, which is the true positives plus the false negatives.
  4. Compute specificity. Divide the true negatives by the whole "truly negative" column, which is the true negatives plus the false positives.
  5. Repeat for the other candidate model and compare. Decide which error hurts your users more, then favour the model that is stronger there.
  6. If the matrix has three or more categories, pick one category at a time. Treat that category as "positive" and everything else as "negative", then apply the same two formulas.

The second half of the video shows that last step on a three by three matrix. Josh predicts a person's favourite movie out of Troll 2, Gore Police and Cool as Ice. There is no single sensitivity for the whole table, so he works out a separate pair for each movie.

Formulas

Sensitivity
$$ \text{Sensitivity} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN} $$

Symbols: \(TP\) is the number of true positives, the positive cases the model called positive. \(FN\) is the number of false negatives, the positive cases the model missed. The denominator \(TP+FN\) is every case that is truly positive. What it tells you: the fraction of real positives the model correctly identified.

Specificity
$$ \text{Specificity} = \frac{TN}{TN + FP} $$

Symbols: \(TN\) is the number of true negatives, the negative cases the model called negative. \(FP\) is the number of false positives, the negative cases the model wrongly flagged. The denominator \(TN+FP\) is every case that is truly negative. What it tells you: the fraction of real negatives the model correctly identified.

Worked example from the video

Example

Josh reuses the heart disease confusion matrix from the confusion matrix video. Logistic regression gave 139 true positives, 32 false negatives, 112 true negatives and 20 false positives.

$$ \text{Sensitivity} = \frac{139}{139 + 32} = \frac{139}{171} = 0.81 $$

So 81% of the people with heart disease were correctly identified.

$$ \text{Specificity} = \frac{112}{112 + 20} = \frac{112}{132} = 0.85 $$

So 85% of the people without heart disease were correctly identified. Doing the same arithmetic on the random forest matrix gives a sensitivity of 0.83 and a specificity of 0.83.

The random forest is slightly better at finding patients who have heart disease. Logistic regression is slightly better at clearing patients who do not. Choose logistic regression if the second job matters more, and the random forest if the first job matters more.

For the three by three movie matrix, Troll 2 has 12 true positives and \(112+83=195\) false negatives, so sensitivity is \(12/207 = 0.06\). It has \(23+77+92+17=209\) true negatives and \(102+93=195\) false positives, so specificity is \(209/404 = 0.52\). Gore Police works out at 0.11 and 0.52, and Cool as Ice follows the same steps.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out Sensitivity and specificity are easy to swap when you are nervous. Anchor them to the column of truth: sensitivity is scored among the people who really are positive, specificity among the people who really are negative.
Watch out A high sensitivity on its own means nothing. A model that calls everyone positive has perfect sensitivity and zero specificity. Always report and read the pair together.
Watch out With three or more categories there is no single sensitivity for the table. You get one value per category, and Josh's movie example shows they can differ a lot between categories.
Watch out The row and column convention matters. Josh puts predictions on the rows and truth on the columns. Some tools do the opposite, so check the layout before you plug numbers into the formulas.

If they ask you in an interview

What is the difference between sensitivity and specificity?
Sensitivity is true positives divided by true positives plus false negatives. It measures how much of the truly positive group the model found. Specificity is true negatives divided by true negatives plus false positives, so it measures how much of the truly negative group the model found. Each one is calculated inside a single column of the confusion matrix, so improving one does not automatically change the other.
Two models score about the same. How do you pick one?
I look at which kind of mistake costs more. In the heart disease example the random forest had slightly higher sensitivity and logistic regression slightly higher specificity. If missing a sick patient is the worse outcome, I take the higher sensitivity. If a false alarm leads to expensive or risky follow up, I take the higher specificity.
How do these metrics work with more than two classes?
You compute them one class at a time. You treat the class of interest as positive and everything else as negative, so the matrix collapses into four regions for that class. In the movie example Troll 2 came out at 0.06 sensitivity and 0.52 specificity, while Gore Police was 0.11 and 0.52. With four classes you would report four pairs.
Can a model have high specificity and still be useless?
Yes, and that happens often with rare events. If only one percent of cases are positive, a model that predicts negative for everyone gets nearly perfect specificity while its sensitivity is zero. That is why I always look at both numbers next to the raw confusion matrix counts.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • The confusion matrix puts predictions on the rows and the known truth on the columns.
  • Sensitivity is the true positives divided by the true positives plus the false negatives.
  • Specificity is the true negatives divided by the true negatives plus the false positives.
  • For the heart disease data, logistic regression scored 0.81 sensitivity and 0.85 specificity.
  • The random forest scored 0.83 on both, so it found more sick patients and cleared fewer healthy ones.
  • Pick the model whose stronger metric matches the mistake you care about avoiding.
  • For three or more categories, calculate a separate sensitivity and specificity for every category.
