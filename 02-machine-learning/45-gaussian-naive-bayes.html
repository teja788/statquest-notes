Gaussian Naive Bayes

▶ Watch on YouTube

Gaussian Naive Bayes classifies things when the measurements are continuous numbers, such as grams of popcorn per day. It fits a normal curve to each measurement in each class, and uses the height of that curve as the likelihood.

In one breath

Josh predicts who loves the 1990 movie Troll 2, using three daily habits: popcorn, soda pop and candy. For each class he fits a Gaussian curve to each measurement, using its mean and standard deviation. To score a new person, he starts with an initial guess, then multiplies it by the height of each curve at that person's values. The numbers get very small, so he takes logs and adds them instead. The class with the larger log score wins.

The main idea

This quest builds on Multinomial Naive Bayes. There we counted discrete things, such as words in a message. Here the measurements are continuous numbers, such as grams of popcorn. So we use smooth curves instead of counts.

The fix is simple. For each class, and for each measurement, we take the mean and the standard deviation from the training data. Then we draw the Gaussian, or normal, curve with that mean and that standard deviation. For popcorn, the people who love Troll 2 have mean 24 and standard deviation 4. The people who do not love it have mean 4 and standard deviation 2. The method is named after these curves.

Now a new person arrives with a value for each measurement. The likelihood of that value is the height of the curve above it. In other words, you find the value on the x axis, go up to the curve, and read the y axis. We then multiply the three likelihoods together, as if the three habits were unrelated. That last part is the "naive" part of the name.

Step by step

  1. Collect the training data. Josh measured popcorn, soda pop and candy for 16 people, 8 who love Troll 2 and 8 who do not.
  2. For every class and every measurement, compute the mean and the standard deviation. Draw the matching Gaussian curve. That gives six curves in this example.
  3. Take the new person: 20 g of popcorn, 500 ml of soda pop and 25 g of candy each day.
  4. Make an initial guess for each class. Any probability is allowed, but a common choice comes from the training data. Here 8 of the 16 people love Troll 2, so both guesses are 0.5.
  5. Score one class. Multiply its initial guess by the three likelihoods, read as curve heights for that class.
  6. Take the natural log of everything. The multiplication becomes a sum of logs, which protects you from underflow.
  7. Repeat for the other class, then pick the class with the larger log score.

Formulas

Score for one class
$$ \text{score}(C) \;=\; P(C)\times L(x_1 \mid C)\times L(x_2 \mid C)\times L(x_3 \mid C) $$

Symbols: \(C\) is one class, here "loves Troll 2" or "does not love Troll 2". \(P(C)\) is the initial guess for that class, called the prior probability. \(x_1, x_2, x_3\) are the new person's popcorn, soda pop and candy values. \(L(x \mid C)\) is the likelihood of that value under the class \(C\), which is the height of that class's curve. What it tells you: the score of a class is its initial guess, adjusted by how well each new measurement fits that class.

The Gaussian curve height
$$ L(x \mid C) \;=\; \frac{1}{\sigma_C\sqrt{2\pi}}\;e^{-\frac{(x-\mu_C)^2}{2\sigma_C^2}} $$

Symbols: \(x\) is the new measured value. \(\mu_C\) is the mean of that measurement in class \(C\), for example 24 grams of popcorn for the people who love Troll 2. \(\sigma_C\) is the standard deviation of that measurement in the same class, for example 4. \(e\) is Euler's number, about 2.72, and \(\pi\) is about 3.14. What it tells you: this is the curve Josh draws, so it gives the exact number he reads off the y axis.

Log score
$$ \log\big(\text{score}(C)\big) \;=\; \log P(C) + \log L(x_1 \mid C) + \log L(x_2 \mid C) + \log L(x_3 \mid C) $$

Symbols: \(\log\) is the natural log, log base \(e\), the usual choice in statistics and machine learning. The other symbols are the same as above. What it tells you: the log turns the product into a sum, so tiny numbers become moderate negative numbers instead of underflowing to zero.

Worked example from the video

Example

A new person eats 20 g of popcorn, drinks 500 ml of soda pop and eats 25 g of candy each day. Both initial guesses are 0.5, because 8 of the 16 training people love Troll 2.

For the class "loves Troll 2", the likelihood for popcorn is 0.06, the likelihood for soda pop is 0.004, and the likelihood for candy is a very, very small number. Multiplying them would risk underflow, so we take logs and add:

$$ \log\big(\text{score}(\text{Loves})\big) = -0.69 + (-2.8) + (-5.5) + (-115) = -124 $$

Now we do exactly the same for the class "does not love Troll 2": the same initial guess of 0.5, the three likelihoods read from that class's curves, and the same sum of logs. That score comes out at −48.

$$ -48 \;>\; -124 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{classify as: does not love Troll 2} $$

The raw data almost suggests the opposite. This person eats far more popcorn than the average person who does not love Troll 2, and drinks as much soda pop as the average person who does love it. But they eat far more candy than the people who love Troll 2, so the candy log likelihood, −115, pulls the score down hard. Candy alone decides the answer here.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out A likelihood is not a probability. It is the height of the curve at one point, so it can be larger than 1. Probability is the area under a region of the curve, which is a different quantity.
Watch out Do not multiply the raw likelihoods in code. Every computer has a limit to how close a number can get to zero. Below that limit you hit underflow and the result becomes meaningless. Take logs and add them.
Watch out Compare the signed values carefully. Both log scores are negative, and −48 is larger than −124. The class with the score closest to zero is the winner.
Watch out One measurement can dominate the sum, as candy does here. That may be fine, or it may mean the other measurements add nothing. Use cross validation to decide which measurements to keep.

If they ask you in an interview

How does Gaussian Naive Bayes differ from Multinomial Naive Bayes?
Multinomial Naive Bayes works with counts of discrete things, such as words in a message. Gaussian Naive Bayes works with continuous measurements, such as grams of candy per day. Instead of counting, we fit a normal curve to each measurement in each class, using its mean and standard deviation. The likelihood is then the height of that curve at the new value.
Why do we take logs of the scores?
The score is a product of a prior and several likelihoods, and those likelihoods can be extremely small. Multiplying them can push the result below the smallest number the computer can represent, which is underflow. The log turns the product into a sum of moderate negative numbers. The log is an increasing function, so the ranking of the classes does not change.
Where do the prior probabilities come from?
The prior is just an initial guess about each class before we look at the new measurements. It can be any probability you want. A common choice is the proportion of each class in the training data. In this example 8 of 16 people love Troll 2, so both priors are 0.5.
How would you decide which measurements to use?
I would test the subsets with cross validation and compare the classification results. In this example candy carries most of the signal, so a model with candy alone might do as well as the full model.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • Gaussian Naive Bayes is for continuous measurements, not counts.
  • For each class and each measurement, fit a normal curve with the training mean and standard deviation.
  • The likelihood of a new value is the height of that curve above the value.
  • Start each class with an initial guess, the prior, often the class proportion in the training data.
  • The score is the prior times the likelihoods, one per measurement.
  • Take natural logs and add them, so tiny numbers do not underflow.
  • The class with the larger log score wins; here −48 beat −124.
  • One strong measurement can decide everything, so use cross validation to choose your measurements.
