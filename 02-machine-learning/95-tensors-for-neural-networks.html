Tensors for Neural Networks

▶ Watch on YouTube Also in: Neural Networks / Deep Learning (video 26)

This video explains what a tensor means in machine learning. It shows why neural networks need tensors once the data gets large, and what tensors add beyond ordinary arrays.

In one breath

The word "tensor" means one thing in math and physics and another in machine learning. The video covers only the machine learning meaning. Real neural networks do a huge amount of math, because inputs like colour images and video contain hundreds of thousands of numbers. A tensor stores the input data and the weights and biases, in shapes from a single value up to many dimensions. On the surface this is just a new name for scalars, arrays and matrices. The real difference is that tensors are built to run fast on GPUs and TPUs, and they work out the derivatives for backpropagation automatically.

The main idea

Josh starts with a warning. People in math and physics define a tensor one way, and people in machine learning define it another way. This video only uses the machine learning meaning, where tensors are used together with neural networks.

For someone building a neural network, a tensor is simply a place to store numbers. It holds the input data, and it also holds the weights and biases of the network. A weight is a number that scales a value inside the network, and a bias is a number that shifts it. Seen this way, tensors can seem boring, because they mostly give new names to things programmers already use.

A single value, which most languages call a scalar, becomes a zero-dimensional tensor. A list of values, called an array, becomes a one-dimensional tensor. A single image, called a matrix, becomes a two-dimensional tensor. Video, which most languages store as a multi-dimensional array (an ND array in Python), becomes an N-dimensional tensor.

So what is the big deal? Unlike plain arrays and matrices, tensors were designed to use hardware acceleration. This means the math done on the data runs quickly on special chips. Tensors also handle backpropagation for you through automatic differentiation.

Step by step

  1. Josh recalls a very simple network from earlier videos. It takes one input, drug dosage, and predicts one output, efficacy. Even with three training points, fitting the squiggle needed a lot of math.
  2. A fancier network took two flower measurements as inputs and had three outputs, one per iris species. It needed even more math to make its predictions.
  3. A convolutional neural network took a 6 by 6 black and white image, 36 pixels in all, and predicted whether it showed an X or an O. That took a whole lot of math too.
  4. Real networks are much bigger. A typical input image is 256 by 256 pixels and in colour, split into red, green and blue channels. The network treats each channel separately, so the pixel count triples.
  5. One image is not enough, because training uses a ton of images. Video is a series of images, so it means even more math. Handling all this math is what tensors were designed for.
  6. Josh then shows that tensors store inputs and weights and biases. He renames scalar, array, matrix and ND array as 0-D, 1-D, 2-D and N-D tensors.
  7. Next comes the real benefit. Tensors and their math are sped up by GPUs (graphics processing units). There are also TPUs (tensor processing units), chips built specifically to work with tensors.
  8. Finally, training finds the best weights and biases with backpropagation, which needs many derivatives and the chain rule. Tensors do this for you with automatic differentiation, so you can build a very fancy network without deriving anything by hand.

Formulas

The video has no formal formula. Its only calculation is the pixel count that shows why the math becomes so large.

Pixel count of one input image
$$ 6 \times 6 = 36 $$ $$ 256 \times 256 = 65{,}536 $$ $$ 3 \times 65{,}536 = 196{,}608 $$

Symbols: the first two numbers in each product are the image height and width in pixels; the 3 is the number of colour channels (red, green, blue), which the network treats separately. What it tells you: moving from the toy 6 by 6 image to a realistic colour image multiplies the numbers to process by more than five thousand, and that is for a single image.

Worked example from the video

Example

Start with the X-or-O network. Its input is a 6 by 6 black and white image, so \(6 \times 6 = 36\) pixels. As a tensor, this single image is a two-dimensional tensor.

Now make it realistic. A 256 by 256 image has \(256 \times 256 = 65{,}536\) pixels. In colour it has three channels, so the network must do math on \(3 \times 65{,}536 = 196{,}608\) pixel values.

Training needs a ton of such images, and video is a series of frames, each with three colour channels. That input is stored as an N-dimensional tensor. Tensors make this practical in two ways. GPUs or TPUs run the math quickly, and automatic differentiation computes the derivatives that backpropagation needs to update the weights and biases.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out "Tensor" has two meanings. The math and physics meaning is different from the machine learning meaning, and this video says nothing about the math and physics one. Do not mix the two in an answer.
Watch out Calling a tensor "just an N-dimensional array" misses the point. The shapes are the same, but tensors are designed for hardware acceleration and automatic differentiation.
Watch out Tensors do not only hold the input data. They also hold the weights and biases that make up the network.
Watch out Colour matters for size. Because each red, green and blue channel is handled separately, a colour image has three times as many pixel values as the same image in black and white.

If they ask you in an interview

What is a tensor in the context of neural networks?
It is the structure that stores the network's input data and its weights and biases. It can be a single value, a list, a matrix, or something with many dimensions, like video. What makes it more than an array is that it is built to run fast on GPUs and TPUs and to compute derivatives automatically.
How does a tensor differ from an ordinary ND array?
In shape, they are the same idea, so a 2-D tensor is just a matrix. The difference is in what tensors were designed for. They let the math run quickly with hardware acceleration, and they take care of backpropagation through automatic differentiation.
Why do neural networks need hardware acceleration?
Real inputs are large. One 256 by 256 colour image already has 196,608 pixel values, and training uses many images, or even video. GPUs, and TPUs built specifically for tensors, let all that math finish in a reasonable amount of time.
What does automatic differentiation give you?
Backpropagation needs many derivatives and a lot of chain rule to find the best weights and biases. With automatic differentiation, the tensors work out those derivatives for you. That means you can build a very complex network without deriving the gradients by hand.
Is the machine learning tensor the same as the physics tensor?
No. The math and physics communities define the word one way, and the machine learning community defines it another way. In machine learning, it means the data container used with neural networks, with built-in speed and automatic differentiation.
