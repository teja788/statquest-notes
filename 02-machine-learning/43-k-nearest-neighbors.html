K-nearest neighbors

▶ Watch on YouTube

K-nearest neighbors is a very simple way to classify a new data point. You look at the points nearest to it, and you let them vote.

In one breath

Start with data whose categories you already know, and plot it. Add the new point whose category you do not know. Look at its K closest neighbors. Give the new point the category that most of them have. K is your choice, so you test a few values.

The main idea

Josh uses cells from an intestinal tumor. Each cell was measured, and the measurements were reduced to a plot with PCA. Cells of the same type sit close together, so the plot shows clear clusters of green, red and orange cells.

Now a new cell arrives from another tumor. Nobody sorted those cells, so its type is unknown. The idea is simple: find the cells it sits nearest to, and copy their label.

The letter K is the number of neighbors you look at. If K equals 1, only the closest cell decides. If K equals 11, the 11 closest cells vote, and the most common category wins.

Step by step

  1. Start with data whose categories you already know. Here it is cells from an intestinal tumor, clustered with PCA.
  2. Add the new point with the unknown category to the same plot.
  3. Find its K nearest neighbors, that is, the K known points closest to it.
  4. Count the categories of those neighbors and give the new point the category with the most votes.
  5. Repeat for every unknown point. Nothing is fitted in advance, so each new point is handled on its own.

The same steps work on a heat map clustered with hierarchical clustering. A new cell inside the light blue block is called light blue, whether K is 1 or 5. Near the edge with K equal to 11, seven neighbors are light blue and four light green, so light blue wins.

Formulas

The video shows no equation. It uses two simple rules that are worth writing down.

Distance to a neighbor
$$ d(x, x_i) = \sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{p} \left( x_j - x_{ij} \right)^2} $$

Symbols: \(x\) is the new point with the unknown category. \(x_i\) is training point \(i\), whose category you know. \(p\) is the number of coordinates; on Josh's PCA plot \(p = 2\). What it tells you: the K smallest distances give you the K nearest neighbors.

The vote
$$ \hat{y} = \arg\max_{c} \sum_{i \in N_K(x)} \mathbf{1}(y_i = c) $$

Symbols: \(\hat{y}\) is the category you assign. \(c\) is one possible category, such as red. \(N_K(x)\) is the set of K nearest neighbors. \(\mathbf{1}(y_i = c)\) is 1 when neighbor \(i\) is category \(c\), so the sum counts votes. What it tells you: pick the category with the largest count.

Worked example from the video

Example

The new cell first lands deep inside the green cluster. With K equal to 1, the closest cell is green, so the answer is green. With K equal to 11, all 11 closest cells are green, so it is still green.

Josh then moves the new cell to a harder spot, about halfway between the green and the red cells. With K equal to 11, the neighbors are mixed: 7 are red, 3 are orange and 1 is green. Red has the most votes, so the cell is called red.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out There is no physical or biological rule that gives the best K. Hide part of the training data, classify it as if it were unknown, and check the answers against the labels you have.
Watch out Both ends of the scale hurt. With K equal to 1 or 2, one outlier can decide the answer. With a very large K, a category with few samples is always outvoted.
Watch out Ties happen when a point sits right between two categories. An odd K avoids most ties. If a tie still happens, flip a coin or leave the point unassigned.

If they ask you in an interview

How does K-nearest neighbors work?
You keep the labelled data as it is. For a new point, you measure the distance to every labelled point and take the K closest. Those neighbors vote, and the top category becomes the label. Nothing is fitted beforehand.
How do you choose K?
There is no formula for it, so I tune it. I hold out part of the training data, pretend its labels are unknown, classify it, and compare with the true labels. I repeat for a few values of K and keep the best. I also prefer an odd K, because it avoids most ties.
What goes wrong if K is too small or too large?
A very small K is sensitive to noise, so one unusual point nearby can flip the answer. A very large K smooths everything out. Then a rare category with few samples is outvoted every time.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • K-nearest neighbors classifies a new point by looking at the labelled points nearest to it.
  • The labelled data used for the clustering is called the training data.
  • K is the number of neighbors you let vote, and when K is 1 the closest point decides.
  • When the neighbors disagree, the category with the most votes wins.
  • In Josh's example, 7 red, 3 orange and 1 green neighbor give the answer red.
  • No rule gives the best K, so you test values on held-out training data.
  • Small K is noisy, large K hides small categories, and an odd K avoids most ties.
