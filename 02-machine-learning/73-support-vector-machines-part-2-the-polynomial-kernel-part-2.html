Support Vector Machines Part 2: The Polynomial Kernel (Part 2 of 3)

▶ Watch on YouTube Also in: —

This video explains the two parameters of the polynomial kernel and shows how the kernel calculates high-dimensional relationships between observations.

In one breath

The polynomial kernel is \((a \times b + r)^d\), where a and b are two observations. Expanded, it becomes a dot product: multiply matching terms, then add. The terms are each point's coordinates in a higher dimension. With r = 1/2 and d = 2, they are the dosage and the dosage squared. So you never move the data; you just plug each pair into the kernel.

The main idea

Josh returns to the drug dosage data from Part 1. The drug cures patients only when the dosage is just right. On one axis the groups overlap too much. So there is no good support vector classifier, the boundary that separates the two classes.

In Part 1, each point got a y-axis value equal to its dosage squared, so a line could split the groups. This video shows how the polynomial kernel, a formula applied to each pair of points, gives that squared coordinate.

Step by step

  1. Set r = 1/2 and d = 2 in the kernel \((a \times b + r)^d\).
  2. Expand the square into two terms, multiply them out, and combine like terms.
  3. Rewrite the result as a dot product of two lists of three terms.
  4. Read the coordinates: first terms are the x-axis, second terms the y-axis, third terms the z-axis.
  5. Repeat with r = 1 and d = 2. The x-axis becomes \(\sqrt{2}\) times the dosage, and the y-axis stays the same.
  6. Use the kernel as a shortcut. The classifier needs only the dot product for each pair of points, and the kernel equals it.

Formulas

Polynomial kernel
$$ K(a, b) = (a \times b + r)^d $$

Symbols: \(a\) and \(b\) are two observations, here two dosages. \(r\) determines the coefficient of the polynomial. \(d\) sets the degree of the polynomial. \(K(a,b)\) is their high-dimensional relationship. What it tells you: one quick calculation gives the pair's high-dimensional relationship.

Expansion with r = 1/2, d = 2
$$ \left(ab + \tfrac12\right)^2 = \left(ab + \tfrac12\right)\left(ab + \tfrac12\right) = ab + a^2b^2 + \tfrac14 $$ $$ = \left(a,\ a^2,\ \tfrac12\right) \cdot \left(b,\ b^2,\ \tfrac12\right) $$

Symbols: \(\cdot\) is the dot product: multiply matching terms and add. \(a, a^2\) (and \(b, b^2\)) are the new x and y coordinates. \(\tfrac12\) is a z coordinate, equal for every point. What it tells you: each point moves to (dosage, dosage²), the trick from Part 1.

Expansion with r = 1, d = 2
$$ (ab + 1)^2 = 2ab + a^2b^2 + 1 = \left(\sqrt2\,a,\ a^2,\ 1\right) \cdot \left(\sqrt2\,b,\ b^2,\ 1\right) $$

Symbols: \(\sqrt2\,a\) is the new x, \(a^2\) the new y, and 1 a constant z. What it tells you: with r = 1, the points move on the x-axis by a factor of \(\sqrt2\).

Worked example from the video

Example Josh uses the kernel with r = 1/2 and d = 2 on two dosages, 9 and 14. He plugs them straight in: $$ \left(9 \times 14 + \tfrac12\right)^2 = (126.5)^2 = 16{,}002.25 $$ This is one of the two-dimensional relationships the classifier needs, yet the data was never transformed. As a check, the dot product gives the same value: \(9 \times 14 + 9^2 \times 14^2 + \tfrac14 = 126 + 15{,}876 + 0.25 = 16{,}002.25\).

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out The kernel does not move the data. It only gives the relationship between a pair of points, as if they were in the higher dimension.
Watch out The third term of the dot product is a constant, 1/2 or 1. It is the same for all points, so you can ignore the z-axis.
Watch out Different r values give different coordinates. Choose r and d with cross-validation, not by guessing.

If they ask you in an interview

What is the polynomial kernel, and what do its parameters do?
It is \((a \times b + r)^d\), where a and b are two observations. The parameter r sets the coefficient of the polynomial, and d sets its degree.
How does the polynomial kernel relate to a higher-dimensional space?
Expanded, the kernel becomes a dot product. Its terms are each point's new coordinates. For r = 1/2 and d = 2, they are the dosage and the dosage squared.
Why don't we need to transform the data explicitly?
The classifier only needs the dot product for each pair of points. The kernel equals that dot product. So I plug each pair into the kernel and never compute the new coordinates.
