XGBoost Part 1 (of 4): Regression

▶ Watch on YouTube

XGBoost is gradient boost with its own special regression tree. This video shows how that tree is grown, how it is pruned, and how its leaves turn into predictions.

In one breath

XGBoost starts with a flat prediction of 0.5 and computes the residuals. It fits its own kind of tree to them, scoring each node with a similarity score. It picks the split with the largest gain, and prunes branches whose gain is below a number called gamma. A regularization parameter called lambda shrinks the scores and outputs. The prediction then moves by each leaf's output times a learning rate called eta.

The main idea

Josh uses a very small training set. On the x-axis is drug dosage. On the y-axis is drug effectiveness. Two observations have large positive effectiveness, so the drug helped. Two have large negative effectiveness, so the drug did more harm than good.

The first step is an initial prediction. It can be any number, but the default in XGBoost is 0.5, for both regression and classification. That is a flat horizontal line through the data. The residuals are the gaps between the observed values and this line. Here they are −10.5, 6.5, 7.5 and −7.5.

Like ordinary gradient boost, XGBoost now fits a regression tree to the residuals. But it does not use an off-the-shelf regression tree. It uses its own tree, built from a quantity called the similarity score.

The tree starts as one leaf holding all four residuals. The similarity score squares the sum of the residuals, not each residual on its own. So residuals with opposite signs cancel before the squaring. A node of mixed residuals gets a small score, and a node of similar residuals gets a large score.

To judge a split, we compare the two child scores with the parent score. That difference is the gain. A large gain means the split put similar residuals together.

Step by step

  1. Make the initial prediction. The default is 0.5 for every observation. Compute the residual for each observation.
  2. Put all residuals in one leaf and compute its similarity score. With lambda equal to 0, the four residuals give a score of 4.
  3. Try the first threshold. Take the average dosage of the two leftmost points, which is 15, and split on dosage below 15.
  4. Compute the similarity score of each child leaf, then compute the gain as left plus right minus parent.
  5. Slide the threshold to the next midpoint, 22.5, then to the next, 30. Compute the gain each time.
  6. Keep the threshold with the largest gain. Here dosage below 15 wins with a gain of 120.33.
  7. Repeat the search inside each child node with more than one residual. Josh stops at two levels, but the default allows six.
  8. Prune from the bottom up. Subtract gamma from the gain of the lowest branch. If the answer is negative, remove the branch and check the next one up.
  9. Compute the output value of every surviving leaf, then update the predictions using the learning rate eta.

Formulas

Similarity score
$$ \text{Similarity} = \frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n} r_i\right)^2}{n + \lambda} $$

Symbols: \(r_i\) is the residual of observation \(i\) in this node, that is observed value minus current prediction. \(n\) is how many residuals landed in the node. \(\lambda\) (lambda) is the regularization parameter, and Josh uses its default of 0. What it tells you: the residuals are added before squaring, so similar residuals score high and mixed ones score low.

Gain of a split
$$ \text{Gain} = \text{Sim}_{\text{left}} + \text{Sim}_{\text{right}} - \text{Sim}_{\text{parent}} $$

Symbols: \(\text{Sim}_{\text{left}}\) and \(\text{Sim}_{\text{right}}\) are the similarity scores of the two child leaves. \(\text{Sim}_{\text{parent}}\) is the similarity score of the node before the split. What it tells you: how much better the two leaves group similar residuals than the parent. The threshold with the largest gain wins.

Pruning rule
$$ \text{Gain} - \gamma \;\begin{cases} < 0 & \text{remove the branch} \\ \ge 0 & \text{keep the branch} \end{cases} $$

Symbols: \(\gamma\) (gamma) is a tree complexity parameter that you choose. \(\text{Gain}\) is the gain of the branch being tested. What it tells you: a branch must earn at least gamma to stay. Start at the lowest branch, work upwards, and stop when a branch is kept.

Output value of a leaf
$$ \text{Output} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} r_i}{n + \lambda} $$

Symbols: the same \(r_i\), \(n\) and \(\lambda\) as above. What it tells you: it is the similarity score without the square. When \(\lambda = 0\) it is the average residual in the leaf. When \(\lambda > 0\) it is smaller, so one observation moves the prediction less.

New prediction
$$ \hat{y}_{\text{new}} = \hat{y}_{\text{old}} + \eta \cdot \text{Output} $$

Symbols: \(\hat{y}_{\text{old}}\) is the prediction before this tree, starting at 0.5. \(\eta\) (eta) is the learning rate, and its XGBoost default is 0.3. \(\text{Output}\) is the output value of the leaf the observation falls into. What it tells you: each tree moves the prediction only a small step.

Worked example from the video

Example

The four residuals after the initial prediction of 0.5 are −10.5, 6.5, 7.5 and −7.5, in order of dosage. Set \(\lambda = 0\) throughout.

Root. The sum is −4, so the similarity score is \((-4)^2 / 4 = 4\). Note that 7.5 and −7.5 cancelled, and 6.5 cancelled most of −10.5.

Threshold search. Each candidate threshold is the average of two neighbouring dosages.

  • Dosage < 15: left has one residual, \((-10.5)^2/1 = 110.25\); right has three, \(6.5^2/3 = 14.08\). Gain = 110.25 + 14.08 − 4 = 120.33.
  • Dosage < 22.5: gain = 8 + 0 − 4 = 4.
  • Dosage < 30: gain = 4.08 + 56.25 − 4 = 56.33.

Dosage < 15 has the largest gain, so it becomes the first branch.

Second level. The left leaf holds one residual, so it cannot split. The right node holds 6.5, 7.5 and −7.5, with similarity 14.08. Splitting at dosage < 22.5 gives 42.25 + 0 − 14.08 = 28.17. Splitting at dosage < 30 gives 98 + 56.25 − 14.08 = 140.17, so that threshold wins. Josh stops here because he capped the depth at two levels.

Pruning with gamma = 130. The lowest branch gives 140.17 − 130, which is positive, so it stays. The root gain of 120.33 is below 130, but the root stays because the branch below it stayed. With gamma = 150, 140.17 − 150 is negative, so that branch goes. Then 120.33 − 150 is also negative, so the root goes too. Only the initial prediction of 0.5 is left.

Effect of lambda = 1. The root score falls from 4 to \(16/5 = 3.2\), which is 80% of before. The left leaf falls from 110.25 to 55.12, which is 50%. The right node falls from 14.08 to 10.56, which is 75%. Nodes with fewer residuals shrink more. Both gains now fall below gamma = 130, so the whole tree would be pruned.

Output values with lambda = 0. Left leaf: −10.5. Middle leaf: \((6.5 + 7.5)/2 = 7\). Right leaf: −7.5. With lambda = 1, the left leaf output is \(-10.5/2 = -5.25\) instead.

New predictions with eta = 0.3. For dosage 10: \(0.5 + 0.3 \times (-10.5) = -2.65\). For dosage 20: \(0.5 + 0.3 \times 7 = 2.6\). Every new residual is smaller than the old one. XGBoost then builds the next tree on the new residuals.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out The numerator squares the sum of the residuals, not the sum of the squares. If you square each residual first, nothing cancels, and a mixed node no longer scores low.
Watch out Setting gamma to 0 does not turn pruning off. With lambda = 1, Josh splits the node holding 6.5 and 7.5. The scores are 65.3 at the parent and 21.12 and 28.12 at the leaves. The gain is −16.06, so gain minus gamma is still negative and the branch goes.
Watch out Lambda does not shrink every node by the same fraction. It is added to \(n\) in the denominator, so small nodes shrink most. A leaf with one residual lost half its score, while the root lost only a fifth.

If they ask you in an interview

How does an XGBoost tree differ from a normal regression tree?
An XGBoost tree scores each node with a similarity score. That is the squared sum of the residuals divided by the count plus lambda. It then picks the split with the largest gain, where the two children beat the parent by the most. It also prunes with gamma after the tree is built.
What do lambda and gamma actually do?
Lambda is a regularization term in the denominator of the similarity score and the output value. It shrinks both, and it shrinks small leaves the most. Gamma is a threshold used only for pruning: a branch is removed when its gain minus gamma is negative. A larger lambda gives smaller gains, so it also leads to more pruning.
Why does the similarity score add the residuals before squaring them?
We want to know whether the residuals in a node point the same way. Mixed positive and negative values cancel, so the score is small. Similar residuals, or a single one, do not cancel, so the score is large. A split that groups similar residuals therefore earns a high gain.
How does XGBoost turn a tree into a prediction?
Each leaf gets an output value: the sum of its residuals over the count plus lambda. With lambda at zero, that is the average residual in the leaf. The new prediction is the old one plus eta times the output. Eta is the learning rate, and its default is 0.3.
Does setting gamma to zero disable pruning?
No. A gain can be negative when the two child leaves score less than the parent. With gamma at zero, gain minus gamma is still negative, so the branch is removed. Josh shows this with lambda = 1 and a gain of −16.06.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • XGBoost begins with a flat prediction of 0.5 and fits trees to the residuals.
  • Each node's similarity score is the squared sum of its residuals over the count plus lambda, so mixed residuals score low.
  • Gain is the two child scores minus the parent score, and the threshold with the biggest gain wins.
  • Pruning works bottom up: remove a branch when its gain minus gamma is negative.
  • Lambda shrinks similarity scores and output values, which leads to more pruning.
  • Each leaf outputs the sum of its residuals over the count plus lambda.
  • The prediction moves by eta times that output, with eta defaulting to 0.3.
