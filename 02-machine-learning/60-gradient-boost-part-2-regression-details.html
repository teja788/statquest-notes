Gradient Boost Part 2 (of 4): Regression Details

▶ Watch on YouTube

This video walks through the original gradient boost algorithm for regression, line by line. Part 1 gave the intuition. This part shows the exact maths behind every symbol, on a data set of three people.

In one breath

Gradient boost starts with one leaf that predicts the average weight. It then repeats four parts: compute residuals, fit a tree, find each leaf's output, and update the predictions. The loss is half the squared residual, because its derivative is simply the negative residual. With that loss, each leaf's output is the average of the residuals in it. After M trees, the last model is the final answer.

The main idea

Josh uses a data set with three people. For each person we know height, favourite colour, and gender, and we want to predict weight. The three weights are 88, 76, and 56. That is small enough to do every calculation by hand.

The algorithm needs two inputs. The first is the data: each row of measurements is \(x_i\) and each measured weight is \(y_i\), with \(i\) running from 1 to \(n\). The second is a differentiable loss function, which is just a rule that scores how badly we predict weight. A function is differentiable when we can take its derivative, and gradient boost takes derivatives constantly.

The chosen loss is half of the squared residual. A residual is the observed value minus the predicted value. The one half looks odd, but it does no harm. It shrinks every score by the same factor, so the best fit stays the best. In the video, one line has a sum of squared residuals of 0.26 and another has 6.43. Halving both keeps 0.26 as the better fit.

The real reason for the one half is convenience. When you differentiate the loss, the 2 from the power cancels the 1/2. What remains is the negative residual. So the gradient in gradient boost is just a residual with a minus sign.

Step by step

  1. Initialise with a constant. Find the single number that minimises the total loss over all samples. With this loss that number is the average weight, so \(F_0(x)=73.3\). The model is one leaf that predicts 73.3 for everybody.
  2. Part A: compute pseudo residuals. For every sample, take the negative derivative of the loss at the current prediction. That equals observed minus predicted. For the first tree the three values are 14.7, 2.7, and -17.3.
  3. Part B: fit a regression tree. Use height, favourite colour, and gender to predict those residuals, not the weights. Then label the leaves as terminal regions \(R_{j,m}\), where \(m\) is the tree number and \(j\) is the leaf number.
  4. Part C: compute one output value per leaf. For each leaf, find the \(\gamma\) that minimises the loss of the samples in that leaf, measured on top of the previous prediction. Here that gives -17.3 for one leaf and 8.7 for the other.
  5. Part D: update every prediction. Take the previous prediction and add the learning rate times the output value of the leaf the sample falls into. With a learning rate of 0.1 the three predictions move to 74.2, 74.2, and 71.6.
  6. Repeat, then stop. Set \(m=2\) and run A to D again on the new residuals. After \(M\) trees, \(F_M(x)\) is the final model that you use on new data.

Formulas

Loss function
$$ L\big(y_i, F(x_i)\big) = \tfrac{1}{2}\big(y_i - F(x_i)\big)^2 $$

Symbols: \(y_i\) is the observed weight of person \(i\); \(F(x_i)\) is the predicted weight for that person's row of measurements; the bracket is the residual. What it tells you: the score for one sample is half its squared residual, so large misses cost much more than small ones.

Derivative of the loss
$$ \frac{\partial L}{\partial F(x_i)} = 2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}\big(y_i - F(x_i)\big) \cdot (-1) $$

Bring the square down to the front by the chain rule, then multiply by the derivative of the inside, which is -1.

$$ \frac{\partial L}{\partial F(x_i)} = -\big(y_i - F(x_i)\big) $$

The 2 and the 1/2 cancel, so the derivative is exactly the negative residual. What it tells you: this is why the 1/2 is in the loss, and why the gradient in gradient boost is a residual in disguise.

Step 1 — the initial constant
$$ F_0(x) = \arg\min_{\gamma} \sum_{i=1}^{n} L\big(y_i, \gamma\big) $$

Symbols: \(\gamma\) (gamma) is a single predicted value used for everyone; \(\arg\min_\gamma\) means "the value of \(\gamma\) that makes the sum smallest"; \(n\) is the number of samples.

$$ \sum_{i=1}^{n} -\big(y_i - \gamma\big) = 0 $$

Plug in the derivative of each term and set the sum to zero, because the minimum is where the slope is flat. Josh solves it directly, since the maths is easy enough to skip gradient descent.

$$ \gamma = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} y_i $$

Solve for \(\gamma\) and the answer is the average of the observed weights. What it tells you: with this loss, the best constant first guess is always the mean.

Step 2A — pseudo residuals
$$ r_{i,m} = -\left[\frac{\partial L\big(y_i, F(x_i)\big)}{\partial F(x_i)}\right]_{F = F_{m-1}} = y_i - F_{m-1}(x_i) $$

Symbols: \(r_{i,m}\) is the pseudo residual for sample \(i\) in tree \(m\); \(F_{m-1}\) is the model built so far; the minus sign in front flips the derivative. What it tells you: with this loss the pseudo residual is an ordinary residual, but with another loss it would only resemble one, which is why the word "pseudo" is used.

Step 2C — output value of a leaf
$$ \gamma_{j,m} = \arg\min_{\gamma} \sum_{x_i \in R_{j,m}} L\big(y_i,\; F_{m-1}(x_i) + \gamma\big) $$

Symbols: \(R_{j,m}\) is leaf \(j\) of tree \(m\); \(x_i \in R_{j,m}\) means only the samples that land in that leaf are summed; \(\gamma_{j,m}\) is the number the leaf will output.

$$ \sum_{x_i \in R_{j,m}} -\big(y_i - F_{m-1}(x_i) - \gamma\big) = 0 $$

Differentiate with respect to \(\gamma\) and set the result to zero, exactly as in Step 1.

$$ \gamma_{j,m} = \frac{1}{|R_{j,m}|}\sum_{x_i \in R_{j,m}} r_{i,m} $$

Solving gives the average of the residuals in that leaf. What it tells you: two differences from Step 1 matter here. The previous prediction is now inside the loss, and only the samples in the leaf are counted.

Step 2D — update the model
$$ F_m(x) = F_{m-1}(x) + \nu \sum_{j=1}^{J_m} \gamma_{j,m}\, \mathbf{1}\big(x \in R_{j,m}\big) $$

Symbols: \(\nu\) (nu) is the learning rate, a number between 0 and 1; \(J_m\) is the number of leaves in tree \(m\); the indicator \(\mathbf{1}(\cdot)\) equals 1 when the sample falls in that leaf and 0 otherwise. What it tells you: the new model is the old model plus a small, scaled step. The sum exists only in case a sample can be found in more than one leaf.

Worked example from the video

Example

The three observed weights are 88, 76, and 56. Step 1 gives \(F_0(x) = (88+76+56)/3 = 73.3\), so one leaf predicts 73.3 for everyone.

Part A subtracts 73.3 from each weight. That gives residuals of 14.7, 2.7, and -17.3.

Part B fits a stump that splits on height at 1.55. Sample \(x_3\) goes to the left leaf, \(R_{1,1}\). Samples \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) go to the right leaf, \(R_{2,1}\).

Part C solves each leaf. For the left leaf only \(x_3\) counts, so \(\tfrac{1}{2}(56 - 73.3 - \gamma)^2\) is minimised at \(\gamma_{1,1} = -17.3\). For the right leaf the derivative gives \(-(14.7-\gamma)-(2.7-\gamma)=0\), so \(2\gamma = 17.4\) and \(\gamma_{2,1} = 8.7\), the average of the two residuals.

Part D uses a learning rate of 0.1. For \(x_1\): \(73.3 + 0.1 \times 8.7 = 74.2\), which is closer to the observed 88 than 73.3 was. Sample \(x_2\) also becomes 74.2, and \(x_3\) becomes \(73.3 + 0.1 \times (-17.3) = 71.6\).

Josh then stops at \(M = 2\) to save time. For a new person whose two leaf outputs are -17.3 and -15.6, the final prediction is \(73.3 + 0.1 \times (-17.3) + 0.1 \times (-15.6) = 70\) kg.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out The stumps here are only a teaching device. Gradient boost normally uses trees that are larger than stumps. Josh uses stumps because three samples cannot fill more than two leaves, and he needs one leaf with two samples in it.
Watch out "The output value is the average of the residuals" is true only for this loss function. Change the loss and Step 2C must be solved again, and the answer is usually not an average.
Watch out Step 2C is not the same minimisation as Step 1. It adds \(\gamma\) on top of the previous prediction \(F_{m-1}(x_i)\), and it sums over the samples in one leaf only.
Watch out The learning rate applies to every tree, including the first one. It never applies to the initial leaf \(F_0(x)\), which enters the prediction at full strength.

If they ask you in an interview

Why is the loss function half the squared residual, and not just the squared residual?
Both rank fits the same way, so the one half changes nothing about which model wins. It is there purely for convenience. When you differentiate it, the 2 from the power cancels the 1/2, and the derivative becomes exactly the negative residual. Gradient boost takes that derivative constantly, so the cleaner form saves a lot of clutter.
What exactly is the "gradient" in gradient boost?
It is the derivative of the loss with respect to the current prediction, evaluated at the model built so far. For squared error loss that derivative is minus the residual, so the negative gradient is just the residual. Each new tree is fitted to those negative gradients, which is why the method generalises to any differentiable loss.
Why are they called pseudo residuals rather than residuals?
Because they are defined as negative gradients, not as observed minus predicted. With the half squared error loss the two definitions happen to coincide, so you get ordinary residuals. With a different loss you get something that behaves like a residual but is not one, and the name pseudo residual covers both cases.
How is a leaf's output value computed when several samples land in it?
You solve a small minimisation for that leaf alone. You look for the constant \(\gamma\) that minimises the total loss of those samples when \(\gamma\) is added to their current predictions. With squared error loss the solution is the average of the residuals in the leaf. In the video, 14.7 and 2.7 give an output of 8.7.
What does the learning rate do, and why use a small one?
The learning rate scales the contribution of every tree before it is added to the model. A small value means each tree moves the prediction only a little. The model then takes many small steps in the right direction. As Josh says, this improves accuracy in the long run, at the cost of needing more trees.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • The inputs are the data \((x_i, y_i)\) and a differentiable loss, here half the squared residual.
  • Step 1 initialises the model with the constant that minimises the total loss, which is the average weight, 73.3.
  • Step 2A computes pseudo residuals as the negative gradient, which for this loss equals observed minus predicted.
  • Step 2B fits a regression tree to those residuals and labels its leaves as terminal regions.
  • Step 2C gives each leaf one output value, which for this loss is the average of the residuals inside it.
  • Step 2D updates every prediction by adding the learning rate times the leaf output value.
  • The loop runs M times, usually 100 or more, and Step 3 returns the final model \(F_M(x)\).
  • Real applications use trees larger than the stumps used here for teaching.
