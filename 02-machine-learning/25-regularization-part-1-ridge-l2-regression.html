Regularization Part 1: Ridge (L2) Regression

▶ Watch on YouTube

Ridge regression adds a penalty to least squares, so the fitted line reacts less to the training data. You accept a small amount of bias and get a large drop in variance.

In one breath

Least squares fits the training data as closely as it can, so with few data points it overfits. Ridge regression instead minimises the sum of squared residuals plus lambda times the squared parameters. That penalty pulls slopes toward zero, so predictions become less sensitive to the inputs. You choose lambda by trying many values with cross-validation, usually 10-fold. Ridge can even fit a model when there are fewer data points than parameters, where least squares has no single answer.

The main idea

Josh measures the weight and the size of a group of mice. The points look linear, so he fits a straight line with least squares. Least squares picks the line with the smallest sum of squared residuals. A residual is the vertical gap between a data point and the line.

Now suppose you only have two measurements. The least squares line passes straight through both points, so the sum of squared residuals is exactly zero. Call those two points the training data and the other points the testing data. On the testing data this line is badly wrong. Its residuals there are large, so the line has high variance. In machine learning words, it is overfit.

Ridge regression looks for a line that fits the training points a bit worse on purpose. It adds a penalty, lambda times the squared slope, to the quantity it minimises. A steep slope now costs something, so the fitted line comes out flatter. Think of the slope as the sensitivity of the prediction. A steep slope means one extra unit of weight changes predicted size a lot. A small slope means the prediction barely moves. So a flatter line predicts new mice better.

Step by step

  1. Fit least squares to the two training points. The line touches both points, so the training sum of squared residuals is zero. The testing residuals are large, which means high variance.
  2. Change what you minimise. Keep the sum of squared residuals, then add lambda times the squared slope on top of it.
  3. Compare candidate lines using this new total instead of the residuals alone. A slightly worse fit with a much smaller slope can now win.
  4. Read the slope as sensitivity. A steep slope makes the prediction jump when weight changes a little. Shrinking the slope makes predictions calmer.
  5. Turn the lambda dial. At lambda equal to zero you get the least squares line back. As lambda grows the slope keeps shrinking and moves asymptotically close to zero, but never reaches it.
  6. Pick lambda by trying many values and scoring each one with cross-validation, typically 10-fold. Keep the value that gives the lowest variance on held-out data.
  7. Apply the same idea to bigger models. Penalise every parameter except the intercept, and for logistic regression add the penalty to the likelihood instead of the squared residuals.

The next figure shows what lambda actually does. Josh sets lambda to 1, then 2, then 3. Each time the slope gets smaller and the line gets flatter.

The last part of the video is the surprising one. Least squares needs at least as many data points as parameters. Two parameters need two points. An equation with 10,001 parameters needs 10,001 mice. A gene expression study may measure 10,000 genes but have only 500 mice. Ridge regression still returns an answer, because the penalty prefers small parameters and breaks the tie.

Formulas

Least squares
$$ \min_{b_0,\,b_1} \; \sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(y_i - (b_0 + b_1 x_i)\bigr)^2 $$

Symbols: \(x_i\) is the weight of mouse \(i\); \(y_i\) is its measured size; \(b_0\) is the y-axis intercept; \(b_1\) is the slope; \(n\) is the number of training points. The bracket is the residual, the vertical gap between a point and the line. What it tells you: least squares only cares about the training residuals, so with few points it will drive them to zero and overfit.

Ridge regression for one predictor
$$ \min_{b_0,\,b_1} \; \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(y_i - (b_0 + b_1 x_i)\bigr)^2}_{\text{sum of squared residuals}} \; + \; \underbrace{\lambda\, b_1^{2}}_{\text{ridge penalty}} $$

Symbols: the first term is the same as above; \(\lambda\) (lambda) is a number from zero to infinity that sets how severe the penalty is; \(b_1^{2}\) is the squared slope. What it tells you: a steep slope now has a price, so the best line trades a little extra residual for a much smaller slope.

Ridge penalty in general
$$ \text{SSR} \; + \; \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{p} b_j^{2} $$

Symbols: SSR is the sum of squared residuals; \(p\) is the number of parameters other than the intercept; \(b_j\) is one such parameter, for example a slope for weight or the diet difference \(0.7\) in \(\text{size} = 1.5 + 0.7 \times \text{high fat diet}\). The intercept \(b_0\) is deliberately missing from the sum. What it tells you: every parameter that multiplies a measurement gets shrunk, however many of them there are.

Worked example from the video

Example

Two mice form the training data. The least squares line passes through both, so its sum of squared residuals is zero, and its slope is 1.3. The ridge line misses the two points by 0.3 and 0.1, and its slope is 0.8. Set \(\lambda = 1\) and score both lines.

$$ \text{least squares: } \; 0 + 1 \times 1.3^{2} = 1.69 $$
$$ \text{ridge: } \; 0.3^{2} + 0.1^{2} + 1 \times 0.8^{2} = 0.09 + 0.01 + 0.64 = 0.74 $$

0.74 is smaller than 1.69, so the penalised criterion prefers the ridge line. The ridge line has a little bias on the training data and much less variance on new data.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out

The intercept is never penalised. Every other parameter is scaled by a measurement, so shrinking it changes how sensitive the prediction is. The intercept is not scaled by anything, so there is nothing to desensitise.

Watch out

Lambda is not found by minimising the same criterion. If you did that, the answer would always be zero. You must try a range of values and judge them with cross-validation on held-out data.

Watch out

Ridge shrinks parameters toward zero, but only asymptotically. Even at a large lambda the slope stays slightly above zero, so ridge does not remove variables from the model. That is what Lasso, in the next video, does differently.

Watch out

The ridge fit looks worse on the training data, and that is the point. Judge it on new data. The bias you add is the price you pay for the variance you remove.

If they ask you in an interview

What problem does ridge regression solve?
With small samples, least squares fits the training data too closely and gives a model with high variance. Ridge regression adds a penalty on the squared parameters, which deliberately introduces a little bias. In exchange the model becomes much less sensitive to the training data, so predictions on new data improve.
What does lambda control, and how do you choose it?
Lambda sets how strongly the parameters are pulled toward zero. At lambda equal to zero you get plain least squares, and as lambda grows the slopes shrink asymptotically toward zero. There is no formula for the best value, so I try a grid of values and pick the one with the lowest cross-validated error, usually with 10-fold cross-validation.
How can ridge fit a model with more parameters than data points?
Least squares needs at least as many observations as parameters, otherwise infinitely many fits give zero residuals and there is no unique answer. The ridge penalty breaks that tie because among all those fits it prefers the one with the smallest squared parameters. That is why ridge can estimate 10,001 gene coefficients from 500 mice.
Does ridge regression apply outside linear regression?
Yes, the same penalty works for logistic regression and for larger models with many terms. For logistic regression the model is fitted by maximum likelihood, so the penalty is applied to the sum of the likelihoods rather than to squared residuals. The effect is the same: the estimated slopes shrink and the predictions become less sensitive.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • Least squares minimises only the sum of squared residuals, so small samples lead to overfit lines with high variance.
  • Ridge regression minimises the sum of squared residuals plus lambda times the squared parameters.
  • The penalty adds a little bias and removes a lot of variance, which improves long-run predictions.
  • Shrinking a slope makes the prediction less sensitive to that measurement.
  • Lambda runs from zero to infinity, and zero gives back the least squares fit.
  • You choose lambda by cross-validation, typically 10-fold.
  • The penalty includes every parameter except the y-axis intercept, and it also works for discrete predictors and for logistic regression.
  • Ridge can still find a solution when there are fewer data points than parameters.
