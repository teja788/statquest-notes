Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA)

▶ Watch on YouTube

Linear discriminant analysis squeezes many measurements down to one or two new axes. It chooses those axes to push known groups as far apart as it can.

In one breath

Josh starts with a cancer drug that helps some people and makes others feel worse. He asks whether gene expression can tell the two groups apart. One gene leaves a messy overlap in the middle, and more than three genes cannot be drawn at all. LDA builds a new axis out of all the genes at once, then projects the data onto it. It picks that axis so the category means land far apart while each category stays tight.

The main idea

The drug works well for some patients and makes others feel worse. Josh wants to know who should get it. He is a geneticist, so he looks at gene expression for an answer. With one gene, Gene X, he draws a plain number line. Green dots are people the drug helps, and red dots are people it harms. Low transcription of Gene X mostly means the drug works, and high transcription mostly means it does not. In the middle the two colours mix, so there is no obvious cut-off. One gene does an okay job, and no better.

Two genes do better. With Gene X and Gene Y on a scatter plot, a straight line can split most of the green from most of the red. Three genes need a flat plane, and a plane drawn on a flat screen is hard to judge. You would have to rotate the picture to trust it. With four genes, or ten thousand, you cannot draw the plot at all.

The same wall showed up with principal component analysis. PCA reduces dimensions by chasing the genes with the most variation. But variation is not what we want here. LDA also reduces dimensions, and it maximises the separation among the known categories.

Take the simplest case: two genes down to one number line. One bad option is to ignore Gene Y and drop every point onto the Gene X axis. That throws away the useful information Gene Y carries, and the two colours blur together. Ignoring Gene X is no better. LDA instead builds a brand new axis out of both genes. It then projects the data onto that new axis in the way that separates the two categories most.

Step by step

  1. Start with data that is already labelled. LDA has to know which category every point belongs to, so it is a supervised method.
  2. Look for a new axis. That axis is a blend of all the genes, not one gene picked out of the set.
  3. First criterion: after the points land on the new axis, the two category means should sit far apart. Josh writes each mean with the Greek letter μ.
  4. Second criterion: the spread inside each category should be small. The video calls this spread the scatter and writes it as s².
  5. Judge both criteria at the same time with one ratio. The squared distance between the means goes on top, and the two scatter values go underneath.
  6. Keep the axis that makes that ratio as large as possible. Then project every point onto it and read off the new positions.
  1. With more genes nothing really changes. LDA still builds one new axis out of all of them, and still balances the same two criteria.
  2. With three categories, first find the point at the centre of all the data. Then measure the distance from each category centre to that overall centre.
  3. Three category centres define a plane, so LDA now creates two axes. LD1 carries the most separation between categories, and LD2 the second most.

Formulas

The ratio LDA maximises, two categories
$$ \frac{(\mu_{\text{green}} - \mu_{\text{red}})^2}{s_{\text{green}}^2 + s_{\text{red}}^2} $$

Symbols: \(\mu_{\text{green}}\) is the mean of the green points after they land on the new axis. \(\mu_{\text{red}}\) is the same for the red points. \(s_{\text{green}}^2\) is the scatter of the green points around their own mean, and scatter is the video's word for variation. \(s_{\text{red}}^2\) is the scatter for red. What it tells you: an axis is good when the two means are far apart and each group stays tight. The top is squared because Josh does not know which mean will be larger, and a negative gap would be meaningless.

The same ratio, written with d
$$ \frac{d^2}{s_{\text{green}}^2 + s_{\text{red}}^2} $$

Symbols: \(d\) is short for the distance between the two means, so \(d = \mu_{\text{green}} - \mu_{\text{red}}\). The bottom is unchanged. What it tells you: exactly the same thing, in a shorter form. Josh switches to this version before he moves on to three categories.

Three categories
$$ \frac{d_1^2 + d_2^2 + d_3^2}{s_1^2 + s_2^2 + s_3^2} $$

Symbols: \(d_1\), \(d_2\) and \(d_3\) are the distances from each category centre to the point at the centre of all the data. \(s_1^2\), \(s_2^2\) and \(s_3^2\) are the scatter values of each category around its own centre. What it tells you: the shape of the rule never changes. Each extra category simply adds one distance term on top and one scatter term underneath.

Worked example from the video

Example

Josh first shows why the ratio needs both parts. He draws a data set with a little overlap on Gene Y and a wide spread along Gene X. If he chases \(d\) alone, the projected groups still mix in the middle. When he optimises both parts together, the means end up slightly closer, but the scatter drops a lot. That second axis gives the clean split.

He finishes with real data: three known categories and 10,000 genes. A raw plot would need 10,000 axes, which nobody can draw. LDA reduces that to two axes, and the three categories are easy to see. The separation is not perfect, but it is clearly three groups.

He then runs PCA on the same genes. That plot shows a lot of overlap between the black and the blue points. PCA is not failing here. It only looks for the genes with the most variation, and that direction does not split these groups.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out LDA is supervised and PCA is not. LDA needs the category labels before it starts. If those labels are wrong or noisy, the new axes are optimised for the wrong target.
Watch out A big gap between the means is not enough on its own. The video shows a data set where that choice still leaves the two groups mixed in the middle. The scatter in the denominator is what stops that from happening.
Watch out The number of new axes follows the categories, not the genes. Three category centres define a plane, so you get two axes. Ten thousand genes do not give you ten thousand LDA axes.

If they ask you in an interview

What is LDA, and how is it different from PCA?
Both methods reduce dimensions by building new axes out of the original variables. PCA is unsupervised and picks the directions with the most variation. LDA is supervised and picks the directions that best separate categories you already know. So on labelled data LDA usually gives a much cleaner picture of the classes.
What does LDA actually optimise?
It maximises a ratio. On top is the squared distance between the category means after projection. Underneath is the sum of the scatter, which is the variation inside each category. A good axis pushes the means apart and keeps each group tight at the same time.
Why not just maximise the distance between the class means?
Because a wide group can still overlap another group even when its mean sits far away. The video shows exactly that case, where the means are far apart but the projected points mix in the middle. Putting the scatter in the denominator trades a little distance for a much tighter group, and that gives the cleaner split.
How many discriminant axes do you get?
It depends on the number of categories, not the number of features. Two categories give one new axis. Three category centres define a plane, so you get two axes. The axes are ranked, so LD1 explains the most separation between categories and LD2 the next most.
How do you tell which variables drive an LDA axis?
In much the same way as with PCA. With PCA you read the loading scores. With LDA you look at which variables correlate with the new axes. That tells you which measurements separate the groups.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • LDA reduces many variables down to a few new axes, just like PCA does.
  • Unlike PCA, LDA uses the known category labels and aims at separability.
  • It picks the axis that maximises the squared distance between the means divided by the sum of the within-category scatter.
  • Maximising the distance alone is not enough, because wide groups still overlap.
  • With three categories it measures each category centre against the centre of all the data, and returns two ranked axes.
  • The axes are ranked, so LD1 carries the most separation and LD2 the second most.
  • On Josh's real data, 10,000 genes collapse to two LDA axes with three visible groups.
