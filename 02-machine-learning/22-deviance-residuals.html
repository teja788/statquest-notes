Deviance Residuals

▶ Watch on YouTube

A deviance residual shows how much one data point adds to the residual deviance. It is the logistic regression answer to the plain residual you know from least squares.

In one breath

The residual deviance compares the saturated model with the proposed model. Likelihoods multiply, so log likelihoods add, and the deviance splits into one term per point. The square root of each term is that point's deviance residual. Square them and add them, and the residual deviance comes back. Plot them to spot outliers.

The main idea

This video follows on from saturated models and deviance statistics. The residual deviance is twice the gap between two log likelihoods. One is the saturated model, which fits every point perfectly. The other is the proposed model, the one you fitted.

Josh uses three data points. The likelihood of the data is point one times point two times point three. Taking the log turns that product into a sum. So the deviance becomes a sum of small differences, one per data point. The square root of each difference is a deviance residual.

Step by step

  1. Write the saturated likelihood as a product over the data points, then take the log.
  2. Do the same for the proposed model, and put both log likelihoods into the deviance equation.
  3. Regroup the terms by data point instead of by model. Now each point owns one term.
  4. Take the square root of each term. That is the deviance residual for that point.
  5. Square the deviance residuals and add them, and you are back at the residual deviance.

In logistic regression the equation can be rewritten using the distance from each point to the best fitting squiggly line. Points above the squiggle get a positive residual. Points below it get a negative one. That sign makes the residual plot readable.

Formulas

Residual deviance, split by point
$$ D \;=\; 2\left[\log L_{\text{sat}} - \log L_{\text{prop}}\right] \;=\; 2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\log L_{\text{sat},i} - \log L_{\text{prop},i}\right] $$

Symbols: \(D\) is the residual deviance. \(L_{\text{sat}}\) is the likelihood under the saturated model, which fits every point perfectly. \(L_{\text{prop}}\) is the likelihood under the model you fitted. \(n\) is the number of points. What it tells you: likelihoods multiply, so their logs add, and the deviance is a sum of per point pieces.

Deviance residual
$$ d_i \;=\; \operatorname{sign}\!\left(y_i - \hat{p}_i\right)\sqrt{\,2\left[\log L_{\text{sat},i} - \log L_{\text{prop},i}\right]\,} $$

Symbols: \(d_i\) is the deviance residual for point \(i\). \(y_i\) is the observed outcome, 0 or 1. \(\hat{p}_i\) is the probability the fitted squiggle gives that point. So the sign is positive above the squiggle and negative below it. What it tells you: \(d_i\) is the square root of one point's contribution to the total deviance.

Putting them back together
$$ \sum_{i=1}^{n} d_i^{\,2} \;=\; D $$

Symbols: the same \(d_i\) and \(D\) as above. What it tells you: squaring and adding the deviance residuals rebuilds the residual deviance, just as squaring ordinary residuals gives the sum of squares.

Worked example from the video

Example

Josh uses three data points. The saturated likelihood is \(L_{\text{sat},1}\times L_{\text{sat},2}\times L_{\text{sat},3}\), and the proposed model gives the matching product.

Taking logs turns both products into sums, so the deviance becomes \(D = 2\left[(\log L_{\text{sat},1}-\log L_{\text{prop},1}) + (\log L_{\text{sat},2}-\log L_{\text{prop},2}) + (\log L_{\text{sat},3}-\log L_{\text{prop},3})\right]\).

The first bracket belongs to point one, and so on. The square root of each bracket gives \(d_1\), \(d_2\) and \(d_3\). Square those three, add them, and you are back at \(D\).

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out A deviance residual is a square root, not a plain difference. It measures a gap in log likelihood, not a gap in the units of the response.
Watch out The square root itself is never negative. The sign comes from whether the point sits above or below the squiggle. Without it you could not tell the two sides apart in a plot.
Watch out A large deviance residual is a flag, not a verdict. Josh checks the second and fifth points for a labelling mistake before calling them outliers.

If they ask you in an interview

What is a deviance residual?
It is the square root of the contribution one data point makes to the residual deviance. The residual deviance is twice the gap in log likelihood between the saturated and proposed models. Log likelihoods add across points, so that gap splits into one term per point. The square root of a point's term, with a sign attached, is its deviance residual.
How do deviance residuals relate to ordinary least squares residuals?
They play the same role. In least squares you square the residuals and add them to get the sum of squares. With deviance residuals you square and add them to get the residual deviance. Both numbers tell you how well the model fits.
Why do we plot them, and what are we looking for?
We plot them to find outliers. If the model fits well, the residuals sit close to zero and centred on it. Points far from zero are fitted badly. I would check those rows for a labelling mistake first.

Recap in 30 seconds
