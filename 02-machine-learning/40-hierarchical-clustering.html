Hierarchical Clustering

▶ Watch on YouTube

Hierarchical clustering reorders the rows and columns of a heat map so that similar things sit together. The dendrogram beside the heat map records the order in which they joined.

In one breath

A heat map shows samples in columns and genes in rows. Red means the gene is highly expressed and blue means it is lowly expressed. Hierarchical clustering repeatedly finds the two most similar rows and merges them into a cluster. It keeps going until everything sits in one big cluster. The dendrogram draws that merge history, and short branches mark the pairs that were most alike.

The main idea

Josh starts with two versions of the same heat map. The left one has no clustering, so the colours look like noise. The right one is clustered, so blocks of red and blue appear. Nothing about the data changed. Only the order of the rows changed, and that alone makes the pattern visible.

The working example is small: three samples and four genes. We only cluster the rows, which are the genes. Genes 1 and 2 are different, because gene 1 is red in sample 1 while gene 2 is blue there. Genes 1 and 3 are similar, because both are red in sample 1 and both are blue in sample 3. Gene 1 also looks similar to gene 4, but gene 3 is the closest match of all.

So the method is simple. Find the closest pair, glue them together, and treat the glued pair as one row. Then repeat. Each merge is one branch point in the dendrogram, and the branch length tells you how similar the two things were.

Step by step

  1. Take gene 1 and compare it with every other gene. Gene 3 turns out to be its closest match.
  2. Do the same for gene 2, then gene 3, then gene 4. Now every row knows its own best match.
  3. Look across all those pairs and pick the single most similar pair. Genes 1 and 3 win, so they merge into cluster 1.
  4. Go back to step 1, but treat cluster 1 as if it were one gene. This time gene 2 and gene 4 are the closest pair, so they merge into cluster 2.
  5. Repeat once more. Only two clusters are left, so they merge into cluster 3 and the work is finished.
  6. Draw the dendrogram. Cluster 1 was formed first and gets the shortest branch. Cluster 3 was formed last and gets the longest.

Two choices sit inside this loop, and both are yours to make. The first is how you measure the distance between two rows. The second is how you measure the distance between a row and a cluster that already holds several rows. The figure below shows the three common answers to that second question.

Formulas

The method never says what "most similar" means. You supply that. Euclidean distance is the most common choice, so start there.

Euclidean distance, two samples
$$ d(a,b)=\sqrt{(a_1-b_1)^2+(a_2-b_2)^2} $$

Symbols: \(a\) and \(b\) are two genes, that is two rows of the heat map. \(a_1\) is the value of gene \(a\) in sample 1 and \(a_2\) is its value in sample 2. \(b_1\) and \(b_2\) are the same two readings for gene \(b\). What it tells you: it is the straight-line gap between the two genes. Small distance means the two genes behave alike across the samples.

Euclidean distance, any number of samples
$$ d(a,b)=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a_i-b_i)^2} $$

Symbols: \(n\) is how many samples, that is how many columns the heat map has. \(i\) counts the samples from 1 to \(n\). \(a_i\) and \(b_i\) are the two genes' values in sample \(i\). What it tells you: extra samples just add more squared differences under the root. Nothing else about the method changes.

Manhattan distance
$$ d(a,b)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lvert a_i-b_i\rvert $$

Symbols: the symbols mean the same as above, and \(\lvert\;\rvert\) means absolute value, so a negative difference is counted as positive. What it tells you: instead of squaring the differences and taking a root, you simply add up their sizes. Picture the differences as line segments laid end to end, and the total length is the Manhattan distance.

Worked example from the video

Example

Josh shrinks the heat map to two samples and two genes, and shows the numbers behind the colours. Gene 1 reads 1.6 in sample 1 and 0.5 in sample 2. Gene 2 reads -0.5 in sample 1 and -1.9 in sample 2.

$$ d=\sqrt{\bigl(1.6-(-0.5)\bigr)^2+\bigl(0.5-(-1.9)\bigr)^2} $$

Do the two subtractions first. The gap in sample 1 is 2.1 and the gap in sample 2 is 2.4.

$$ d=\sqrt{2.1^2+2.4^2}=\sqrt{10.17}=3.2 $$

Those two gaps are the sides of a right triangle. The sample 1 gap is the horizontal side and the sample 2 gap is the vertical side. The Pythagorean theorem, \(h=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\), says the hypotenuse is the distance between the genes. So Euclidean distance is just Pythagoras with one term per sample.

Apply the Manhattan rule to the same two gaps and you add them instead: 2.1 plus 2.4 gives 4.5. The number differs, but the idea of "these two genes are far apart" is the same.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out The distance metric is chosen, not discovered. Josh is blunt about this: there is no biological or physical reason to prefer Euclidean over Manhattan. A heat map drawn with each one looks very similar but not identical. Pick the one that gives you more insight, and say which one you used.
Watch out Linkage is a second, separate choice. Once genes 1 and 3 are one cluster, you still have to decide how to measure its distance to gene 4. Centroid uses the cluster average, single linkage uses its closest point, and complete linkage uses its furthest point. In R, the hclust function uses complete linkage by default.
Watch out Read the dendrogram by branch height, not by neighbours. Two rows can end up side by side in the picture and still only join at a tall branch. Height records how similar the two groups were when they merged, and that is the part that carries meaning.
Watch out Clustering changes the presentation, not the data. Every cell keeps its value. Only the order of rows or columns moves. A striking block of colour is a sign that the ordering worked, not proof of a new biological finding.

If they ask you in an interview

What does hierarchical clustering do to a heat map?
It reorders the rows, or the columns, or both, so that similar ones sit next to each other. It works bottom up: find the two most similar items, merge them into a cluster, then repeat with that cluster treated as a single item. It stops when everything is in one cluster. The values in the heat map never change, only their order.
How do you measure similarity between two rows?
You choose a distance metric. Euclidean distance is the usual default: square the difference in each sample, add them, take the square root. Manhattan distance instead adds the absolute differences. Both are reasonable, and the choice is a judgement call rather than a rule.
What is linkage, and which one would you use?
Linkage is the rule for measuring distance to a cluster that already contains several members. Centroid compares to the cluster average, single linkage to its nearest member, and complete linkage to its furthest member. I would start with the software default, which is complete linkage in R's hclust, then check whether the picture holds up under another rule.
How do you read a dendrogram?
The branch height shows when each merge happened and how similar the two groups were. Short branches mean the merge happened early between very similar items. The tallest branch is the final merge that joins everything. So I read from the short branches outward when I look for real structure.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • Hierarchical clustering reorders heat map rows and columns so that similar ones sit together.
  • It repeatedly merges the two most similar items, then treats the new cluster as a single item.
  • The dendrogram records that merge history, and short branches mark the most similar pairs.
  • Similarity needs a distance metric, and Euclidean distance is Pythagoras with one squared term per sample.
  • Manhattan distance adds absolute differences instead, and it gives a similar but not identical picture.
  • Comparing to a cluster needs a linkage rule: centroid, single or complete.
  • Both choices are arbitrary, though most software ships with sensible defaults.
