Neural Networks Pt. 2: Backpropagation Main Ideas

▶ Watch on YouTube Also in: Neural Networks / Deep Learning (video 5)

This video shows how a neural network learns one of its parameters. The chain rule gives a derivative, which is the slope of the error. Gradient descent then uses that slope to improve the parameter.

In one breath

Josh reuses the drug dosage network from Part 1 and names its weights w1 to w4 and biases b1 to b3. He assumes every parameter is already optimal except the last bias, b3. He measures fit with the sum of the squared residuals and links it to b3 through the predicted values. The chain rule gives the derivative of that sum with respect to b3. Gradient descent then moves b3 from 0 to its best value, 2.61.

The main idea

Backpropagation is the method that finds good values for the weights and biases. A weight scales a value on a connection, and a bias is a number added to it. Conceptually, backpropagation starts with the last parameter and works backwards to the others.

Josh explains all the main ideas with one parameter: the final bias, b3. The network output, called the green squiggle, is the blue curve plus the orange curve plus b3. Changing b3 shifts the whole squiggle up or down. So there is one value of b3 that makes the squiggle fit the data best.

To find that value, Josh plots the sum of the squared residuals against b3. A residual is the observed value minus the predicted value. The result is a pink curve with a lowest point. Gradient descent is a method that uses the slope to step downhill toward that point. It gets there in a few steps, instead of testing many values by hand.

Step by step

  1. Name the parameters: weights w1, w2, w3, w4 and biases b1, b2, b3. Assume all are already optimal except b3. Dosages run from 0 (low) to 1 (high).
  2. Run the dosages through the top hidden node. The softplus activation function gives the blue curve, which is then multiplied by −1.22.
  3. Run the dosages through the bottom hidden node. Softplus gives the orange curve, which is multiplied by −2.3. Add the blue and orange curves to get the green squiggle.
  4. Give b3 a starting value. Bias terms are often set to 0 at the start, so b3 = 0. Adding 0 leaves the squiggle far below the data.
  5. Measure the fit with the sum of the squared residuals. At b3 = 0 it is 20.4. Trying b3 = 1, 2 and 3 gives 7.8, 1.11 and 0.46.
  6. Use the chain rule to find the derivative of the sum of squared residuals with respect to b3. The link between them is the predicted values.
  7. Plug the derivative into gradient descent. Take steps until the step size is close to zero.

The chain rule is a way to take the derivative of a function that sits inside another function. The figure below shows why it fits here. b3 changes the predicted values, and the predicted values change the sum of squared residuals. The derivative travels back along this chain.

Formulas

Sum of squared residuals
$$\text{SSR} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left(\text{Observed}_i - \text{Predicted}_i\right)^2$$

Symbols: \(\Sigma\) means add things together. \(i\) is an index for each data point, starting at 1. \(n\) is the number of data points, here 3. \(\text{Observed}_i\) is the measured value and \(\text{Predicted}_i\) is the green squiggle's value at that dosage. What it tells you: one number for how badly the squiggle fits; smaller is better.

Predicted value
$$\text{Predicted}_i = \text{blue}_i + \text{orange}_i + b_3$$

Symbols: \(\text{blue}_i\) and \(\text{orange}_i\) are the y-values of the two scaled curves at dosage \(i\). \(b_3\) is the final bias. What it tells you: b3 shifts every predicted value by the same amount.

Chain rule for b3
$$\frac{d\,\text{SSR}}{d\,b_3} = \frac{d\,\text{SSR}}{d\,\text{Predicted}} \times \frac{d\,\text{Predicted}}{d\,b_3}$$

The error depends on b3 only through the predicted values, so we multiply two simpler derivatives.

$$\frac{d\,\text{SSR}}{d\,\text{Predicted}} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} 2\left(\text{Observed}_i - \text{Predicted}_i\right)\times(-1)$$

We use the chain rule again on each squared term. The power 2 moves to the front, then we multiply by the inside's derivative, −1, because Predicted has a minus sign.

$$\frac{d\,\text{Predicted}}{d\,b_3} = 0 + 0 + 1 = 1$$

The blue and orange curves were made before b3, so their derivatives are 0. The derivative of b3 with respect to itself is 1.

$$\frac{d\,\text{SSR}}{d\,b_3} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} -2\left(\text{Observed}_i - \text{Predicted}_i\right)\times 1$$

We multiply the two parts to get the slope for any b3. The "× 1" changes nothing, but it reminds us the answer has two parts.

Gradient descent update
$$\text{Step size} = \text{slope} \times \text{learning rate}, \qquad b_3^{\text{new}} = b_3^{\text{old}} - \text{Step size}$$

Symbols: the slope is \(d\,\text{SSR}/d\,b_3\) at the current b3. The learning rate is a small number, 0.1 in the video. What it tells you: a steep slope gives a big step, and a flat slope gives a small step.

Worked example from the video

Example

Start at b3 = 0. The three observed values are 0, 1 and 0. The squiggle predicts −2.6, −1.61 and −2.61. The residuals are 0 − (−2.6) = 2.6, 1 − (−1.61) = 2.61 and 0 − (−2.61) = 2.61. Squared and added, they give 20.4.

Slope at b3 = 0. Plug into the derivative: −2(2.6) − 2(2.61) − 2(2.61) = −15.64. The video shows −15.7, likely from unrounded predictions. With learning rate 0.1, the step size is −15.7 × 0.1 = −1.57. The new b3 is 0 − (−1.57) = 1.57.

Second step. At b3 = 1.57 the squiggle moves up and the residuals shrink. The slope is now −6.26, so the step size is −6.26 × 0.1 ≈ −0.63. Josh reports the new b3 as 2.19.

Stop. Josh keeps stepping until the step size is close to zero. That happens at b3 = 2.61, so 2.61 is the optimal value.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out This video optimizes only b3. All other parameters are assumed already optimal. Optimizing all of them together is the topic of the next parts.
Watch out The −2 comes from two places: the power 2 and the −1 from the inside of the square. The sign matters. A negative slope gives a negative step size, and subtracting it makes b3 larger.
Watch out The derivative of Predicted with respect to b3 is 1 because the blue and orange curves do not depend on b3. The "× 1" does nothing numerically, but it shows the two-part structure of the chain rule.
Watch out Gradient descent stops when the step size is close to zero, not after a fixed guess. Plugging in many b3 values would also find the minimum, but gradient descent gets there much faster.

If they ask you in an interview

What are the main ideas of backpropagation?
There are two. First, use the chain rule to get the derivative of the loss, here the sum of squared residuals, with respect to an unknown parameter. Second, start the parameter at some value and plug that derivative into gradient descent to optimize it.
Why do we need the chain rule to optimize the final bias?
The sum of squared residuals does not contain b3 directly. It depends on the predicted values, and the predicted values depend on b3. So the derivative is the derivative of the loss with respect to the predictions, times the derivative of the predictions with respect to b3.
Why is the derivative of the prediction with respect to b3 equal to 1?
The prediction is the blue curve plus the orange curve plus b3. The two curves are computed before b3 and do not change with it, so their derivatives are 0. The derivative of b3 with respect to itself is 1.
Walk me through one gradient descent step for b3.
At b3 = 0 the slope is about −15.7. With a learning rate of 0.1 the step size is −1.57. We subtract the step size from the old value, so b3 becomes 1.57, and we repeat until the step size is close to zero.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • Backpropagation finds the weights and biases, working from the last parameter backwards.
  • The network output is the blue curve plus the orange curve plus the final bias b3.
  • The fit is measured with the sum of squared residuals, which is 20.4 when b3 = 0.
  • The chain rule links the loss to b3 through the predicted values.
  • The derivative is the sum of −2 times each residual, times 1.
  • Gradient descent with learning rate 0.1 moves b3 from 0 to 1.57, then 2.19, and stops at 2.61.
