PCA main ideas in only 5 minutes

▶ Watch on YouTube

This video gives the short version of principal component analysis. It shows what a PCA plot is for, and how to read one.

In one breath

Josh starts with cells that look the same from the outside. He measures many genes in each cell, so each cell has many numbers. Two cells can be compared on a simple plot, and three cells on a rotating 3D plot, but four or more cells cannot. PCA solves this. It turns the correlations among all the cells into one 2D plot, where similar cells sit close together.

The main idea

Josh has normal-looking cells and suspects there are three hidden types. He cannot see the difference from the outside, so he sequences the messenger RNA in each cell. That tells him which genes are active, and therefore what each cell is doing. If the samples were people, the measurements could be weight, blood pressure or reading level.

With only two cells, the comparison is easy. Put cell 1 on one axis and cell 2 on the other, then plot one dot per gene. In Josh's data the dots slope downward: gene 1 is high in cell 1 and low in cell 2, while gene 9 is the reverse. That inverse correlation says the two cells use different genes, so they are probably different types.

The trouble starts at four cells. You would need a plot for every pair, or one graph with an axis per cell. A PCA plot replaces them with a single 2D picture of all the cells.

Step by step

  1. Measure many variables in every sample. Here Josh measures how active each gene is in each cell.
  2. Notice that pairwise plots do not scale. Two cells give one plot, three give a rotating 3D plot, four or more give chaos.
  3. Run PCA. It converts the correlations among the cells into a 2D graph.
  4. Read the clusters. Highly correlated cells land close together. Colour code the groups to see them clearly.
  5. Go back to the original cells. Josh finds three clusters, which confirms his suspicion of three cell types.
  6. Respect the ranking of the axes. PC1 carries more variation than PC2, so the same gap means more along PC1.

Formulas

This short video has no equations. It only states that the axes are ranked by importance. Here is that rule written out.

Rule of thumb
$$ \text{Var}(\text{PC}_1) \ \ge\ \text{Var}(\text{PC}_2) \ \ge\ \dots \ \ge\ \text{Var}(\text{PC}_p) $$

Symbols: \(\text{PC}_k\) is the \(k\)-th axis of the PCA plot. \(\text{Var}(\text{PC}_k)\) is how spread out the samples are along that axis. \(p\) is the number of components. What it tells you: the components come out in order of decreasing importance, so a gap along PC1 means more than an equal gap along PC2.

Worked example from the video

Example

Josh has a table of cells and genes. With two cells he plots one dot per gene. Gene 1 sits high on the cell 1 axis and low on the cell 2 axis, while gene 9 sits the other way round. The cloud slopes down, so cell 1 and cell 2 are inversely correlated.

He then adds cell 3. Cell 1 against cell 3 slopes up, so those two are alike. Cell 2 against cell 3 slopes down, so cell 2 differs. All three fit on a rotating 3D plot, but a fourth cell breaks the trick. The PCA plot handles them all and shows three coloured clusters.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out Do not read the two axes as equally important. Josh is explicit that PC1 outranks PC2, so equal distances on screen do not mean equal differences.
Watch out In this example the plot axes are the cells and the dots are the genes. It is easy to flip the roles, so check what one dot stands for.
Watch out PCA finds clusters, but it does not name them. Josh goes back to the original cells to work out what each cluster means. PCA is also only one option: heat maps, t-SNE and multiple dimension scaling do similar jobs.

If they ask you in an interview

In one minute, what does a PCA plot do?
It takes data with far too many measurements to picture and squeezes it into a 2D graph. The graph keeps the correlation structure, so samples that behave alike end up near each other. In the gene example, three clusters mean three types of cell.
Why not just plot every pair of variables instead?
That works for two, and a rotating 3D plot works for three. Beyond that the number of pairwise plots grows quickly and no one can hold them all in their head. PCA gives you one picture instead of many.
Two clusters are far apart on PC2 and two others are the same distance apart on PC1. Which pair differs more?
The pair separated along PC1. The components are ranked by how much variation they capture, and PC1 always captures the most. So the same visual distance means a larger real difference on PC1.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • Samples that look identical can still differ, so we measure many variables in each one.
  • Two samples can be compared on a scatter plot, and three on a rotating 3D plot.
  • Four or more samples break both of those approaches.
  • PCA turns the correlations among the samples into a single 2D plot.
  • Samples that are highly correlated cluster together, and colour coding makes the groups easy to see.
  • The axes are ranked, so differences along PC1 matter more than equal differences along PC2.
  • Heat maps, t-SNE and multiple dimension scaling are other tools for the same job.
