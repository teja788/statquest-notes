ROC and AUC

▶ Watch on YouTube

A classifier gives you a probability, not a label. You pick a threshold to turn that probability into a label. ROC and AUC show you every threshold at once.

In one breath

Josh fits a logistic regression that predicts if a mouse is obese from its weight. Every threshold you choose gives a different confusion matrix. Rather than read dozens of matrices, you plot one point per threshold. The true positive rate goes up the y-axis and the false positive rate along the x-axis. Joining the points gives the ROC graph, which helps you pick a threshold. The area under that curve, the AUC, lets you compare two models.

The main idea

The video starts with mice. The blue points are obese mice and the red points are mice that are not obese. Weight runs along the x-axis. One heavy mouse is not obese, and one light mouse is obese, so weight alone does not separate the groups perfectly.

Logistic regression turns the y-axis into the probability that a mouse is obese. A heavy mouse gets a high probability. A light mouse gets a low probability. That is useful, but a probability is not a decision.

To make a decision you set a threshold. A threshold is a cut-off probability. With a threshold of 0.5, every mouse above 0.5 is called obese and every mouse at or below 0.5 is called not obese. The figure below shows that rule on the eight test mice.

Josh then asks the key question. Why 0.5? If missing an obese mouse is costly, lower the threshold to 0.1. That catches all four obese mice, at the price of more false alarms. Josh gives a sharper version. Imagine you are screening for Ebola. You would lower the threshold a lot, because letting an infected person through is far worse than a false alarm.

Each threshold gives its own confusion matrix. Testing every useful threshold gives a confusing pile of matrices. The ROC graph replaces the pile with one picture.

Step by step

  1. Fit the model. Logistic regression gives each mouse a probability of being obese.
  2. Pick a threshold so low that every sample is called obese. Build the confusion matrix for it.
  3. Compute the true positive rate, which is sensitivity, and the false positive rate, which is one minus specificity.
  4. Plot one point at that pair of rates. The very low threshold lands at the top right corner, at (1, 1).
  5. Raise the threshold one step, so the next lightest sample flips to not obese. Make its confusion matrix and plot its point.
  6. Repeat until the threshold is so high that every sample is called not obese. That point sits at the bottom left corner, at (0, 0).
  7. Join the points. Points to the left of the diagonal are thresholds where you catch positives faster than you raise false alarms.
  8. Measure the area under the joined curve. That single number, the AUC, ranks whole models against each other.

You do not need to test every possible threshold. Many nearby thresholds produce exactly the same confusion matrix, so they give the same point. Only the thresholds that fall between two samples change anything.

Formulas

True positive rate (sensitivity), the y-axis
$$ \text{TPR} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN} $$

Symbols: \(TP\) is true positives, the obese mice correctly called obese. \(FN\) is false negatives, the obese mice wrongly called not obese. The bottom is therefore every truly obese mouse. What it tells you: the share of real positives that the model catches.

False positive rate (1 − specificity), the x-axis
$$ \text{FPR} = \frac{FP}{FP + TN} = 1 - \text{specificity} $$

Symbols: \(FP\) is false positives, the not-obese mice wrongly called obese. \(TN\) is true negatives, the not-obese mice correctly called not obese. The bottom is every truly not-obese mouse. What it tells you: the share of real negatives that the model falsely flags.

Precision, an alternative x-axis
$$ \text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP} $$

Symbols: \(TP\) is true positives and \(FP\) is false positives, so the bottom is everything the model called obese. What it tells you: the share of positive predictions that were right. Note that \(TN\) does not appear, so precision is not diluted when negatives greatly outnumber positives.

Example

Josh tests the model on four mice known to be obese and four known to be not obese. At a threshold of 0.5 the confusion matrix comes out like this.

Actually obeseActually not obese
Predicted obese3 (TP)1 (FP)
Predicted not obese1 (FN)3 (TN)

Now take the extreme low threshold, where every mouse is called obese. There are 4 true positives and 0 false negatives, so \(\text{TPR} = 4/(4+0) = 1\). There are 4 false positives and 0 true negatives, so \(\text{FPR} = 4/(4+0) = 1\). That is the point at (1, 1): every obese mouse caught, and every healthy mouse falsely flagged.

Raise the threshold so the lightest mouse is now called not obese. That mouse was not obese, so it moves from \(FP\) to \(TN\). Now \(\text{TPR} = 4/4 = 1\) and \(\text{FPR} = 3/4 = 0.75\), giving the point (0.75, 1). Raising the threshold once more removes another false positive and gives (0.5, 1). Each step to the left is an improvement.

Keep going and you reach a threshold where \(\text{TPR} = 0.75\) and \(\text{FPR} = 0\). That threshold catches 75% of the obese mice and produces no false positives at all. Finally, a threshold so high that nothing is called obese gives 0 true positives and 0 false positives, the point at (0, 0).

Joining the nine points gives the ROC graph in the figure above. Josh reads its area as 0.9. If a random forest gave a curve with a smaller area, say a blue curve under this red one, you would prefer the logistic regression.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out A threshold of 0.5 is a habit, not a law. The right threshold depends on the cost of each error. For a screening test where a missed case is dangerous, a low threshold with more false alarms is the correct choice.
Watch out An ROC curve measures the ranking, not one decision. AUC tells you how well the model orders positives above negatives across all thresholds. It does not tell you which threshold to ship.
Watch out With heavy class imbalance the false positive rate can look flattering. Its denominator includes all the true negatives. So a large count of false positives still gives a small rate. Swap in precision when positives are rare, as in a rare disease study.
Watch out Do not read the diagonal as a target. The green line is where the true positive rate equals the false positive rate, which is no better than guessing. You want points above and to the left of it.

If they ask you in an interview

What does an ROC curve actually plot?
Each point on the curve is one classification threshold. The y-axis is the true positive rate, which is sensitivity, and the x-axis is the false positive rate, which is one minus specificity. So the curve is a compact summary of the confusion matrix you would get at every threshold, and it shows the trade-off between catching positives and raising false alarms.
What does AUC mean, and what does 0.5 mean?
AUC is the area under the ROC curve, so it condenses the whole curve into one number between 0 and 1. An AUC of 0.5 sits on the diagonal and means the model ranks positives and negatives no better than chance. Higher is better, and I mainly use AUC to compare two models on the same data rather than to judge one model on its own.
How do you choose the threshold in practice?
I start from the cost of the two error types instead of defaulting to 0.5. If a missed positive is expensive, such as a missed infection, I move the threshold down and accept more false positives. Then I look at the ROC points and pick the one that gives the highest true positive rate I can afford at that false positive rate.
When would you prefer a precision-recall curve?
When the positive class is rare. The false positive rate divides by the number of true negatives, so with thousands of negatives a large number of false alarms still looks like a tiny rate. Precision divides by everything predicted positive, so it stays honest under imbalance, which is why I use it for rare disease or fraud problems.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • Logistic regression gives a probability, and a threshold turns that probability into a label.
  • Every threshold produces its own confusion matrix, so comparing them by hand becomes unmanageable.
  • An ROC graph plots the true positive rate against the false positive rate, one point per threshold.
  • The top right point calls everything positive. The bottom left point calls nothing positive.
  • The green diagonal is chance, so points above and to the left of it are the useful thresholds.
  • The area under the curve, the AUC, summarises the whole curve in one number for comparing models.
  • Replace the false positive rate with precision when negatives greatly outnumber positives.
  • ROC helps you pick a threshold, and AUC helps you pick a method.
