Decision and Classification Trees

▶ Watch on YouTube

This video shows how to read a classification tree, and how to build one from raw data using Gini impurity. It is the base for random forests, gradient boosting and many other models.

In one breath

A decision tree makes a statement, then moves left or right based on true or false. A tree that predicts categories is a classification tree. A tree that predicts numbers is a regression tree. To build a tree, we test every feature as a possible question and measure how mixed the resulting groups are. Gini impurity gives that measure, and the feature with the lowest impurity goes at the top.

The main idea

A decision tree asks a question and then follows one of two paths. If the statement is true you go left. If it is false you go right. You keep going until you reach a point with no arrows leaving it, and that point gives you the answer.

Josh uses three names for the parts of a tree. The box at the very top is the root node, or just the root. Boxes in the middle have arrows coming in and arrows going out, and they are called internal nodes or branches. Boxes with arrows coming in but none going out are leaves.

Trees are relaxed about data. One tree can mix numeric columns with yes or no columns. The same column can appear twice, with a different threshold each time. The same answer can also appear in more than one leaf.

Step by step

The data has seven people. For each person we know if they love popcorn, if they love soda, their age, and whether they love the 1991 film Cool as Ice. The last column is what we want to predict.

  1. Pick a candidate question, for example "Loves Popcorn?". Build a tiny tree with just that question and two leaves.
  2. Run all seven rows down the tiny tree. Count how many people in each leaf love Cool as Ice, and how many do not.
  3. A leaf that holds a mixture of both answers is called impure. Measure the impurity of each leaf with Gini impurity.
  4. Combine the two leaf scores into one number for the split. Use a weighted average, because leaves rarely hold the same number of people.
  5. Repeat for every candidate. For a numeric column such as age, sort the rows, take the average of each adjacent pair, and score each of those averages as a threshold.
  6. Put the candidate with the lowest total Gini impurity at the root. Here that is "Loves Soda", with 0.214.
  7. Repeat the whole search inside each impure node. Stop when a node is pure, or when splitting no longer helps. Then give each leaf the answer held by most of its people.

Formulas

Gini impurity of one leaf
$$ G_{\text{leaf}} = 1 - p_{\text{yes}}^{2} - p_{\text{no}}^{2} $$

Symbols: \(G_{\text{leaf}}\) is the impurity score of a single leaf. \(p_{\text{yes}}\) is the share of people in that leaf who love Cool as Ice. \(p_{\text{no}}\) is the share who do not. Each share is a count divided by the total number of people in the leaf. What it tells you: it is 0 when the leaf holds one answer only, and it grows as the leaf becomes more mixed.

Total Gini impurity of a split
$$ G_{\text{split}} = \frac{n_L}{n_L + n_R}\,G_L + \frac{n_R}{n_L + n_R}\,G_R $$

Symbols: \(n_L\) and \(n_R\) are the numbers of people in the left and right leaves. \(G_L\) and \(G_R\) are their Gini impurities. What it tells you: it is a weighted average, so a big leaf counts more than a small one. Compare this single number across candidates and keep the smallest.

Candidate thresholds for a numeric column
$$ t_j = \frac{a_j + a_{j+1}}{2} $$

Symbols: \(a_j\) is the \(j\)-th age after sorting the rows from lowest to highest. \(t_j\) is the midpoint between two neighbouring ages. What it tells you: a numeric column has no natural yes or no split, so you test the midpoint of every adjacent pair as a threshold.

Worked example from the video

Example

Take the "Loves Popcorn" tree. Four people love popcorn, and among them one loves Cool as Ice and three do not. So the left leaf scores:

$$ G_L = 1 - \left(\tfrac{1}{4}\right)^{2} - \left(\tfrac{3}{4}\right)^{2} = 1 - 0.0625 - 0.5625 = 0.375 $$

Three people do not love popcorn. Two of them love Cool as Ice and one does not, so the right leaf scores:

$$ G_R = 1 - \left(\tfrac{2}{3}\right)^{2} - \left(\tfrac{1}{3}\right)^{2} = 0.444 $$

The leaves hold four and three people, so we take the weighted average:

$$ G_{\text{split}} = \tfrac{4}{7}(0.375) + \tfrac{3}{7}(0.444) = 0.405 $$

The same work on "Loves Soda" gives 0.214. For age, we sort the rows and test each midpoint. The threshold \(\text{Age} < 9.5\) sends one person left, and that leaf is pure, so \(G_L = 0\). The other six go right and give \(G_R = 0.5\), so the total is \(\tfrac{6}{7}(0.5) = 0.429\). Two age thresholds, 15 and 44, tie for the best age score of 0.343.

Loves Soda has the lowest score of all, so it goes at the root. The four soda lovers form an impure node with three yes and one no. Popcorn splits them at 0.25, but \(\text{Age} < 12.5\) splits them at 0, so age wins. The three people who do not love soda already agree, so that node is a leaf. Each leaf takes the answer held by most of its people.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out Do not add raw leaf impurities together. The total for a split is a weighted average, with weights equal to the share of people in each leaf. Without the weights, a tiny pure leaf would look far more valuable than it is.
Watch out A leaf built from one person is a warning sign, and this tree has one. That is overfitting. The two cures are pruning the finished tree, or limiting growth, such as three people per leaf. A limit leaves impure leaves, and that is fine: a leaf that is 75 percent one-sided still tells you how sure it is. Pick the limit with cross validation.
Watch out Gini impurity is not the only option. Entropy and information gain do a similar job, and the numbers come out quite similar. Josh uses Gini because it is popular and the most straightforward.

If they ask you in an interview

How does a decision tree choose which feature to split on?
It treats every feature as a candidate question and scores each one. We send the training rows down a one-level split, then measure how mixed the two groups are with Gini impurity. The two leaves are combined with a weighted average. The lowest total impurity wins, and we repeat the search inside each impure child.
What exactly is Gini impurity?
It is one minus the sum of the squared class probabilities inside a group. If every person in the group has the same label, the score is zero, which means the group is pure. The more evenly the labels are mixed, the higher the score.
How does a tree handle a numeric feature such as age?
It turns the number into a set of yes or no questions. You sort the rows by that feature, take the midpoint of each adjacent pair of values, and treat each midpoint as a candidate threshold. Then you score every threshold with Gini impurity, exactly like a categorical split. Ties can happen, and either tied threshold is acceptable.
Why do decision trees overfit, and what do you do about it?
A tree keeps splitting until its leaves are pure, so leaves can end up holding one or two rows. Those leaves memorise noise. You either prune the tree, or limit its growth, for example with at least three people per leaf. Cross validation picks the limit.
What is the difference between a classification tree and a regression tree?
A classification tree sorts things into categories, such as loves or does not love a film. A regression tree predicts a number, such as mouse size from diet. In a classification tree, each leaf outputs the category held by most of its people.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • A decision tree makes a statement and moves left when it is true and right when it is false.
  • A tree that predicts categories is a classification tree, and one that predicts numbers is a regression tree.
  • The parts have names: the root at the top, internal nodes or branches in the middle, and leaves at the ends.
  • A leaf that mixes two answers is impure, and Gini impurity puts a number on that mixture.
  • The total score of a split is the weighted average of its leaf impurities, so leaf size matters.
  • Numeric features are handled by sorting the values and testing the midpoint of each adjacent pair.
  • The feature with the lowest total impurity goes at the top, and the search then repeats inside each impure node.
  • Leaves built from very few rows signal overfitting, so use pruning or a minimum leaf size chosen by cross validation.
