The SoftMax Derivative, Step-by-Step

▶ Watch on YouTube Also in: Neural Networks / Deep Learning (video 11)

This video derives the derivative of the SoftMax function, one algebra step at a time. You need these derivatives to train a neural network that ends with a SoftMax layer.

In one breath

Josh uses the iris network that predicts setosa, versicolor, or virginica. The SoftMax output for setosa is the predicted probability for setosa. With the quotient rule, its derivative with respect to its own raw output is \(p_s(1-p_s)\). Its derivative with respect to another class's raw output is \(-p_s\,p_{\mathit{ve}}\) or \(-p_s\,p_{\mathit{vi}}\). With the video's numbers, these give 0.21, −0.07, and −0.15.

The main idea

The network gives three raw output values, one for each iris species. These values are all over the place, so a SoftMax layer turns them into predicted probabilities. The SoftMax value for setosa is a fraction. Its numerator is \(e\) raised to the setosa raw value. Its denominator is the sum of \(e\) raised to all three raw values.

All three raw values sit in the denominator, so all three change the setosa probability. That means we need three derivatives: one for setosa's own raw value, and one each for versicolor and virginica.

Step by step

  1. Write the SoftMax for setosa as a fraction. This fraction equals the predicted probability for setosa.
  2. Apply the quotient rule, because the probability is one function divided by another.
  3. For setosa's own raw value, split the result into pieces. Each piece is a probability or 1, which gives \(p_s(1-p_s)\).
  4. For versicolor's raw value, the numerator's derivative is zero. Splitting what is left gives \(-p_s\,p_{\mathit{ve}}\).
  5. Virginica is left as homework. It works the same way and gives \(-p_s\,p_{\mathit{vi}}\).

Formulas

SoftMax for setosa
$$ p_s = \frac{e^{s}}{e^{s} + e^{\mathit{ve}} + e^{\mathit{vi}}} $$

Symbols: \(s\), \(\mathit{ve}\), \(\mathit{vi}\) are the raw output values for setosa, versicolor, and virginica. \(e\) is Euler's number, about 2.718. \(p_s\) is the predicted probability for setosa. What it tells you: every raw value appears in the denominator, so every raw value affects \(p_s\).

Quotient rule
$$ \frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{f}{g}\right) = \frac{f'\,g - g'\,f}{g^{2}} $$

Symbols: \(f\) is the numerator and \(g\) is the denominator. \(f'\) and \(g'\) are their derivatives with respect to the variable \(x\). What it tells you: take the numerator's derivative times the denominator, subtract the denominator's derivative times the numerator, and divide by the denominator squared.

Derivative with respect to its own raw value
$$ \frac{\partial p_s}{\partial s} = \frac{e^{s}\,(e^{s}+e^{\mathit{ve}}+e^{\mathit{vi}}) - e^{s}\,e^{s}}{(e^{s}+e^{\mathit{ve}}+e^{\mathit{vi}})^{2}} $$

This is the quotient rule with \(f' = g' = e^{s}\).

$$ = \frac{e^{s}}{e^{s}+e^{\mathit{ve}}+e^{\mathit{vi}}}\left(\frac{e^{s}+e^{\mathit{ve}}+e^{\mathit{vi}}}{e^{s}+e^{\mathit{ve}}+e^{\mathit{vi}}} - \frac{e^{s}}{e^{s}+e^{\mathit{ve}}+e^{\mathit{vi}}}\right) $$

We pull \(e^{s}\) out of the numerator and split the rest into two terms.

$$ = p_s\,(1 - p_s) $$

The outside fraction and the last term are both \(p_s\), and the middle term is 1. What it tells you: the slope of a class's probability with respect to its own raw value depends only on that probability.

Derivative with respect to another class's raw value
$$ \frac{\partial p_s}{\partial \mathit{ve}} = \frac{0\cdot(e^{s}+e^{\mathit{ve}}+e^{\mathit{vi}}) - e^{\mathit{ve}}\,e^{s}}{(e^{s}+e^{\mathit{ve}}+e^{\mathit{vi}})^{2}} $$

The numerator \(e^{s}\) has no versicolor term, so its derivative is 0 and the first term drops out.

$$ = -\frac{e^{s}}{e^{s}+e^{\mathit{ve}}+e^{\mathit{vi}}}\cdot\frac{e^{\mathit{ve}}}{e^{s}+e^{\mathit{ve}}+e^{\mathit{vi}}} = -p_s\,p_{\mathit{ve}} $$

Splitting the fraction gives two familiar pieces: \(p_s\) with a minus sign, and \(p_{\mathit{ve}}\), the predicted probability for versicolor. What it tells you: for virginica the same steps give \(-p_s\,p_{\mathit{vi}}\).

Worked example from the video

Example

In the iris network, the predicted probability for setosa is 0.69 and for versicolor is 0.1.

With respect to setosa: \(0.69 \times (1 - 0.69) = 0.69 \times 0.31 \approx 0.21\).

With respect to versicolor: \(-0.69 \times 0.1 \approx -0.07\).

With respect to virginica: \(-p_s\,p_{\mathit{vi}}\) gives about \(-0.15\).

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out Do not stop at the derivative with respect to setosa's own raw value. The versicolor and virginica raw values sit in the denominator, so they also change the setosa probability.
Watch out When the derivative is with respect to versicolor, the numerator \(e^{s}\) has a derivative of zero. The whole first part of the quotient rule disappears.
Watch out The two cases have different forms. The own-class case is \(p_s(1-p_s)\). The other-class case is \(-p_s\,p_j\), with a minus sign.

If they ask you in an interview

What is the derivative of a SoftMax output with respect to its own input?
It is the predicted probability times one minus that probability, \(p_i(1-p_i)\). You get it with the quotient rule and a little algebra. For a setosa probability of 0.69, it is about 0.21.
And with respect to another class's input?
It is minus the product of the two probabilities, \(-p_i\,p_j\). The numerator does not contain the other input, so only the denominator term of the quotient rule survives. With 0.69 and 0.1, that is about −0.07.
Why do we need derivatives with respect to every raw output?
Every raw output appears in the SoftMax denominator. So every raw output changes each predicted probability. To train the network, we need all of these derivatives, not just the one for the matching class.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • The SoftMax output for a class is its predicted probability.
  • It is a fraction, so its derivative needs the quotient rule.
  • With respect to its own raw value, the derivative is \(p_s(1-p_s)\).
  • With respect to another class's raw value, the derivative is \(-p_s\,p_j\).
  • In the iris example, the three derivatives are 0.21, −0.07, and −0.15.
