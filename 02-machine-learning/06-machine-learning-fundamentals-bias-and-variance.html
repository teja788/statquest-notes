Machine Learning Fundamentals: Bias and Variance

▶ Watch on YouTube

Bias and variance are the two ways a model can let you down. Josh shows both with one small data set of mouse weights and heights.

In one breath

We measure the weight and height of many mice. Light mice are short, heavier mice are taller, then the growth flattens out. Josh splits the data into a training set and a testing set. He fits a straight line and a squiggly line. The squiggly line wins on the training data and loses badly on the testing data. Bias is missing the true shape. Variance is the score changing a lot between data sets.

The main idea

We want to predict mouse height from mouse weight. The true relationship is a curve that rises and then flattens. Very heavy mice get fatter, not taller. In real work we never know that curve. Josh leaves it in the picture so we can judge each model.

He splits the data first. The training set is used to fit the models. The testing set is held back, so it behaves like future data.

Linear regression fits a straight line to the training set. A straight line cannot bend, so it never follows the arc. This inability to capture the true relationship is called bias. The straight line has a lot of bias.

The second method fits a squiggly line. It is very flexible, so it hugs the arc and touches every training point. Its bias is very small. That same flexibility causes its problem later.

Step by step

  1. Plot weight against height. The points rise and then level off, so the true relationship is an arc.
  2. Split the data. Blue dots are the training set, green dots are the testing set.
  3. Fit a straight line to the training set. It misses the arc, and that miss is bias.
  4. Fit a squiggly line. It reaches every training point, so its bias is very small.
  5. Score both fits on the training set with sums of squares. The squiggly line wins.
  6. Score both fits on the testing set. Now the straight line wins. That change between data sets is variance.
  7. Aim for the sweet spot between simple and complex. Regularization, boosting and bagging are the three usual tools.

Formulas

Sum of squares
$$ \mathrm{SS} \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left( y_i - \hat{y}_i \right)^{2} $$

Symbols: \(n\) is the number of mice. \(y_i\) is the real height of mouse \(i\). \(\hat{y}_i\) is the height the fitted line predicts for that mouse. We square each distance so negative distances do not cancel positive ones. What it tells you: one number for how far the line sits from the data. Smaller is a better fit.

Reading bias and variance from the scores
$$ \text{bias} \;\approx\; \mathrm{SS}_{\text{train}} \qquad\qquad \text{variance} \;\approx\; \mathrm{SS}_{\text{test}} - \mathrm{SS}_{\text{train}} $$

Symbols: \(\mathrm{SS}_{\text{train}}\) is the sum of squares on the training set. \(\mathrm{SS}_{\text{test}}\) is the same sum on the testing set. What it tells you: this is a rule of thumb, not a proof. High training error means high bias. A big jump on new data means high variance.

Worked example from the video

Example

On the training set the squiggly line passes through every blue dot. Every distance is zero, so its sum of squares is \(0^2 + 0^2 + \dots + 0^2 = 0\). The straight line leaves a gap at each dot, so its sum is larger. The squiggly line wins this first contest.

Josh then repeats the calculation on the green testing points. Both lines stay exactly as they were. The straight line's sum of squares stays close to before. The squiggly line now sits far from many testing points, so its sum becomes very large. The straight line wins this second contest.

So the squiggly line has low bias and high variance, and we call it overfit. The straight line has high bias and low variance. Its predictions are good rather than great, but they are consistently good.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out Bias here does not mean the intercept term, and it does not mean unfair data. It means the model cannot match the true relationship.
Watch out A perfect training score is a warning sign, not a prize. A sum of squares of zero usually means the model memorised the points.
Watch out Variance here is not the spread of one column of numbers. It is how much the fit changes when you give the model different data.

If they ask you in an interview

What are bias and variance, in plain words?
Bias is how much a method fails to capture the true relationship. A straight line fitted to a curve has high bias, because it can never bend. Variance is how much the fit changes when the data changes. A flexible model can score perfectly on one data set and terribly on the next.
How would you spot overfitting?
I compare the error on the training data with the error on held-out testing data. If the training error is tiny and the testing error is much larger, the model is overfit.
Why does a flexible model usually have higher variance?
It bends to match noise as well as signal. The noise is different in every sample, so the fitted shape changes a lot between data sets. Future performance then becomes hard to predict.
What do you do about high variance?
I look for the sweet spot between a simple model and a complex one. The three common tools are regularization, boosting and bagging. Random forests are an example of bagging.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • We split the mouse data into a training set for fitting and a testing set for judging.
  • Bias is a method's inability to reproduce the true relationship, so the straight line has high bias.
  • The squiggly line is flexible enough to follow the arc, so its bias is very low.
  • We compare fits with sums of squares, the squared vertical distances from the points to the line.
  • The squiggly line scores zero on the training set and then loses badly on the testing set.
  • That large difference between data sets is variance, and such a model is called overfit.
  • The ideal method has low bias and low variance, so it is accurate and consistent.
  • Regularization, boosting and bagging are the three usual ways to find that sweet spot.
