Entropy (for data science)

▶ Watch on YouTube

Entropy is a single number that says how mixed a set of outcomes is. Josh builds it from a simpler idea called surprise, so you can derive the formula instead of memorising it.

In one breath

Surprise is high when something is unlikely and low when it is likely. Josh defines surprise as the log of one over the probability, because that gives zero surprise for a certain event. Entropy is then the expected value of surprise, which means the average surprise per draw. He works it out for a biased coin and gets 0.47, then for three groups of chickens. Entropy is highest when the two colours are balanced, and it falls as one colour takes over.

The main idea

Josh starts with chickens, not equations. Imagine orange and blue chickens sorted into three areas, A, B and C. If you reach into an area and grab a chicken at random, how startled would you be by the colour you pull out?

In area A, six of the seven chickens are orange. Grabbing an orange one is common, so it is barely surprising. Grabbing the single blue one is rare, so it is quite surprising. Area B is the mirror image, with many blue chickens and one orange. Area C has equal numbers, so both colours surprise you the same moderate amount.

That gives the first insight. Surprise moves in the opposite direction to probability. When a probability is low, the surprise is high. When a probability is high, the surprise is low.

A first idea is to use \( 1/p \) as the measure of surprise. It does grow as the probability shrinks, but it fails at the easy end. Josh uses a rigged coin that always lands heads. Here the probability is 1, so the surprise should be 0. Yet \( 1/1 = 1 \). So he takes the log of the inverse instead, and the log of 1 is 0.

Step by step

  1. Look at the three chicken areas. Surprise runs opposite to probability, so rare colours startle you and common ones do not.
  2. Reject \( 1/p \) as the measure of surprise. It gives 1 for a certain event, and a certain event should give 0.
  3. Define surprise as \( \log(1/p) \). It is 0 when the probability is 1, and it grows as the probability falls. With two outcomes we use log base 2.
  4. Check the impossible case. A probability of 0 gives the log of 0, which is undefined. That is fine, because the event never happens.
  5. Switch to a coin that gives heads 90% of the time. The surprise is 0.15 for heads and 3.32 for tails.
  6. Flip it three times and get heads, heads, tails. The log rules make the total surprise the sum of the three, 3.62.
  7. Estimate the total surprise over 100 flips. Multiply each surprise by its expected count, then add the two terms to get 46.7.
  8. Divide by 100 flips. The average surprise per flip is 0.47, and that is the entropy of the coin.
  9. Write the average in sigma notation and rearrange to reach Shannon's standard form. Then apply it to the chickens.

Formulas

Surprise
$$ \text{Surprise}(x) \;=\; \log_2\!\left(\frac{1}{p(x)}\right) $$

Symbols: \( x \) is one possible outcome, such as heads or an orange chicken. \( p(x) \) is the probability of that outcome. \( \log_2 \) is the logarithm in base 2, which is the usual choice when there are two outcomes. What it tells you: how startling one single observation is, measured in bits, with 0 for a sure thing.

Surprises add up
$$ \log_2\!\left(\frac{1}{p_1 p_2 p_3}\right) \;=\; \log_2\!\frac{1}{p_1} + \log_2\!\frac{1}{p_2} + \log_2\!\frac{1}{p_3} $$

Symbols: \( p_1, p_2, p_3 \) are the probabilities of three independent flips, whose joint probability is the product. What it tells you: the surprise of a sequence is the sum of the surprises of its parts, which is exactly why a log was the right choice.

Entropy as expected surprise
$$ H \;=\; \sum_{x} \log_2\!\left(\frac{1}{p(x)}\right) p(x) $$

Symbols: \( H \) is the entropy. The sum runs over every possible outcome \( x \). The first factor is the surprise of that outcome and \( p(x) \) is how often you expect to see it. What it tells you: the surprise you expect per draw, so it is a weighted average of the surprises.

Derivation to Shannon's form
$$ H \;=\; \sum_{x} p(x)\,\log_2\!\left(\frac{1}{p(x)}\right) $$

Step 1. Swap the order of the two factors so the probability comes first.

$$ H \;=\; \sum_{x} p(x)\,\Big[\log_2(1) - \log_2 p(x)\Big] $$

Step 2. Use the log rule that turns a fraction into a subtraction.

$$ H \;=\; \sum_{x} \Big[\,p(x)\cdot 0 \;-\; p(x)\log_2 p(x)\Big] $$

Step 3. The log of 1 is 0, and the probability multiplies both terms in the bracket.

$$ H \;=\; -\sum_{x} p(x)\,\log_2 p(x) $$

Step 4. Pull the minus sign outside the sum. Symbols: same as above, and the leading minus sign only exists because the logs of probabilities are negative. What it tells you: this is the equation Claude Shannon published in 1948. It is the same quantity as the expected surprise, only written more compactly.

Worked example from the video

Example

Take the coin that lands heads 90% of the time. Josh puts the probabilities and their surprises in a small table.

HeadsTails
Probability0.90.1
Surprise0.153.32

Flip it 100 times. You expect about \( 0.9 \times 100 = 90 \) heads, each worth 0.15 of surprise, and about \( 0.1 \times 100 = 10 \) tails, each worth 3.32. The total expected surprise is 46.7.

$$ \frac{(0.9 \times 100 \times 0.15) + (0.1 \times 100 \times 3.32)}{100} \;=\; \frac{46.7}{100} \;=\; 0.47 $$

The 100s cancel, and what is left is \( 0.15 \times 0.9 + 3.32 \times 0.1 = 0.47 \). That is the entropy of this coin: the surprise you expect from one flip.

Example

Now the chickens. Area A holds six orange and one blue, so the probabilities are 6/7 and 1/7.

$$ H_A = \frac{6}{7}\log_2\!\frac{7}{6} + \frac{1}{7}\log_2\!\frac{7}{1} = 0.59 $$

The blue chicken is very surprising, but it is so rare that the answer sits close to the small surprise of the orange chickens. Area B has one orange and ten blue.

$$ H_B = \frac{1}{11}\log_2\!\frac{11}{1} + \frac{10}{11}\log_2\!\frac{11}{10} = 0.44 $$

Area C has equal numbers of both colours, so every draw carries the same moderate surprise of 1, and the entropy is 1. No small surprise pulls the average down. That makes area C the most uncertain of the three.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out

Do not use \( 1/p \) as a measure of surprise, even though it points the right way. A guaranteed outcome would score 1 instead of 0. The log fixes that, and it also makes surprises add across independent events.

Watch out

The surprise of an impossible outcome is undefined. Josh says that is acceptable, because the event never happens.

Watch out

Entropy is not the plain average of the two surprise values. Each surprise is weighted by its own probability. In area A the blue chicken has a surprise of 2.81, yet the entropy is only 0.59.

Watch out

The minus sign in Shannon's form is bookkeeping, not meaning. It appears because \( \log_2 p \) is negative for probabilities below 1. If you get confused, use the expected surprise version instead.

If they ask you in an interview

What is entropy, in plain words?
Entropy is the surprise you expect from a single random draw. Surprise is high for rare outcomes and zero for certain ones. So entropy is a weighted average of surprise, where each outcome is weighted by how often it happens. It tells you how mixed a set of outcomes is.
Why is surprise defined with a logarithm?
There are two reasons. A certain event must score zero surprise, and the log of one is zero, while the plain inverse gives one. The log also turns products into sums, so the surprise of a sequence is the sum of the individual surprises.
When is entropy highest and when is it lowest?
For two categories, entropy is highest when they are balanced at fifty-fifty, which gives one bit. It falls as one category takes over, and it reaches zero when everything is one category. That is why a classification tree prefers splits that push each child towards one class.
Where does entropy show up in data science?
It is used to choose splits when building classification trees. It is also the base of mutual information, which quantifies the relationship between two things. And it underlies relative entropy, the Kullback-Leibler distance, and cross-entropy, which appear in t-SNE and UMAP.
Why base 2, and does the base matter?
Base 2 is customary with two outcomes, and it makes the answer read in bits, so a fair coin scores exactly one. Changing the base only multiplies every entropy by a constant, so comparisons are unaffected.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • Surprise moves opposite to probability: rare outcomes surprise you, common ones do not.
  • Surprise is the log of one over the probability, so a certain outcome scores zero.
  • Because it is a log, the surprise of a sequence is the sum of the individual surprises.
  • Entropy is the expected value of surprise, meaning the average surprise per draw.
  • The coin that lands heads 90% of the time has an entropy of 0.47.
  • Rearranging the expected surprise gives Shannon's 1948 form, with the minus sign in front.
  • Areas A, B and C of chickens give entropies of 0.59, 0.44 and 1.
  • Entropy peaks when the categories are balanced and drops as one category dominates.
