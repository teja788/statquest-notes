One-Hot, Label, Target and K-Fold Target Encoding

▶ Watch on YouTube

Many popular algorithms, including neural networks and XGBoost, do not work well with discrete columns. This video shows four ways to turn a discrete column into numbers, and how to reduce data leakage.

In one breath

One-hot encoding makes one new column per option, which fails when the options are many. Label encoding uses one number per option, which is compact but invents an order. Target encoding uses the mean of the target for each option, so the number carries signal. A weighted version pulls rare options toward the overall mean. Target encoding leaks the target, and k-fold target encoding reduces that leakage.

The main idea

Josh starts with a table of seven people. He uses favorite color and height to predict who loves Troll 2. Favorite color has three discrete values: blue, red and green. Discrete means the value is one of a fixed set of labels, not a number on a scale.

In theory most algorithms handle such a column. In practice many popular ones cannot, so we turn the labels into numbers first. One-hot encoding makes one 0/1 column per option. Label encoding gives each option a number. Target encoding replaces each option with the mean of the target for that option. The target is the thing we want to predict.

Step by step

  1. Count the options in the discrete column. Favorite color has three. The target, loves Troll 2, has two, so yes becomes 1 and no becomes 0.
  2. If the count is small, use one-hot encoding. Three options become three new columns of 0s and 1s.
  3. One-hot fails when the count is large. United States postal codes have 41,683 options, so one column becomes 41,683 columns.
  4. Try label encoding instead: blue is 0, red is 1, green is 2. It keeps one column, but the numbers are arbitrary.
  5. Use target encoding to make the numbers mean something. Of the three people who like blue, one loves Troll 2, so blue becomes 1/3 = 0.33. Only one person likes red and that person does not love Troll 2, so red becomes 0. Two of the three green people love Troll 2, so green becomes 0.67.
  6. Fix the confidence problem with a weighted mean. Red is based on one person only, so 0 is a weak estimate. The weighted version blends each option mean with the overall target mean, using a weight m you choose.
  7. Reduce leakage with k-fold target encoding. Split the data into k equal subsets. Encode each subset using only the target values from the other subsets.

The picture below shows what the weighted mean does. Blue and green have three rows each, so their values barely move. Red has one row, so it is pulled a long way toward the overall mean.

Josh shows k-fold target encoding with two folds. Seven rows do not split evenly, so subset A gets four rows and B gets three. A's values come from B's targets, and B's values come from A's targets.

Formulas

Plain target encoding
$$ \bar{y}_c \;=\; \frac{1}{n_c}\sum_{i \,\in\, c} y_i $$

Symbols: \(c\) is one option of the discrete column, for example blue. \(n_c\) is how many rows have that option. \(y_i\) is the target value of row \(i\), here 1 for loves Troll 2 and 0 for not. What it tells you: each label becomes the share of its rows with a target of 1.

Weighted target encoding
$$ \text{encoding}(c) \;=\; \frac{n_c \cdot \bar{y}_c \;+\; m \cdot \bar{y}}{n_c + m} $$

Symbols: \(n_c\) is the number of rows with option \(c\). \(\bar{y}_c\) is the mean of the target for those rows. \(\bar{y}\) is the overall mean of the target across all rows, here \(3/7\). \(m\) is a weight you choose, called a hyperparameter; the video uses \(m = 2\). What it tells you: the encoding blends the option mean with the overall mean. The option mean wins only when enough rows support it.

With \(m = 2\), an option needs at least three rows before its own mean counts for more than the overall mean. Think of the overall mean as the best guess with no data. Because it blends a guess with data, some people call this Bayesian mean encoding.

K-fold version
$$ \text{encoding}(c \mid \text{fold } k) \;=\; \frac{n_c^{(-k)} \cdot \bar{y}_c^{(-k)} \;+\; m \cdot \bar{y}^{(-k)}}{n_c^{(-k)} + m} $$

Symbols: the superscript \((-k)\) means "computed from every fold except fold \(k\)". The other symbols mean the same as above. What it tells you: it is the same blend, but no row uses its own target value, which reduces the leakage.

Worked example from the video

Example

Seven people. Three of them love Troll 2, so the overall mean is \(\bar{y} = 3/7 \approx 0.43\). Three people like blue and one of them loves Troll 2, so \(\bar{y}_{\text{blue}} = 1/3\) and \(n = 3\). With \(m = 2\):

$$ \text{blue} \;=\; \frac{3 \cdot \tfrac{1}{3} \;+\; 2 \cdot \tfrac{3}{7}}{3 + 2} \;=\; \frac{1 + 0.857}{5} \;=\; 0.37 $$

One person likes red and does not love Troll 2, so \(\bar{y}_{\text{red}} = 0\) and \(n = 1\):

$$ \text{red} \;=\; \frac{1 \cdot 0 \;+\; 2 \cdot \tfrac{3}{7}}{1 + 2} \;=\; \frac{0.857}{3} \;=\; 0.29 $$

Two of the three green people love Troll 2, so \(\bar{y}_{\text{green}} = 2/3\) and \(n = 3\), which gives 0.57. Compare these with the plain means of 0.33, 0.00 and 0.67. Blue and green hardly moved, but red jumped from 0.00 to 0.29.

Now the two-fold version, for the blue rows in subset A. We ignore A's targets and use subset B. In B one person likes blue and does not love Troll 2, so \(\bar{y}_{\text{blue}} = 0/1\) and \(n = 1\). One of B's three people loves Troll 2, so \(\bar{y} = 1/3\). With \(m = 2\):

$$ \text{blue in A} \;=\; \frac{1 \cdot 0 \;+\; 2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{3}}{1 + 2} \;=\; \frac{0.667}{3} \;=\; 0.22 $$

Red in A is a special case. Subset B has nobody who likes red, so \(\bar{y}_c = 0\) and \(n = 0\). The formula still works and returns the overall mean of B, which gives 0.33.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out Label encoding invents an order. Some algorithms treat the numbers as if the order means something. A decision tree would be forced to group red with green, or blue with red. That grouping comes from our arbitrary numbers, not from the data.
Watch out Target encoding uses the target to build a feature, and that is data leakage. Leakage gives a model that works well on training data but poorly on testing data. In other words, it overfits. K-fold target encoding is one popular way to reduce it.
Watch out A rare option gives a fragile mean. Red was based on one person, so its plain mean of 0 means little. The weight \(m\) pulls such options toward the overall mean.

If they ask you in an interview

When would you choose target encoding over one-hot encoding?
I choose target encoding when the column has many distinct options. One-hot on United States postal codes would add 41,683 columns, which is hard to work with. Target encoding keeps one numeric column that carries signal about the target. The cost is that I must manage leakage.
What is wrong with label encoding for a nominal feature?
Label encoding assigns arbitrary numbers, and some algorithms read those numbers as an order. A decision tree splitting on that column must group options that sit next to each other numerically. That grouping reflects my numbering, not any real structure in the data.
Why do we use a weighted mean in target encoding, and what does m control?
An option with only a few rows gives a noisy mean, so I should not trust it fully. The weighted mean blends the option mean with the overall target mean. With m equal to 2, an option needs at least three rows before its own mean counts for more. So m is a hyperparameter that sets how far rare options are pulled toward the overall mean.
How does k-fold target encoding reduce leakage?
I split the data into k subsets and encode each one using only the target values from the other subsets. That way no row uses its own target value for its encoding. If k equals the number of rows, it is called leave-one-out target encoding. Some people also have success with k equal to five.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • Many algorithms need numbers, so discrete columns must be converted first.
  • One-hot encoding makes one 0/1 column per option, but it fails when there are many options.
  • Label encoding is compact but assigns arbitrary numbers that algorithms may read as an order.
  • Target encoding uses the target mean per option: blue 0.33, red 0.00 and green 0.67.
  • The weighted version blends in the overall mean with weight m, giving 0.37, 0.29 and 0.57.
  • Target encoding leaks the target, so k-fold encoding computes each subset from the other subsets.
  • Leave-one-out is the case where k equals the number of rows.
