Logistic Regression Details Pt 3: R-squared and p-value

▶ Watch on YouTube

Part 2 gave us the best fitting squiggle. This video answers the next question: is that squiggle actually useful?

In one breath

For linear models we score a fit with sums of squares. Logistic regression cannot do that, because its residuals are infinite. So we swap sums of squares for log likelihoods. We compare the log likelihood of the fitted squiggle with the log likelihood of a model that ignores weight. That comparison gives McFadden's pseudo R-squared, which measures effect size. Twice the same difference is a chi-squared value, which gives the p-value.

The main idea

First, a warning from Josh. Everyone agrees how to compute R-squared for linear models. Nobody agrees for logistic regression, where more than ten definitions compete. So look at what people in your own field already report. This video uses McFadden's pseudo R-squared, because it is common and easy to read off software output.

Now the quick review. In linear regression we measure residuals, the vertical gaps from each point to a line. We square them and add them up. Around the best fitting line that total is SS(fit), a measure of a good fit. Around the flat mean line it is SS(mean), a measure of a bad fit. R-squared is the share of the variation around the mean that the line removes. It runs from 0 to 1.

Logistic regression puts log odds on the y-axis, so the raw data sit at plus and minus infinity. Every residual is therefore infinite and useless. But we still have a score for any candidate squiggle: the log likelihood of the data. So we replace SS(fit) with LL(fit), the log likelihood of the data under the fitted squiggle. And we replace SS(mean) with LL(overall probability), the log likelihood under a model that uses no weight at all.

Step by step

  1. Fit the log odds line with maximum likelihood, exactly as in Part 2. This gives the best fitting squiggle on the probability scale.
  2. Project each mouse onto that squiggle and read off its predicted probability. Add up the logs of those probabilities. That total is LL(fit), and here it equals -3.77.
  3. Now build the "no weight" model. Take the total number of obese mice divided by the total number of mice that are not obese, then take the log. That gives a flat log odds line at 0.22.
  4. Translate that flat log odds line back to probability. It becomes a flat line at p = 0.56, which is simply the overall probability of obesity. Both routes give the same number.
  5. Project the mice onto that flat line and add the logs again. That total is LL(overall probability), and here it equals -6.18.
  6. Combine the two totals with the R-squared formula. That gives McFadden's pseudo R-squared, which is 0.39 for this data.
  7. Double the gap between the two log likelihoods. That number is a chi-squared statistic, and its tail area is the p-value.

Before trusting this R-squared, Josh checks that it behaves. He builds data where weight predicts nothing: light and heavy mice are obese in equal measure. The best fitting line has a slope of almost exactly zero, so LL(fit) equals -6.18, the same as the baseline. The formula returns zero. He then builds data where weight predicts obesity perfectly. Every predicted probability is 1 for the outcome that happened, and the log of 1 is zero. So LL(fit) is zero and the formula returns one.

This shows a useful habit of log likelihoods. They are always negative or zero, because we take logs of probabilities between 0 and 1. A good fit sits close to zero.

The p-value uses the same two numbers. Twice the difference between LL(fit) and LL(overall probability) follows a chi-squared distribution. The degrees of freedom equal the difference in the number of parameters. The fitted model has an intercept and a slope, so two. The baseline model has only an intercept, so one. That leaves one degree of freedom.

Formulas

R-squared for a linear model (the pattern to copy)
$$ R^2 = \frac{SS(\text{mean}) - SS(\text{fit})}{SS(\text{mean})} $$

Symbols: \(SS(\text{mean})\) is the sum of squared distances from the points to the flat mean line, a bad fit. \(SS(\text{fit})\) is the sum of squared distances to the best fitting line, a good fit. What it tells you: the fraction of the variation around the mean that the line removes.

The overall log odds of obesity
$$ \log(\text{odds}) = \log\!\left(\frac{\text{number of obese mice}}{\text{number of mice that are not obese}}\right) $$

Symbols: the two counts are simple head counts, ignoring weight completely. What it tells you: the height of the flat baseline log odds line, which was 0.22 here and became p = 0.56 on the probability scale.

McFadden's pseudo R-squared
$$ R^2 = \frac{LL(\text{overall probability}) - LL(\text{fit})}{LL(\text{overall probability})} $$

Symbols: \(LL(\text{fit})\) is the log likelihood of the data under the fitted squiggle. \(LL(\text{overall probability})\) is the log likelihood under the flat baseline that ignores weight. What it tells you: the effect size, on a scale from 0 for a useless predictor to 1 for a perfect one.

The p-value
$$ 2\big(LL(\text{fit}) - LL(\text{overall probability})\big) \sim \chi^2_{\,df}, \qquad df = 2 - 1 = 1 $$

Symbols: the two log likelihoods are the same ones as above. \(\chi^2\) is the chi-squared distribution, and \(df\) is its degrees of freedom, here the fitted model's two parameters minus the baseline model's one parameter. What it tells you: the tail area beyond this value is the p-value, so a bigger gap between the models means a smaller p-value.

The version you will meet in textbooks
$$ R^2 = \frac{2\big(LL(\text{saturated}) - LL(\text{overall})\big) - 2\big(LL(\text{saturated}) - LL(\text{fit})\big)}{2\big(LL(\text{saturated}) - LL(\text{overall})\big)} $$

Symbols: \(LL(\text{saturated})\) is the log likelihood of a model with one parameter per data point. What it tells you: the same thing as the simple formula. For logistic regression \(LL(\text{saturated}) = 0\), so every saturated term cancels. Other generalized linear models keep it, because there it is not zero.

Worked example from the video

Example

We predict obesity in mice from their weight. The best fitting squiggle gives a log likelihood of LL(fit) = -3.77. Ignoring weight, the overall probability of obesity is 0.56, and that flat line gives LL(overall probability) = -6.18.

The effect size:

$$ R^2 = \frac{-6.18 - (-3.77)}{-6.18} = \frac{-2.41}{-6.18} = 0.39 $$

The significance test:

$$ \chi^2 = 2\big(-3.77 - (-6.18)\big) = 2 \times 2.41 = 4.82 $$

With one degree of freedom, a chi-squared value of 4.82 gives p = 0.03. So the relationship between weight and obesity is unlikely to be due to chance, and R-squared of 0.39 tells us how strong that relationship is.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out There is no single R-squared for logistic regression. More than ten versions exist and they give different numbers on the same data. Always say which one you used, and check what your field normally reports.
Watch out Do not try to compute residuals on the log odds scale. The data points sit at plus and minus infinity there, so every residual is infinite. Log likelihood exists precisely to replace them.
Watch out Keep the two ideas apart. R-squared reports effect size, meaning how much the predictor helps. The p-value reports significance, meaning how likely the result is by chance.

If they ask you in an interview

Why can't we use the usual R-squared for logistic regression?
The usual R-squared is built from residuals. In logistic regression the y-axis holds log odds, so the observed points sit at plus and minus infinity and every residual is infinite. We need a different score for a fit, so we use the log likelihood of the data. McFadden's pseudo R-squared then compares the fitted model's log likelihood with that of a model using no predictors.
How do you compute McFadden's pseudo R-squared?
You need two log likelihoods. One is for the fitted model, found by summing the logs of its predicted probabilities. The other is for the null model, which uses only the overall probability of the outcome. R-squared is the difference between them divided by the null log likelihood. It is 0 when the predictor adds nothing and 1 when the fit is perfect.
Where does the p-value come from?
It is a likelihood ratio test. Twice the difference between the two log likelihoods follows a chi-squared distribution. The degrees of freedom equal the difference in the number of parameters between the two models. For one predictor that is two parameters against one, so one degree of freedom. The tail area beyond the observed value is the p-value.
Why are log likelihoods always negative here?
Every term is the log of a predicted probability, and probabilities lie between 0 and 1. The log of a number in that range is negative or zero. So the total runs from minus infinity up to zero. A good model pushes it towards zero, and a poor model leaves it as a large negative number.
Textbook formulas include a saturated model. Why does it disappear here?
The saturated model has one parameter per observation, so it fits the data perfectly. For logistic regression its log likelihood is zero, so those terms cancel out. Other generalized linear models have a non-zero saturated log likelihood, so the general formulas keep the term.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • Logistic regression has infinite residuals, so sums of squares cannot score a fit.
  • Log likelihoods take their place: LL(fit) for the fitted squiggle, LL(overall probability) for a model that ignores the predictor.
  • McFadden's pseudo R-squared is their difference divided by LL(overall probability).
  • It behaves like the familiar R-squared, running from 0 for a useless predictor to 1 for a perfect one.
  • In the mouse example LL(fit) is -3.77 and LL(overall probability) is -6.18, which gives R-squared of 0.39.
  • Twice that same difference is 4.82, a chi-squared value with one degree of freedom, and the p-value is 0.03.
  • General formulas add a saturated model term, but that term is zero for logistic regression and drops out.
  • There is no agreed R-squared for logistic regression, so state which version you report.
