Gradient Descent, Step-by-Step

▶ Watch on YouTube Also in: Neural Networks / Deep Learning (video 4)

Gradient Descent finds the best values for parameters by walking downhill on a loss curve. The same method fits a line, a logistic regression squiggle, t-SNE clusters and much more.

In one breath

We choose a loss function that scores how badly a model fits the data. Here the loss is the sum of the squared residuals. We take the derivative of that loss with respect to each parameter. We start from a random guess and step downhill, where each step is the slope times a small learning rate. The steps get smaller as the slope flattens, and we stop when the step size is tiny.

The main idea

Josh starts with three people. Each person has a weight and a height. A straight line through the points predicts height from weight. The line has two parameters: the intercept and the slope. To keep things simple at first, Josh fixes the slope at the least squares value, 0.64, and asks Gradient Descent to find only the intercept.

To judge a line we need a score. Here the score is the sum of the squared residuals. A residual is the observed height minus the predicted height. In machine learning we call such a score a loss function. If we plot this loss against every possible intercept, we get a bowl shaped curve. The best intercept sits at the bottom of the bowl.

One slow method is to try many intercepts and keep the best. Gradient Descent is smarter. It uses the slope of the curve to decide how far to move. Far from the bottom the curve is steep, so it takes a big step. Near the bottom the curve is almost flat, so it takes a baby step. That is why it settles neatly on the answer instead of jumping past it.

Step by step

  1. Choose a loss function. Here it is the sum of the squared residuals for the three people.
  2. Take the derivative of the loss with respect to every parameter. With two or more derivatives of the same function, the set is called the gradient.
  3. Pick random starting values. Josh sets the intercept to 0, and later sets the slope to 1.
  4. Plug the current values into the derivatives. This gives the slope of the loss curve at that point.
  5. Compute the step size. Multiply the slope by the learning rate, a small number that keeps the step safe.
  6. Compute the new parameter: old value minus the step size. Then go back to step 4 and repeat.
  7. Stop when the step size is very close to zero, in practice 0.001 or smaller. Also stop after a maximum number of steps, in practice 1000 or more.

Formulas

The line and its loss
$$\text{predicted height}=\text{intercept}+\text{slope}\times\text{weight}$$
$$SSR=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\text{height}_i-(\text{intercept}+\text{slope}\times\text{weight}_i)\Big)^2$$

Symbols: \(SSR\) is the sum of the squared residuals, our loss. \(n\) is the number of people, here 3. \(\text{height}_i\) is the observed height of person \(i\). \(\text{weight}_i\) is that person's weight. The intercept and the slope are the two parameters we want. What it tells you: one number that says how badly the current line fits all the data.

Derivative with respect to the intercept

The derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives, so Josh works on one person at a time. Take the first person, with weight 0.5 and height 1.4.

$$\frac{d}{d\,\text{intercept}}\Big(1.4-(\text{intercept}+0.64\times0.5)\Big)^2$$

The square wraps the inside, so we use the chain rule. We move the 2 to the front and multiply by the derivative of the inside.

$$=2\Big(1.4-(\text{intercept}+0.64\times0.5)\Big)\times(-1)$$

The inside gives \(-1\), because 1.4 and \(0.64\times0.5\) do not change with the intercept. We then multiply 2 by \(-1\) to tidy the result.

$$=-2\Big(1.4-(\text{intercept}+0.64\times0.5)\Big)$$

The other two people give the same shape of term, so we add all three pieces.

$$\frac{d\,SSR}{d\,\text{intercept}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}-2\Big(\text{height}_i-(\text{intercept}+\text{slope}\times\text{weight}_i)\Big)$$

Symbols: the same as above. The \(-2\) comes from the square and from the \(-1\) inside. What it tells you: the slope of the loss curve for any intercept you plug in.

Derivative with respect to the slope

Now the slope is free too. We use the chain rule on the first person again.

$$\frac{d}{d\,\text{slope}}\Big(1.4-(\text{intercept}+\text{slope}\times0.5)\Big)^2=2\Big(1.4-(\text{intercept}+\text{slope}\times0.5)\Big)\times(-0.5)$$

The inside gives \(-0.5\), because the intercept is treated as a constant and the slope is multiplied by 0.5.

$$=-2\times0.5\Big(1.4-(\text{intercept}+\text{slope}\times0.5)\Big)$$

We move \(-0.5\) to the front, and keep 0.5 visible to remind us it is the first weight. Adding all three people gives the full derivative.

$$\frac{d\,SSR}{d\,\text{slope}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}-2\,\text{weight}_i\Big(\text{height}_i-(\text{intercept}+\text{slope}\times\text{weight}_i)\Big)$$

Symbols: as above; each person's weight sits in front because the chain rule brings it out. What it tells you: the two derivatives together form the gradient, which guides each step down the loss surface.

Step size and update
$$\text{step size}=\text{slope of the curve}\times\text{learning rate}$$
$$\text{new parameter}=\text{old parameter}-\text{step size}$$

Symbols: the slope of the curve is the derivative at the current value. The learning rate is a small fixed number, 0.1 in the first example. What it tells you: the step shrinks by itself as the slope flattens, so the walk slows down near the bottom.

Worked example from the video

Example

The three people are (weight 0.5, height 1.4), (2.3, 1.9) and (2.9, 3.2). The slope is fixed at 0.64 and the first intercept guess is 0. The predicted height of the first person is \(0+0.64\times0.5=0.32\), so the residual is \(1.4-0.32=1.1\). The other two residuals are 0.4 and 1.3. Squaring and adding them gives a loss of 3.1.

Now plug the intercept 0 into the derivative. The slope of the loss curve is \(-5.7\). With a learning rate of 0.1 the step size is \(-5.7\times0.1=-0.57\). The new intercept is \(0-(-0.57)=0.57\). The residuals visibly shrink.

Repeat. At 0.57 the slope is \(-2.3\), the step size is \(-0.23\), and the new intercept is 0.8. At 0.8 the slope is \(-0.9\), the step size is \(-0.09\), and the new intercept is 0.89. The next intercepts are 0.92, then 0.94, then 0.95. After six steps the answer is 0.95, exactly the least squares value.

For both parameters at once, Josh starts with intercept 0 and slope 1, and uses a learning rate of 0.01. Each round computes two derivatives, two step sizes and two new values. The loop ends at intercept 0.95 and slope 0.64, again matching least squares.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out The learning rate matters a lot. The rate of 0.1 that worked for the intercept alone fails for two parameters. Josh has to drop it to 0.01. A rate that is too large makes the loss grow instead of shrink.
Watch out Gradient Descent does not solve for where the derivative equals zero. Least squares does that. Gradient Descent only steps towards that place. This is why it still works when no closed form solution exists.
Watch out The sum of the squared residuals is only one loss function. Other data types need other losses. The algorithm itself does not change; only the derivatives change.

If they ask you in an interview

Why use Gradient Descent when least squares has a formula?
Least squares works only when we can solve for the point where the derivative equals zero. For most models we cannot do that. Gradient Descent needs only the derivative of the loss, so it also fits logistic regression, t-SNE and much more. It trades an exact formula for a general procedure.
What is the gradient, exactly?
The gradient is the collection of derivatives of one loss function, one for each parameter. For a line it holds two entries: the derivative for the intercept and the derivative for the slope. We plug the current values in, and it tells us the slope of the loss surface in each direction. More parameters simply means more derivatives; nothing else changes.
How do you choose the learning rate?
A rate that is too large overshoots, and the loss can rise. In practice a sensible rate can be found automatically by starting large and reducing it at each step. Josh's own example needed 0.1 for one parameter and 0.01 for two.
When would you use Stochastic Gradient Descent instead?
When the data set is large. Ordinary Gradient Descent uses every data point to compute the derivatives at every step, which is slow with millions of rows. Stochastic Gradient Descent uses a randomly chosen subset at each step instead. The idea is identical; only the amount of data per step changes.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • A loss function scores how badly a model fits; here it is the sum of the squared residuals.
  • Gradient Descent takes the derivative of that loss with respect to each parameter.
  • It starts from random parameter values, such as an intercept of 0.
  • At each round it plugs the current values into the derivatives to get the slope.
  • The step size is that slope times the learning rate, and the new value is old minus step.
  • Steps are large far from the minimum and tiny close to it, so the walk settles smoothly.
  • It stops when the step size is below about 0.001 or when the step limit is reached.
  • The set of derivatives is the gradient, and Stochastic Gradient Descent speeds it up on big data.
