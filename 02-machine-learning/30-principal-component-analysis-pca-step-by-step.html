Principal Component Analysis (PCA), Step-by-Step

▶ Watch on YouTube Also in: —

PCA turns a table with many variables into one simple two-dimensional picture. Josh builds that picture one step at a time, using singular value decomposition (SVD).

In one breath

Josh measures genes in six mice. With two genes you can draw an X-Y plot, but with four genes you can draw nothing. PCA first moves the centre of the data onto the origin. It then finds the line through the origin that the data spreads along the most, and calls it PC1. The next line, perpendicular to PC1, is PC2. Plotting each sample on PC1 and PC2 gives a flat map where similar samples sit close together.

The main idea

Josh measures the activity of Gene 1 and Gene 2 in six mice. If you dislike mice, read "mice" as samples and "genes" as the variables you measure for each sample. One gene fits on a number line. Two genes fit on an X-Y plot. Three genes need a 3D plot, and four genes cannot be drawn at all.

PCA gives you a way out. It builds new axes from mixtures of the old ones, then draws the data on the best two. It also tells you which gene drives the spread, and how good the flat picture really is.

The work starts with centring. You calculate the average of each gene. Together the averages give the centre of the data. You then slide the whole cloud so the centre sits on the origin. Sliding does not change how the points sit relative to each other.

Now you fit a line through the origin. Start with a random line and rotate it until it fits the data as well as possible. That best line is Principal Component 1, or PC1. Here PC1 has a slope of 0.25. For every 4 units along Gene 1, it rises 1 unit along Gene 2. So the data spread out mostly along Gene 1.

Josh calls that ratio a cocktail recipe: mix 4 parts Gene 1 with 1 part Gene 2. Mathematicians call the same thing a linear combination of the variables. Because this graph has only two dimensions, PC2 needs no search. It is simply the line through the origin that is perpendicular to PC1.

Step by step

First, look at how PCA scores a candidate line. Take one point. Its distance to the origin is fixed while the line rotates. Project the point onto the line and you get a right angle. Label the three sides \(a\), \(b\) and \(c\). Because \(a\) is fixed, a bigger \(c\) forces a smaller \(b\). So minimising the distance to the line and maximising the distance to the origin are the same job. PCA maximises \(c\) because it is easier to calculate.

  1. Centre the data, then shift the cloud so the centre lands on the origin.
  2. Draw a random line through the origin. Project every point onto it at a right angle.
  3. Score the line. Measure each projected point's distance to the origin, square the six values, and add them up. This is SS(distances).
  4. Rotate and repeat. Keep the line with the largest SS(distances). That winning line is PC1.
  5. Read the recipe off PC1. Here it goes 4 across and 1 up, so PC1 is 4 parts Gene 1 and 1 part Gene 2.
  6. Scale that recipe to one unit long. The result is the eigenvector, and its two numbers are the loading scores.
  7. Find PC2, the perpendicular line through the origin. Its recipe is −1 part Gene 1 to 4 parts Gene 2.
  8. Turn the sums of squares into variation. A PC's eigenvalue is the average of its squared distances.
  9. Draw the plot. Rotate so PC1 is horizontal, then place each sample at its projected positions.
  10. With more genes, keep adding perpendicular PCs. Their number is the count of variables or samples, whichever is smaller.

The eigenvalues let you check the picture before you trust it. A scree plot shows the percentage of variation each PC accounts for. In Josh's three-gene example PC1 takes 79%, PC2 takes 15% and PC3 takes 6%.

Formulas

Score for a candidate line
$$ \text{SS(distances)} = d_1^2 + d_2^2 + \cdots + d_n^2 $$

Symbols: \(d_i\) is the distance from the \(i\)-th projected point to the origin. \(n\) is the number of samples, here 6. Squaring stops negative distances from cancelling positive ones. What it tells you: how far the data spread along that line. PCA keeps the line with the largest value, and that line is PC1.

Why maximising works
$$ a^2 = b^2 + c^2 $$

Symbols: \(a\) is the fixed distance from a data point to the origin. \(b\) is the distance from the point to the line. \(c\) is the distance from the projected point to the origin. What it tells you: since \(a\) never changes, \(b\) and \(c\) move in opposite directions. Minimising \(b\) and maximising \(c\) give the same best line.

Scaling the recipe to a unit vector
$$ a = \sqrt{4^2 + 1^2} = 4.12 $$
$$ \left(\tfrac{4}{4.12},\ \tfrac{1}{4.12}\right) = (0.97,\ 0.242) $$

Symbols: 4 and 1 are the steps along Gene 1 and Gene 2 that trace PC1. \(a\) is the length of that arrow. Dividing both steps by 4.12 makes the arrow exactly one unit long. What it tells you: the ratio is unchanged, so PC1 still uses four times more Gene 1 than Gene 2. The pair \((0.97, 0.242)\) is the eigenvector for PC1, and the two numbers are its loading scores.

Eigenvalue and singular value
$$ \lambda = \frac{\text{SS(distances)}}{n-1} \qquad s = \sqrt{\text{SS(distances)}} $$

Symbols: \(\lambda\) is the eigenvalue of the PC, the average of its squared distances. \(s\) is its singular value. \(n\) is the number of samples. What it tells you: that average is exactly the usual formula for variation, so an eigenvalue is simply the amount of variation captured by one PC.

Share of variation per PC
$$ \text{share of PC}_i = \frac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 + \cdots + \lambda_k} $$

Symbols: \(\lambda_i\) is the eigenvalue of the PC you care about. \(k\) is the total number of PCs. What it tells you: the fraction of the total spread that this one axis explains. These fractions are the bars of the scree plot.

Worked example from the video

Example

Two genes are measured in six mice. After centring, the best fitting line through the origin has a slope of 0.25. So PC1 goes 4 units along Gene 1 for every 1 unit along Gene 2, and its recipe is 4 parts Gene 1 to 1 part Gene 2.

The length of that arrow is \(\sqrt{4^2+1^2}=4.12\). Dividing each side by 4.12 gives \(4/4.12 = 0.97\) and \(1/4.12 = 0.242\). The eigenvector for PC1 is therefore \((0.97,\ 0.242)\), and those are the loading scores.

PC2 is perpendicular, so its recipe is −1 part Gene 1 to 4 parts Gene 2. Scaled the same way it becomes \((-0.242,\ 0.97)\). On PC2, Gene 2 counts four times as much as Gene 1.

Now suppose the variation is 15 for PC1 and 3 for PC2. The total is \(15+3=18\). PC1 holds \(15/18 = 0.83\), or 83%. PC2 holds \(3/18\), or 17%.

In the three-gene example the shares are 79%, 15% and 6%. PC1 and PC2 together give 94%, so a flat plot of them summarises the 3D graph well. With four genes the first two PCs hold 90%.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch outEvery principal component must pass through the origin. That only makes sense after you have centred the data on the origin. Skip the centring step and the first component will chase the mean instead of the spread.
Watch outPeople describe PCA in two ways: it minimises the distance from points to the line, or it maximises the spread of the projected points. Both describe the same line. The Pythagorean link between \(b\) and \(c\) is the reason.
Watch outThe number of principal components is not always the number of variables. In practice it is the smaller of the number of variables and the number of samples.
Watch outDo not trust a 2D PCA plot before you check the scree plot. If PC3 and PC4 also hold a lot of variation, the flat picture is only rough. It can still show clusters.

If they ask you in an interview

What does PCA actually do?
PCA finds new axes that are mixtures of the original variables. The first axis is the direction along which the data spread the most, and each later axis is perpendicular to all the earlier ones. You then plot the samples on the first two axes. That gives a readable picture of data with far too many dimensions to draw.
How does PCA decide that one line fits better than another?
It projects every point onto the line and measures how far each projected point sits from the origin. It squares those distances and adds them up, and the largest sum wins. That is also the line closest to the points, because the two distances are the short sides of a right triangle with a fixed hypotenuse.
What are loading scores, eigenvalues and singular values?
Loading scores are the recipe for a principal component, scaled so the direction vector has length one. They say how much each original variable contributes to that component. The eigenvalue is the average of the squared projected distances, so it is the variation captured by the component. The singular value is just the square root of the sum of those squared distances.
How do you know if a 2D PCA plot is trustworthy?
I look at the scree plot, which shows the percentage of variation each component accounts for. If PC1 and PC2 together cover most of the variation, say 90% or more, the flat plot is a fair summary. If the later components still hold a lot, the plot is only approximate. I would still check it for clusters, because they often survive.
Which variable matters most, and how does PCA tell you?
You read the loading scores of the component you care about. The variable with the largest absolute loading drives that axis. In the two-gene example PC1 is 0.97 Gene 1 and 0.242 Gene 2, so Gene 1 drives the spread.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • PCA compresses data with many variables into a plot you can read.
  • You first move the centre of the data onto the origin.
  • PC1 is the line through the origin with the largest sum of squared projected distances.
  • Maximising that sum is the same as minimising the distance from the points to the line.
  • The slope of PC1 gives a recipe, and scaling it to unit length gives the loading scores.
  • PC2 is perpendicular to PC1, and each later PC is perpendicular to all before it.
  • Eigenvalues are variations, so they give the percentage share of each PC in a scree plot.
  • If the first two PCs hold most of the variation, the 2D PCA plot is a good summary of the data.
