XGBoost Part 2 (of 4): Classification

▶ Watch on YouTube

XGBoost builds classification trees the same way it builds regression trees. The changes are the similarity score, the cover, the output value, and predictions made in log odds.

In one breath

XGBoost starts with a default predicted probability of 0.5 for every sample. It computes residuals, then grows a tree that groups similar residuals together. The similarity score is the sum of residuals, squared, divided by the sum of \(p(1-p)\) plus lambda. Gain and gamma decide pruning, exactly as in regression. Leaf outputs are added in log odds, scaled by the learning rate, then converted back to a probability.

The main idea

Josh uses a very small training set: four drug dosages. Two dosages made the drug effective and two did not. The goal is to predict the probability that the drug works at a given dosage.

The first step is an initial prediction. It can be any value, but by default XGBoost uses 0.5. So before any tree is built, every dosage gets a 50% chance of being effective. This default is the same for regression and for classification.

A residual is the difference between the observed value and the predicted value. An effective dose sits at probability 1, so its residual is \(1 - 0.5 = +0.5\). An ineffective dose sits at probability 0, so its residual is \(0 - 0.5 = -0.5\). These four residuals are the data that the first tree is fitted to.

Now the big change. The numerator of the similarity score is the same as in regression: the sum of residuals, squared. The denominator is different. For each residual in the leaf, take the previous probability times one minus that probability, and add these up. Then add lambda, the regularization parameter that shrinks scores. The same term appears in ordinary gradient boost for classification. Part three shows why regression and classification share one equation underneath.

Step by step

  1. Make the initial prediction. Every dosage gets probability 0.5.
  2. Compute the four residuals: −0.5 for dosage 2, +0.5 for dosage 8, +0.5 for dosage 12, and −0.5 for dosage 18.
  3. Put all four residuals in one leaf, the root. They add up to zero, so the numerator is zero and the similarity is 0.
  4. Try the threshold dosage < 15, which is the midpoint of the last two dosages. The left leaf gets three residuals and scores 0.33. The right leaf gets one residual and scores 1.
  5. Compute the gain: \(0.33 + 1 - 0 = 1.33\). No other threshold beats this, so it becomes the root split.
  6. Split the three left residuals. The threshold dosage < 10 lets two residuals cancel, so its gain is only 0.66. The threshold dosage < 5 keeps the two +0.5 residuals together and gives a gain of 2.66, so it wins.
  7. Stop growing. Josh limits the tree to two levels, so the tree is finished.
  8. Check cover, a minimum-size rule for leaves. The default minimum of 1 would remove every leaf here, so Josh sets it to 0.
  9. Prune with gamma, then compute an output value for every leaf. With lambda equal to zero the outputs are −2, +2 and −2.

Formulas

Similarity score for classification
$$ \text{Similarity} = \frac{\left(\sum_i r_i\right)^2}{\sum_i \left[\, p_i\,(1-p_i) \,\right] + \lambda} $$

Symbols: \(r_i\) is the residual of observation \(i\) in this leaf. \(p_i\) is the probability that was predicted for observation \(i\) before this tree. \(\lambda\) is the regularization parameter that shrinks the score. The sums run over every residual in the leaf. What it tells you: a leaf scores high when its residuals are large and point the same way, because they are added first and squared afterwards.

Gain and pruning
$$ \text{Gain} = \text{Sim}_{\text{left}} + \text{Sim}_{\text{right}} - \text{Sim}_{\text{parent}} \qquad \text{prune if } \text{Gain} - \gamma < 0 $$

Symbols: \(\text{Sim}_{\text{left}}\) and \(\text{Sim}_{\text{right}}\) are the similarity scores of the two child leaves. \(\text{Sim}_{\text{parent}}\) is the score before the split. \(\gamma\) is the tree complexity parameter you choose. What it tells you: gain measures how much better the residuals are grouped after the split, and gamma sets the price a split must pay to survive.

Cover
$$ \text{Cover} = \sum_i p_i\,(1-p_i) $$

Symbols: the same \(p_i\) as above, summed over the residuals in the leaf. Cover is simply the denominator of the similarity score with \(\lambda\) removed. What it tells you: it is the minimum-size rule for a leaf. For regression the same definition reduces to the plain number of residuals, but for classification it depends on the previous probabilities.

Output value of a leaf
$$ \text{Output} = \frac{\sum_i r_i}{\sum_i \left[\, p_i\,(1-p_i) \,\right] + \lambda} $$

Symbols: the residuals \(r_i\), the previous probabilities \(p_i\), and \(\lambda\). Note the numerator is the plain sum of residuals, not the squared sum. What it tells you: it is the step this leaf contributes, measured in log odds. Apart from \(\lambda\), it is the same formula as ordinary gradient boost for classification.

Making a prediction
$$ \log(\text{odds}) = \log\!\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right) $$
$$ \log(\text{odds})_{\text{new}} = \log(\text{odds})_{\text{old}} + \eta \times \text{Output} $$
$$ p_{\text{new}} = \frac{e^{\log(\text{odds})_{\text{new}}}}{1 + e^{\log(\text{odds})_{\text{new}}}} $$

Symbols: \(p\) is a probability, \(\eta\) is the learning rate that XGBoost calls eta and defaults to 0.3, and Output is the leaf value the sample lands in. What it tells you: trees are added in log odds space, and the logistic function in the last line converts the running total back into a probability.

Worked example from the video

Example

Take the split dosage < 15 on the first tree, with \(\lambda = 0\). The left leaf holds residuals −0.5, +0.5 and +0.5. Two of them cancel, so the numerator is \((0.5)^2 = 0.25\). Each previous probability is 0.5, so each contributes \(0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25\) and the denominator is 0.75. The similarity is \(0.25 / 0.75 = 0.33\). The right leaf has one residual: \(0.25 / 0.25 = 1\). The gain is \(0.33 + 1 - 0 = 1.33\).

Now the leaf for dosage 2. Its output value is \(-0.5 / 0.25 = -2\). Convert the initial prediction: \(\log(0.5/0.5) = 0\). Add the scaled output: \(0 + 0.3 \times (-2) = -0.6\). Push that through the logistic function and the new probability is 0.35. The residual falls from −0.5 to −0.35, which is one small step in the right direction.

The leaf holding dosages 8 and 12 gives \(1.0 / 0.5 = 2\). So dosage 8 moves to \(0 + 0.3 \times 2 = 0.6\) in log odds, which is a probability of 0.65. With \(\lambda = 1\) instead, that same output shrinks to 0.67, while the single-observation leaf shrinks much harder, from −2 to −0.4.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out The numerator sums the residuals first and squares the total afterwards. It does not square each residual. That is why a leaf with +0.5 and −0.5 scores zero: the residuals cancel, and XGBoost treats such a leaf as useless.
Watch out Cover bites hard in classification. With the default 0.5 starting probability, one observation gives a cover of 0.25 and three give 0.75. The default minimum cover is 1, so XGBoost would reject every leaf and leave only the root. Josh sets min_child_weight to 0 to keep the tree. In regression cover equals the number of residuals, so the default of 1 has no effect.
Watch out Lambda shrinks small leaves much more than large ones. Adding \(\lambda = 1\) moves the single-observation leaf from −2 to −0.4, but the two-observation leaf only from 2 to 0.67. That is the point: lambda reduces how much an isolated observation can move the prediction.
Watch out From the second tree onward the previous probabilities differ between observations. So the denominators in both the similarity score and the output value become a mixture of values, not a simple count times 0.25.

If they ask you in an interview

How does the XGBoost similarity score for classification differ from regression?
The numerator is the same in both cases: the sum of the residuals, squared. Only the denominator changes. For regression it is the number of residuals plus lambda. For classification it is the sum of \(p(1-p)\) over the previous probabilities, plus lambda.
What is cover, and why does it matter more for classification?
Cover is the denominator of the similarity score with lambda removed. For regression it is the count of residuals in a leaf, so the default minimum of 1 has no effect. For classification it is the sum of \(p(1-p)\), which is at most 0.25 per observation. So at \(p = 0.5\) a leaf needs four observations to reach a cover of 1. The minimum is set with the min_child_weight parameter.
How do lambda and gamma both cause pruning?
Gamma is direct. You subtract it from the gain at the lowest branch, and if the difference is negative you cut that branch and move up. Lambda is indirect. It sits in the denominator of the similarity score, so it shrinks every score and therefore every gain. So the same gamma now prunes more branches. Lambda also shrinks leaf outputs, most of all for leaves with few observations.
Walk me through one prediction update.
Start with the initial probability, 0.5 by default, and convert it to log odds, which gives zero. Drop the sample down the tree to find its leaf output value. Multiply that output by the learning rate, eta, which defaults to 0.3, and add it to the running log odds. Then apply the logistic function to get the new probability. With an output of −2 the log odds move to −0.6 and the probability drops from 0.5 to 0.35.
Why bother adding the log odds of the initial prediction when it is zero?
It is only zero because the default initial prediction is 0.5, and \(\log(0.5/0.5) = 0\). You are free to set the initial prediction to anything else. If 75% of your training data were positive, you might start at 0.75. Then the initial log odds would be 1.1, and you add 1.1 instead of zero.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • XGBoost starts classification with a default predicted probability of 0.5 for everyone.
  • The similarity score divides the squared sum of residuals by the sum of \(p(1-p)\) plus lambda.
  • Gain is the two child scores minus the parent score, and the split with the largest gain wins.
  • Pruning subtracts gamma from the gain at the lowest branch, and a negative result removes that branch.
  • Cover is that denominator without lambda, and its default minimum of 1 often blocks small classification leaves.
  • A leaf output is the plain sum of residuals over the same denominator, so lambda shrinks isolated observations most.
  • Predictions are accumulated in log odds, scaled by the learning rate eta, then converted back with the logistic function.
  • Each new tree is fitted to the smaller residuals, and the process repeats until the residuals are tiny or the tree limit is reached.
