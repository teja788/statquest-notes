Cosine Similarity

▶ Watch on YouTube Also in: —

This video shows how the cosine similarity tells a computer how similar or different two phrases are. It is an easy metric to calculate, so it works even when we have far too many phrases to compare by eye.

In one breath

We count how many times each word appears in each phrase. Each phrase then becomes a point, and we draw a line from the origin to it. The cosine similarity is the cosine of the angle between two such lines. Identical phrases score 1, phrases with no shared words score 0, and partial overlap scores between 0 and 1. A simple formula gives the same number straight from the word counts, so we never need to draw the graph.

The main idea

Josh starts with sentences about the 1990 movie Troll 2. By eye, it is easy to see which sentences praise the movie and which one does not. But if we collected a whole month of Twitter traffic, we could not read it all. We need a computer to measure similarity for us.

The trick is to turn each phrase into counts of words. Take the phrases "Hello World" and "Hello". We make a table with one column per word. "Hello World" has one "hello" and one "world". "Hello" has one "hello" and zero "world".

Now each phrase is a point on a graph. The x-axis is the count of "hello" and the y-axis is the count of "world". We draw a line from the origin, (0, 0), to each point. The angle between the two lines tells us how similar the phrases are. Here the angle is 45 degrees, and the cosine of 45 degrees is 0.71.

Step by step

  1. Make a table of word counts. There is one column for each different word and one row for each phrase.
  2. Plot each phrase as a point, using the word counts as its coordinates. With two words, this is a normal two-dimensional graph.
  3. Draw a line from the origin to each point and find the angle between the two lines.
  4. Take the cosine of that angle. This number is the cosine similarity.
  5. Check what happens when a phrase repeats a word. If "Hello" becomes "Hello Hello Hello", its point moves further out on the x-axis. The angle stays at 45 degrees, so the score stays 0.71.
  6. Look at the two extreme cases. Two identical phrases land on the same point, the angle is 0 degrees, and the cosine is 1. Two phrases with no shared words, like "Hello" and "World", make a 90 degree angle, and the cosine is 0.
  7. Replace the drawing with the formula. The four steps above are tedious, and the formula computes the same number directly from the table.

Formulas

The formula looks complicated, but it only adds up products and squares of the word counts. It gives the cosine of the angle without drawing anything.

Cosine similarity
$$ \text{cosine similarity} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} A_i B_i}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} A_i^2}\;\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} B_i^2}} $$

Symbols: \(A\) and \(B\) are the two phrases. \(i\) is the index, which keeps track of the word we are working on; it starts at 1, the first word in the table. \(n\) is the number of different words in the two phrases together. \(A_i\) and \(B_i\) are how many times word \(i\) appears in phrase \(A\) and in phrase \(B\). Each \(\sum\) (sigma) means "add up this term for every word from 1 to \(n\)". What it tells you: the result is the cosine of the angle between the two phrases, computed straight from the table of word counts.

The formula has one more big advantage. With more than two different words, we need more than two dimensions to plot the points. We cannot draw a five-dimensional graph, but we can still plug five columns of counts into the formula.

Worked example from the video

Example

Two words. Let \(A\) = "Hello World" and \(B\) = "Hello". The words are hello and world, so \(n = 2\). The counts are \(A = (1, 1)\) and \(B = (1, 0)\).

$$ \text{top} = (1 \times 1) + (1 \times 0) = 1 $$ $$ \text{bottom} = \sqrt{1^2 + 1^2}\;\sqrt{1^2 + 0^2} = \sqrt{2} \times 1 \approx 1.41 $$ $$ \text{cosine similarity} = \frac{1}{1.41} \approx 0.71 $$

This matches the cosine of 45 degrees that we got from the graph.

Five words. Let \(A\) = "I love Troll 2" and \(B\) = "I love Gymkata". The different words are I, love, Troll, 2 and Gymkata, so \(n = 5\). The counts are \(A = (1, 1, 1, 1, 0)\) and \(B = (1, 1, 0, 0, 1)\).

$$ \text{top} = 1 + 1 + 0 + 0 + 0 = 2 $$ $$ \text{bottom} = \sqrt{4}\;\sqrt{3} = 2 \times 1.73 \approx 3.46 $$ $$ \text{cosine similarity} = \frac{2}{3.46} \approx 0.58 $$

We would need a five-dimensional graph to see this angle, but the formula gives 0.58 directly.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out The score depends only on the angle, not on the length of the lines. "Hello" and "Hello Hello Hello" both score 0.71 against "Hello World". Repeating a word moves the point further out along the same line.
Watch out The count \(n\) covers every different word in both phrases, not just one phrase. A word that is missing from a phrase still gets a column, with a count of 0.
Watch out With word counts, a score of 0 means the phrases share no words at all. It does not mean they are opposites. The range in the video runs from 0 (nothing in common) to 1 (exactly the same).
Watch out Do not try to picture the graph for more than two or three words. Use the formula; it works the same way in any number of dimensions.

If they ask you in an interview

What is cosine similarity?
It is an easy-to-calculate metric that tells us how similar two things are. For text, we turn each phrase into a vector of word counts. The cosine similarity is the cosine of the angle between those two vectors.
What values can it take for word counts, and what do they mean?
It runs from 0 to 1. Identical phrases give an angle of 0 degrees and a score of 1. Phrases with no words in common give 90 degrees and a score of 0. Partial overlap falls in between.
Does repeating a word change the score?
Not if it only stretches the vector. "Hello" and "Hello Hello Hello" point the same way, so they make the same angle with "Hello World". Cosine similarity ignores the length of the lines and looks only at the angle.
How do you compute it when there are many different words?
We skip the graph and use the formula. Multiply the matching counts and add them up, then divide by the square root of each phrase's sum of squared counts, multiplied together. For "I love Troll 2" and "I love Gymkata" that gives 2 divided by about 3.46, which is 0.58.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • Cosine similarity measures how similar two things are, such as two phrases.
  • Each phrase becomes a row of word counts, which we can treat as a point.
  • The score is the cosine of the angle between the lines from the origin to the two points.
  • Identical phrases score 1, phrases with no shared words score 0, and partial overlap scores in between.
  • Only the angle matters, so repeating words in a phrase does not change the score.
  • The formula computes the score straight from the counts, with no drawing.
  • The formula also works when there are more words than we can draw, like the five-word example that gives 0.58.
