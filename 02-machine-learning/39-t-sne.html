t-SNE

▶ Watch on YouTube

t-SNE takes a data set with many dimensions and draws it on a small graph, usually two dimensions. It keeps the clusters that were in the original data.

In one breath

t-SNE first measures how similar every pair of points is in the original high-dimensional space. It measures similarity with a normal curve centred on each point, then scales the scores so they add up to one. Next it drops the points onto the new low-dimensional graph in random order and measures similarity there with a t distribution. Then it moves the points a little at a time, so that the new similarity scores match the original ones. Points that were near each other pull together, and points that were far apart push away.

The main idea

Josh starts with a simple 2-D scatter plot that holds two clear clusters. The goal is to squash it onto a 1-D number line without losing them. This tiny example works the same way as a real one.

The lazy way is to project the points onto one axis. That fails. Project onto the Y axis and the two clusters turn into a mishmash. Project onto the X axis and you get the same mess.

t-SNE instead places the points on the line in a random order, then nudges them. For one point, the points that sit near it in the scatter plot attract it. The points that sit far from it in the scatter plot repel it. The stronger side wins, and the point takes a small step that way. Repeat this for every point, many times, and the clusters reappear on the line.

Step by step

  1. Pick one point in the scatter plot. Call it the point of interest. Measure the straight-line distance from it to every other point.
  2. Draw a normal curve centred on the point of interest. Mark each distance on that curve and read the height of the curve there. Josh calls that height the unscaled similarity.
  3. Because the curve falls away fast, near points get high scores and distant points get very low scores. That is the whole reason a bell curve is used.
  4. Set the width of the curve from the local density. A dense neighbourhood gets a narrow curve, a sparse one a wide curve. The perplexity parameter is the expected density around each point.
  5. Scale the scores for that point so they add up to one. Divide each score by the sum of all the scores for that point.
  6. Repeat for every point. The score from A to B may differ from the score from B to A, because the curves have different widths. t-SNE averages the two directions. A point scores zero with itself. You now have a matrix of similarity scores.
  7. Drop the points onto the number line in random order and build a second similarity matrix there. This time use a t distribution instead of a normal curve, then scale the scores the same way.
  8. The new matrix is a mess compared with the original one. Move the points a small step at a time, always in the direction that makes the new matrix look more like the original one. Like a chess game, it cannot be solved in one move.

Formulas

The video uses pictures, not algebra. These lines write down the same moves: read a height off a curve, scale, and average.

Unscaled similarity
$$ w_{j\mid i} \;=\; \exp\!\left(-\frac{d_{ij}^{2}}{2\sigma_i^{2}}\right) $$

Symbols: \(w_{j\mid i}\) is the unscaled similarity of point \(j\) seen from point \(i\); \(d_{ij}\) is the distance between the two points; \(\sigma_i\) is the width of the curve centred on point \(i\), set by the local density. What it tells you: this is the height of the bell curve at that distance, so it drops away quickly.

Scaling so the scores sum to one
$$ p_{j\mid i} \;=\; \frac{w_{j\mid i}}{\sum_{k \neq i} w_{k\mid i}} $$

Symbols: \(p_{j\mid i}\) is the scaled similarity; the sum in the bottom runs over every other point \(k\), so it is the total of all unscaled scores for point \(i\). What it tells you: after this division, each row adds up to one, so a tight cluster and a loose cluster produce the same numbers.

Averaging the two directions
$$ p_{ij} \;=\; \frac{p_{j\mid i} + p_{i\mid j}}{2}, \qquad p_{ii} = 0 $$

Symbols: \(p_{j\mid i}\) is the score measured from \(i\), \(p_{i\mid j}\) the score measured from \(j\), and \(p_{ij}\) the agreed score for the pair. What it tells you: the two curves have different widths, so the directions disagree. The average settles it. A point scores zero with itself, because that does not help clustering.

Similarity on the low-dimensional graph
$$ q_{ij} \;=\; \frac{\left(1 + \lVert y_i - y_j \rVert^{2}\right)^{-1}}{\sum_{k \neq l}\left(1 + \lVert y_k - y_l \rVert^{2}\right)^{-1}} $$

Symbols: \(y_i\) and \(y_j\) are the positions on the new graph, and \(q_{ij}\) is their scaled similarity. What it tells you: the same recipe as above, but the height comes off a t curve. t-SNE moves the \(y\) positions until the \(q\) values look like the \(p\) values.

Worked example from the video

Example

Josh compares two clusters, one twice as spread out as the other. The tight cluster gets a curve with a standard deviation of 1. Its two neighbours sit at distances 1 and 2, so the curve heights are 0.24 and 0.05.

Scale them: \(0.24 / 0.29 = 0.82\) and \(0.05 / 0.29 = 0.18\).

The loose cluster is half as dense, so its curve has a standard deviation of 2. Its neighbours are twice as far away. The heights are exactly half the ones above, 0.12 and 0.027.

Scale them: \(0.12 / 0.147 = 0.82\) and \(0.027 / 0.147 = 0.18\).

Both clusters give 0.82 and 0.18. That is why scaling matters: a loose cluster now looks as much like a cluster as a tight one.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out Do not confuse t-SNE with dropping an axis. Projecting onto X or onto Y turns two clean clusters into a mishmash. t-SNE works because it uses the distances between all pairs of points.
Watch out The width of the normal curve is not fixed. It follows the local density, so every point gets its own curve. That is why two clusters of very different density can end up looking similar.
Watch out The similarity from A to B is not the same as from B to A, because the two curves have different widths. t-SNE averages the two numbers. Also, the similarity of a point with itself is set to zero on purpose.
Watch out The t distribution is not a small detail. Without it the clusters would clump up in the middle of the plot and be hard to tell apart. Its fatter tails let points that are far apart stay far apart.

If they ask you in an interview

Explain t-SNE in a minute.
t-SNE reduces a high-dimensional data set to a two-dimensional picture and keeps the clusters. It first scores how similar every pair of points is, using a normal curve centred on each point. It then scatters the points at random on the new plot and scores them there with a t distribution. Finally it moves the points a small step at a time until the two score sets match.
Why is a t distribution used, and not a normal curve, on the low-dimensional plot?
A t distribution is shorter in the middle and has fatter tails than a normal curve. Those fat tails give distant pairs a bigger score, so those points are allowed to stay far apart. Without it, everything would clump towards the middle of the plot and the clusters would be hard to see. That is the t in t-SNE.
Why are the similarity scores scaled so they add up to one?
Because the width of the curve depends on the local density, so raw scores from a dense region and a sparse region are not comparable. If one cluster is half as dense, its raw scores are about half the size. Dividing each score by the total for that point removes that difference. After scaling, a loose cluster gives the same numbers as a tight one.
What is perplexity?
Perplexity is the expected density around each point. It sets how wide the normal curve is for that point, which decides how many neighbours really count as similar. A small value looks at very local structure, and a larger one looks at broader structure. It is the main knob you tune when a t-SNE plot does not look sensible.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • t-SNE squashes high-dimensional data onto a small plot and keeps the clusters.
  • Simply projecting onto one axis destroys the clusters, so t-SNE uses all the pairwise distances instead.
  • A distance becomes a similarity score by reading the height of a normal curve centred on the point of interest.
  • The width of that curve follows the local density, and perplexity is the expected density around each point.
  • Scores are scaled to sum to one, so tight and loose clusters give the same numbers.
  • The two directions of a pair disagree, so t-SNE averages them, and a point scores zero with itself.
  • On the new plot the points start in random order, and a t distribution scores them there.
  • t-SNE moves the points a little at a time until the new matrix looks like the original one.
