Ridge vs Lasso Regression, Visualized

▶ Watch on YouTube

Ridge and lasso regression both add a penalty to the fit. This video draws both penalties on one picture. You then see why lasso can push a slope all the way to zero, and ridge cannot.

In one breath

Josh fits lines with different slopes to a small weight and height data set. For each slope he plots the sum of the squared residuals, which traces a parabola with its lowest point at slope 0.45. The ridge penalty, lambda times the slope squared, slides that lowest point toward zero but never onto zero. The lasso penalty, lambda times the absolute value of the slope, puts a sharp kink at slope zero. When lambda is large enough, the kink becomes the lowest point, so the best slope is exactly zero.

The main idea

Both methods start from the same number: the sum of the squared residuals, usually shortened to SSR. A residual is the gap between an observed height and the height the line predicts. Square every gap and add them up. One number now says how bad the line is.

The clever move here is to treat the slope as the thing we vary. Fit a horizontal line and note its SSR. Tilt the line a little and note the SSR again. Put the slope on the x axis and the SSR on the y axis. The points trace a parabola, and its bottom marks the best slope, here 0.45.

A penalty changes this curve, not the data. Ridge adds lambda times the slope squared. Lasso adds lambda times the absolute value of the slope. Both terms are zero when the slope is zero, and both grow as the slope moves away from zero. Wherever the new curve bottoms out is the new best slope.

Step by step

  1. Start with a horizontal line through the weight and height data. It fits badly. Measure the residuals, square them, and add them up.
  2. Plot that number on a new graph. The y axis is the SSR and the x axis is the slope. The horizontal line has slope 0.
  3. Raise the slope to 0.2 and plot the new SSR. Raise it to 0.4 and plot again. Keep going, or simply draw the whole curve.
  4. The curve is a parabola. Its lowest point is at slope 0.45, so 0.45 is the best plain least squares fit.
  5. Now switch to ridge. Add lambda times the slope squared to the SSR. With lambda set to 0 the penalty vanishes, so we get the original parabola back.
  6. Set lambda to 10. Residuals fall as the slope rises, but the penalty grows, so the curve now bottoms out below 0.45.
  7. Raise lambda to 20, then 40. The curve stays a smooth parabola and its lowest point moves closer to zero. Even at lambda 400 the best slope is still above zero.
  8. Now switch to lasso. Add lambda times the absolute value of the slope. At lambda 10 a kink appears at slope zero, and it sharpens at 20. At lambda 40 the kink is the lowest point, so the best slope is exactly 0 and weight is ignored.

Formulas

Sum of squared residuals
$$ \text{SSR} = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_i - \hat{y}_i\right)^2 $$

Symbols: \(y_i\) is the observed height of person \(i\). \(\hat{y}_i\) is the height the line predicts for that person. \(n\) is the number of people. Their difference is the residual. What it tells you: one number for how far the line misses the data, with big misses counted much more heavily.

Ridge, the L2 or squared penalty
$$ \text{SSR} + \lambda\, m^{2} $$

Symbols: \(m\) is the slope, the change in height per unit of weight. \(\lambda\) (lambda) is a number you choose that sets the strength of the penalty. What it tells you: ridge minimises this quantity. Because \(m^{2}\) is a smooth bowl, adding it to a parabola gives another parabola, whose bottom sits closer to zero but never on it.

Lasso, the L1 or absolute value penalty
$$ \text{SSR} + \lambda\, \lvert m \rvert $$

Symbols: the same \(m\) and \(\lambda\) as above. The bars \(\lvert m \rvert\) mean the size of the slope, ignoring its sign. What it tells you: lasso minimises this quantity. \(\lvert m \rvert\) is a V shape with a corner at \(m = 0\), so the total curve inherits that corner, and the corner can become the lowest point.

Worked example from the video

Example

Josh sets lambda to 10 and works through three slopes for the ridge penalty. The penalty is \(10 \times m^{2}\), so it costs nothing at slope zero and grows quickly after that.

slope \(m\)penalty \(10 \times m^2\)what happens to the fit
00horizontal line, worst residuals, no penalty at all
0.20.4residuals shrink, a small penalty appears
0.41.6residuals shrink further, but the penalty is now four times larger

The two forces pull in opposite directions. The falling residuals want a bigger slope and the rising penalty wants a smaller one. The balance point lands below 0.45, which is why lambda equal to 10 shrinks the slope.

With lasso at lambda 40 the penalty is \(40 \times \lvert m \rvert\). Now the balance point is not just small, it is exactly zero, so the best line is flat and weight plays no part.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out Lasso does not zero out a slope because it punishes harder. The size of the penalty is not the point; the shape is. The corner at a slope of zero is what lets zero win.
Watch out Ridge never removes a variable. Josh raises lambda to 10, 20, 40 and even 400, and the best slope keeps shrinking without landing on zero. To drop useless predictors, ridge alone is not enough.
Watch out The y axis here is the SSR plus the penalty, not the SSR alone. Curves for different lambda values sit at different heights, so compare only where each one bottoms out.
Watch out Lambda equal to zero turns both methods back into ordinary least squares, because the penalty is then zero at every slope. That is why the blue curve is the same in both figures.

If they ask you in an interview

Why can lasso set a coefficient to exactly zero when ridge cannot?
It is the shape of the penalty near zero. The squared penalty is smooth there, so the total curve stays a parabola and its minimum always sits a little away from zero. The absolute value penalty has a corner at zero, which puts a kink in the total curve. Once lambda is large enough that kink becomes the lowest point, so the coefficient is exactly zero.
What does lambda control, and what happens as you raise it?
Lambda sets how much you care about a large coefficient compared with a good fit. At lambda zero there is no penalty and you get plain least squares. As lambda grows the best slope shrinks toward zero in both methods. Ridge approaches zero and stops short, while lasso can reach zero and stay there.
Can you explain the picture Josh draws?
He puts the slope on the x axis and the sum of squared residuals plus the penalty on the y axis. Each candidate line becomes one point, and the points trace a curve. The bottom of the curve is the slope the method chooses. One curve per lambda value makes the shrinkage visible as that bottom slides left.
When would you prefer lasso over ridge?
Use lasso when you think some predictors are useless and you want the model to drop them, since it can drive their coefficients to zero. Use ridge when you believe most predictors carry a little signal and you only want to calm them down. In the video, a large lambda with lasso removes weight completely, while ridge always keeps some of it.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • Plot the slope of the fitted line against the sum of squared residuals, and you get a parabola.
  • The bottom of that parabola is the plain least squares answer, here a slope of 0.45.
  • Ridge adds lambda times the slope squared, which keeps the curve a smooth parabola.
  • Raising lambda in ridge slides the lowest point toward zero but never onto it, even at lambda 400.
  • Lasso adds lambda times the absolute value of the slope, which puts a corner in the curve at zero.
  • Raising lambda in lasso sharpens that corner until it becomes the lowest point of the curve.
  • At lambda 40 the lasso slope is exactly zero, so weight drops out of the prediction.
