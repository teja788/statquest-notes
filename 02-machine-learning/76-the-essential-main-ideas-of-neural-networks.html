The Essential Main Ideas of Neural Networks

▶ Watch on YouTube Also in: Neural Networks / Deep Learning (video 2)

This video opens the "black box" of a neural network. It shows, number by number, how a tiny network bends simple curves and adds them into a squiggle that fits data.

In one breath

A drug works at medium dosages but not at low or high ones, so no straight line fits the data. A neural network can fit a squiggle instead. Each connection multiplies the input by a weight and adds a bias. This picks a slice of a bent curve called an activation function. The network then scales these bent shapes, adds them together, and shifts the result to fit the data. Josh suggests a better name: a "big fancy squiggle fitting machine".

The main idea

Josh uses a drug tested at three dosages: low, medium, and high. Low and high dosages were not effective, so their efficacy is 0. Medium dosages were effective, so their efficacy is 1. A straight line cannot fit this pattern. However you rotate it, it predicts only two of the three groups correctly.

A neural network is made of nodes and connections between the nodes. A node is a point where values arrive, and a connection carries a value between nodes. The numbers on the connections are parameters. They play the same role as the slope and intercept of a straight line. They start unknown and are estimated with a method called backpropagation, which is the topic of Part 2. In this video we assume the network is already fit.

Some nodes contain a bent or curved line. These curves are called activation functions, and they are the building blocks of the squiggle. This video uses softplus. Other choices are ReLU (rectified linear unit), a bent line, and the S-shaped sigmoid. Josh notes that most teachers use sigmoid, but ReLU and softplus are much more common in practice.

The layer of nodes between the input and the output is a hidden layer. This network is as simple as they get: one input node (dosage), one hidden layer with two nodes, and one output node (predicted efficacy).

Step by step

To keep the math simple, dosage runs from 0 (low) to 1 (high).

  1. Plug a dosage into the input node. Start with the lowest dosage, 0.
  2. On the way to the top hidden node, multiply the dosage by −34.4 and add 2.14. The result is an x-axis coordinate for the activation function. For dosage 0 this gives 2.14.
  3. Put 2.14 into softplus to get the y-axis value: 2.25. For dosage 0.1, x is −1.3 and y is 0.24. Repeat for every dosage up to 1 to trace a blue curve.
  4. Notice that dosages 0 to 1 map to only a narrow slice of the softplus curve. Only that slice shapes the blue curve.
  5. Scale every y value on the blue curve by −1.3. For dosage 0, 2.25 becomes −2.93. This flips the curve upside down.
  6. Repeat for the bottom hidden node, which uses weight −2.52 and bias 1.29. For dosage 0, x is 1.29 and softplus gives 1.53. Tracing all dosages gives an orange curve from a different slice of the same function.
  7. Scale the orange curve by a positive number, 2.28.
  8. Add the y values of the blue and orange curves to get a green squiggle. Finally, subtract 0.58. Now the green squiggle fits the data.

Formulas

Input to a hidden node
$$ x = w \cdot d + b $$

Symbols: \(d\) is the dosage. \(w\) is the weight, the number we multiply by. \(b\) is the bias, the number we add. \(x\) is the x-axis coordinate we feed into the activation function. What it tells you: the weight and bias choose which slice of the activation function the node uses, and whether it is flipped or stretched.

Softplus activation function
$$ f(x) = \log\left(1 + e^{x}\right) $$

Symbols: \(x\) is the input from the connection. \(e\) is the base of the natural log. \(\log\) is the natural log (log base \(e\)), as is standard in statistics, machine learning, and most programming languages. What it tells you: softplus gives the y-axis value for each x on its bent curve; for example \(f(2.14) = 2.25\).

The whole network from the video
$$ \hat{y} = -1.3\, f(-34.4\,d + 2.14) + 2.28\, f(-2.52\,d + 1.29) - 0.58 $$

Symbols: \(\hat{y}\) is the predicted efficacy. \(f\) is softplus. \(-34.4, -2.52\) are the weights into the hidden nodes and \(2.14, 1.29\) their biases. \(-1.3, 2.28\) are the weights out of the hidden nodes, and \(-0.58\) is the final bias. What it tells you: the whole green squiggle is just two reshaped softplus curves added together and shifted.

Worked example from the video

Example

Dosage 0 (low). Top node: \(-34.4 \times 0 + 2.14 = 2.14\), and \(f(2.14) = 2.25\). Scale: \(2.25 \times (-1.3) = -2.93\). Bottom node: \(-2.52 \times 0 + 1.29 = 1.29\), and \(f(1.29) = 1.53\). Scale: \(1.53 \times 2.28 \approx 3.49\). Output: \(-2.93 + 3.49 - 0.58 = -0.02 \approx 0\). The network predicts "not effective", which matches the data.

Dosage 0.1. Top node: \(-34.4 \times 0.1 + 2.14 = -1.3\), and \(f(-1.3) = 0.24\). This is the second blue dot in the video.

Dosage 0.5 (a new patient). Top node: \(-34.4 \times 0.5 + 2.14 = -15.06\), and \(f(-15.06) \approx 0\), so the blue part adds almost nothing. Bottom node: \(-2.52 \times 0.5 + 1.29 = 0.03\), and \(f(0.03) \approx 0.708\). Scale: \(0.708 \times 2.28 \approx 1.614\). Output: \(0 + 1.614 - 0.58 \approx 1.03\). Because 1.03 is closer to 1 than to 0, Josh concludes that dosage 0.5 is effective.

Common mistakes and subtle points

Watch out The "log" in softplus is the natural log. Using log base 10 gives wrong numbers, so \(f(2.14)\) would not equal 2.25.
Watch out A node does not use its whole activation function. Dosages 0 to 1 map to only a narrow range of x values. Different weights and biases pick different slices, and that is why two identical curves become two different shapes.
Watch out The parameters in this video were not chosen by hand. They come from fitting the network to the data with backpropagation, which Part 2 explains.
Watch out Sigmoid is the usual teaching example, but ReLU and softplus are much more common in practice. The activation function is a choice you make when you build the network.

If they ask you in an interview

In plain words, what does a neural network do?
It fits a squiggle to data. Each hidden node takes an activation function, and the weights and biases slice, flip, and stretch it into a new shape. The network adds these shapes and shifts the result so it fits the data.
What are weights and biases?
They are the parameters on the connections. Weights are the numbers we multiply by, and biases are the numbers we add. They are like the slope and intercept of a straight line, and backpropagation estimates them from the data.
What is an activation function, and which ones are common?
It is the bent or curved line inside a hidden node, and it is the building block of the squiggle. Common choices are ReLU, softplus, and sigmoid. Sigmoid is popular in teaching, but ReLU and softplus are much more common in practice.
Why can a neural network fit data that a straight line cannot?
In the dosage example, efficacy is low, then high, then low again. A straight line can only get two of the three groups right. The network adds reshaped bent curves, so it can make a hump that rises and falls with the data.
How do you decide how many hidden layers and nodes to use?
There are rules of thumb, but in practice you make a guess and check how well the network performs. If it does not do well enough, you add more layers or more nodes. With more of them, the network can fit much more complex squiggles.

Recap in 30 seconds

  • The drug data is 0, then 1, then 0, so a straight line cannot fit it.
  • A neural network is nodes plus connections, and the connections carry weights and biases.
  • Each connection multiplies the input by a weight and adds a bias to get an x value for the activation function.
  • Activation functions such as softplus, ReLU, and sigmoid are the bent building blocks of the fit.
  • The hidden nodes turn two identical softplus curves into two new shapes by slicing, flipping, and stretching them.
  • The output adds the shapes and subtracts 0.58, and this gives a squiggle that predicts 1.03 for dosage 0.5.
  • Backpropagation, covered in Part 2, estimates the weights and biases.
  • With more hidden layers and nodes, a network can in theory fit a squiggle to almost any dataset.
